# A Deload Week in a Supercompensation Period Adds Up to a Stable Gain (Slide 7)

**What it is** — Slide 7: "The optimal load applied during a period of supercompensation (deload week) creates a summation/ additive affect that aids in stabilizing the increase in performance." Slide 10 gives the practical version: after a PR, "STABILIZE new level of performance," "shut it down and do not train at that new PR immediately," "allow for the body to accept that new level of increase." The deck does not say how large the "optimal load" is for a deload week; slide 11 supplies examples (see [[framework--example-deload-schemes-four-week-waves-and-multi-week-unloads]]).

**Why it works** — The author gives no mechanism for the "summation/ additive" effect and cites no study. The wording uses the supercompensation frame the deck also sets beside Verkhoshansky's critique (see [[claim--adaptation-as-active-disequilibrium-and-the-decks-supercompensation-wording]]).

**When to apply** — Only as a practitioner rationale for putting a lighter week after a hard block. Eric's baseline: in-season rule is "maintain intensity, reduce volume" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 105, Inferred from course material; the in-season dose is unknown, Q18). Inference: that is a volume cut like a deload, but not a labelled deload week.

**When NOT to apply** — "Optimal load" and "summation/ additive affect" are undefined. The claim sits on the contested supercompensation frame. Confidence is untested.

**Example** — None beyond the PR advice on slide 10; no athlete or result is given.

# Adaptation as Active Disequilibrium, Beside the Deck's Supercompensation Wording

**What it is** — Two frames appear in the deck.
- Slide 4: "Adaptation is also NOT an equilibrium process (Verkoshansky)." Slide 5: adaptation "Is a phenomenon of accommodation," "characterizes the organism's entire reaction," "reflects the specific features of the external influences," and "is the active maintenance of a definite level of disequilibrium between the organism and the environment." Slide 5 credits the last bullet, "The fundamental reason for the origin and development of accommodative reconstruction within the organism," to Verkhoshansky (spelling as printed).
- Supercompensation wording: slide 2 calls the body "self-regulating and supercompensating." Slide 7: "The optimal load applied during a period of supercompensation (deload week) creates a summation/ additive affect that aids in stabilizing the increase in performance." Slide 9: "take advantage of supercompensation."

**Why it works** — The deck gives no mechanism for either frame and never says the two differ. Inference: slide 7 uses the classic recover-then-rise picture, while slides 4-5 name a source (Verkhoshansky) whose co-authored book in this library argues "adaptive reconstruction" is a better term than supercompensation.

**When to apply** — Treat the deck's deload advice as practice, not as support for any one adaptation theory. Eric's baseline: no stated position on supercompensation versus adaptive reconstruction in `docs/method-baseline.md`.

**When NOT to apply** — Marked contested: the deck uses the supercompensation word (slides 2, 7, 9) while citing a source (slides 4-5) that this library records as critiquing it (see the Related entries); the deck does not acknowledge the tension. Do not cite slide 7's "period of supercompensation" as evidence that supercompensation occurs.

**Example** — None given.
