# The Long-Term Delayed Training Effect: Volume Well Above the Optimum Depresses Speed-Strength, Then a Timed Volume Cut Lets It Rise Above Baseline

**What it is** — The authors report "a steady decrease in speed-strength preparedness during individual training stages of different length (from 5-12 weeks)" caused by "voluminous strength loading," after which "speed-strength returns to its initial level and significantly exceeds it" (Nikitin 1977; Mironenko 1979; Antonova 1982; Levchenko 1982). Because this "does not agree with established ideas which stipulate a continuous rise in special physical preparedness" and "is not accompanied by marked signs of overtraining," it became "a subject of special study" (PDF p. 374 / printed p. 363, checked against the page image). They then state a dose-response rule: "The volume of the training load has a definite optimum level for each athlete, above or below which the body reacts with diminished response." "If the volume of loading is lower than the optimum level, special work-capacity decreases after some initial rise. If the volume of loading significantly exceeds this level, a steady decrease in special work-capacity occurs which, however, rises intensely during the carefully-timed reduction in the volume. This intense rise in special work-capacity is the phenomenon known as the long-term delayed training effect (LDTE)" (same page).

Their characterisation (Fig 6.31, PDF pp. 374-375 / printed pp. 363-364, checked against the page images), points 1, 2 and 4:
1. "Concentration of strength loading is the basic condition for the beginning of the LDTE."
2. "The lower the speed-strength levels fall (within an optimal range) during the concentration of strength loading stage, the higher they rise in the subsequent LDTE phase (graphs of F1 and F2). Excessive concentration of strength loading leads to a more significant drop in speed-strength and disruption of adaptation (graph F3)." In the figure, F3 dips lowest and only creeps back toward the baseline by the end of T2; F1 and F2 end above it.
4. "A subsequent moderate volume of general developmental work, combined with special work of gradually increasing intensity contributes to eliciting the LDTE."

Points 3 (non-intensive means) and 5 (T2 about equal to T1, observed 4-12 weeks, individualised) are already quoted in [[framework--conditions-for-concentrated-unidirectional-loading]] and [[heuristic--allow-restoration-after-a-concentrated-block-before-the-next]].

**Figures.** Fig 6.29 (sprinters, PDF p. 374, read from the image): a shaded rectangle marks the loading period from month 0 to about month 2.5; three curves (starting, explosive and absolute strength, the dips not labelled separately) fall to about 85-96% of initial, recross 100% at about month 2, and at month 4 sit at about 125% (starting), about 119% (explosive) and about 112% (absolute). Fig 6.30 (middle-distance runners, Zhdanovich 1980, PDF p. 375): during a shaded block from about month 1.3 to about month 4.3, vertical jump rises to about 7% and then falls to about 4.5% by month 8; stride length and ten standing long jumps keep rising after the block, to about 11% and about 9-9.5%. The text says vertical jump falls in the competition stage and offers a hedged reason: "One may assume that the reason for this phenomenon is the increase in volume of intense distance work" (PDF p. 374). LDTE after concentrated strength loading is also reported in swimming (Rudokene 1981) and ice-skating (Kabachkova et al 1982).

Two further points: athletes "invariably do not allow or do not have sufficient time for recuperation after extensive strength loading," which minimises the LDTE and forces the body "to unjustifiably deplete its current adaptation energy and sometimes draw upon its deep adaptation energy" (PDF p. 375 / printed p. 364). And "one must differentiate between maintenance of the training effect and the LDTE of loading": maintenance is "the retention of functional adaptation for a given period after training has ceased"; the LDTE is "the relatively prolonged increase in the functional indicators as a result of specially organised, concentrated volumes of loading" (PDF p. 377 / printed p. 366, checked against the page image).

**Why it works** — The authors say the volume-performance link is "characteristically simple" but give no physiological mechanism for the delayed rise in 6.10's strength material; they note that the "physiological conditions, mechanisms and characteristics of the LDTE until recently have not been a subject of much research" (PDF p. 374). Inference: the library holds two readings of the same curve shape, supercompensation or adaptive reconstruction ([[claim--adaptive-reconstruction-vs-supercompensation]]) and fitness-fatigue, where fatigue masks fitness until volume drops ([[framework--fitness-fatigue-modeling-and-taper-timing]]); the book does not settle between them here.

**When to apply** — Planning a concentrated strength block for an advanced athlete when the aim is a later rise in speed-strength, accepting a planned dip during the block. Inference: a dip in speed-strength during such a block is the expected path, not by itself a signal to stop. Eric's baseline: "Recovery is managed through training-load design alone; nothing objective is tracked, sleep included" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 153, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q23)). Inference: the LDTE rests on one load-design move, the timed volume cut, so it fits that practice. Eric's baseline: "The mRSI decision read happens on Wednesdays, against individualized thresholds" (line 75, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q2, Q2a)). Inference: during a deliberate block, an mRSI drop below an athlete's usual band would be expected; the authors' own rule separates a dip "within an optimal range" from the F3 case, but gives no threshold for telling them apart.

**When NOT to apply** — The book gives no numbers for "optimum," "within an optimal range" or "excessive," and says the effect is individual (PDF p. 375). Figs 6.29-6.31 carry no sample sizes or statistics; Fig 6.31 is a "general scheme," not data. The 5-12 weeks on PDF p. 374 is the length of the loading stages; the 4-12 weeks on PDF p. 375 is how long the LDTE lasts. They are different quantities. Inference: in-season, when performance is needed every week, a planned multi-week dip conflicts with Eric's baseline that "in football you want to peak every Saturday" (line 185, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q14)); this is an off-season tool. The book's claim that the dip comes without "marked signs of overtraining" sits beside Bompa's framing of concentrated loading as planned overreaching ([[periodization--bompa/principle--concentrated-loading-overreaching]]); Inference: that is a difference of labels, not of the observed curve.

**Example** — The authors' own: in sprinters (Fig 6.29), about 2.5 months of concentrated strength loading drove starting, explosive and absolute strength to about 85-96% of initial; by month 4 they stood at about 112-125% (values read from the image, PDF p. 374).

# Put Technical Training in the Delayed-Effect Window, and Keep Competition-Period Strength Work Short, Intense and Small

**What it is** — Three linked rules from 6.10:
1. **Strength first, technique in the rebound.** "high-volume strength loads are detrimental to one's current level of speed-strength fitness and, consequently, to technical preparation. To avoid this, it is advisable in training high level athletes to structure the programme so that strength work precedes and does not interfere with technical training. Technical training should take place in the presence of the delayed training effect of the concentrated volume of strength work, i.e. when there is recovery of work capacity and an increase in the level of speed-strength." Technique then "rapidly adjusts to the athlete's higher level of specialised work capacity and there is much less likelihood of reinforcing errors" from the "lowered functional state, caused by high-volume loads" (PDF p. 376 / printed p. 365, checked against the page image).
2. **Do not add volume while the effect emerges.** "The volume of training work, therefore, should not be increased during the period of emergence of the LDTE" (PDF p. 375 / printed p. 364, checked against the page image).
3. **Competition period.** "One should be especially cautious with strength loading during the competition period. At this time, intensive and short-term strength work can be used in a small volume to tone the neuromuscular system during competition preparation, as well as to maintain the achieved level of speed-strength preparedness if the length of the competition period exceeds the duration of the LDTE of strength loading" (PDF pp. 375-376 / printed pp. 364-365).

The authors also state that "the competitive action executed with maximal physical exertion represents the most specific of all the special training means," to be viewed "simultaneously as a highly intense means of strength training," so a strength programme should rise in intensity and be "combined with technique training so as to peak concurrently with the competitive act" (PDF p. 376). They give "the dominant role" in the strength-technique interaction to "special physical preparation, both in the yearly cycle and in long-term training" (same page).

**Why it works** — The authors' reasons: a strength-depressed state reinforces technical errors, while a higher work capacity lets the athlete "concentrate his effort more effectively, increase range of movement, decrease the time of execution of movements, improve his stability, improve the strength of muscular control over all movements and increase his resistance to fatigue" (PDF p. 376). For rule 2 they report that during emergence "it is relatively easy and painless for the body to change to intensive loading, but it will react negatively to extensive work," seen as "slowed or even decreased growth of speed-strength" (PDF p. 375).

**When to apply** — After a concentrated strength block, schedule the heaviest technical emphasis for the weeks when speed-strength is coming back up, and hold volume flat through that period. In a long competition period, keep a small dose of intense, brief strength work. Eric's baseline: "In-season, the stated rule is 'maintain intensity, reduce volume'" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 105, Inferred (transcript + shared slides); the baseline adds "Not claimed in the interview"). Inference: rule 3 says the same thing for the competition period. Eric's baseline: the in-season week has "lifting and hard practice end 72 hours before a Saturday game" (line 186, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q15)). Inference: that sits with rule 3's "small volume" and "short-term" strength work; the authors give no day-level timing.

**When NOT to apply** — **Contested inside the book.** The Addendum restates the emergence-period rule the other way round: "In the period of LDTE realization, volume concentrated loads are handled by the body relatively easily and without harm to the body, but intense loads negatively react to the work volume" (PDF p. 536 / printed p. 525, checked against the page image). Its own example then agrees with Chapter 6: triple jumpers whose specialised strength volume rose "by only a few percent" to "maintain" their level saw "a fast decrease in speed-strength and sports results" (same page, checked against the page image). Inference: the Addendum sentence may be a translation slip, since its example fits Chapter 6, but the book prints both. The authors give no number for "small volume" or for how long technical emphasis should wait. Inference: for skills that need daily practice (team-sport play), technique cannot simply wait for the strength block to end; the rule is written for "high level athletes" in events where the competitive action can be isolated, and they mention the conjugate system "successively introduces different modes of loading ... rather than separating them in time" (PDF p. 376), so the separation is a matter of emphasis.

**Example** — The authors' own, from the Addendum (PDF p. 536, checked against the page image): triple jumpers gained speed-strength and results in May-June after concentrated specialised strength loads in March-April, then lost both quickly when they raised strength volume by a few percent to hold the gain. Inference: an off-season strength block ending in March would hand over to technical and speed emphasis in April with strength volume held, not raised.

# For Aerobic and Anaerobic Productivity, a Long-Term Delayed Effect Has Been Documented Only After Altitude Training

**What it is** — Under "The Delayed Training Effect and Long Duration Work," the authors state: "With respect to aerobic and anaerobic productivity, there is no experimental data that clearly documents occurrence of the LDTE phenomenon in cyclic distance training. The exceptions are cases of intense training under the lowered oxygen conditions of high altitude" (PDF p. 376 / printed p. 365, checked against the page image). Their evidence:
- **Swimmers (Vaitsekhovsky et al 1974).** "significant increases" in "maximum aerobic capacity, the total volume of work which can be executed at the critical level of power, VO2 max and the onset of blood lactate accumulation." "These changes persisted for more than 4 weeks after 3 weeks of training at an altitude of 1700m above sea level, with a peak response taking place about 1 week after the altitude training. All of the athletes in the study improved their results immediately after training at altitude."
- **Skaters (Ivanov 1977).** A 4-minute cycle-ergometer test weekly for 3 weeks at 2250 m, then for 3 weeks at sea level. At altitude, mechanical efficiency fell and oxygen consumption, energy expenditure and lactate rose; back at sea level, "the functional efficiency of the work exceeded the pre-experimental levels as a result of more economical metabolism and a significantly diminished amount of anaerobically produced ATP" (same page).
- **Not an altitude case: swimmers under large loads (Susman 1978; no altitude is mentioned).** The authors cite this as evidence that load-induced homeostatic disturbance shows in psycho-physiological indicators, not as a documented LDTE. Large loads slowed "complex motor reactions," reduced precision of movement patterns and of "tracking a moving object," and lowered work capacity, ECG-assessed cardiovascular state, muscle tonus and adrenal-cortex hormone levels. After loads were reduced and intensity altered, these indicators rose "as the supercompensation (adaptive reconstruction) phase emerged" (PDF p. 377 / printed p. 366, checked against the page image).

**Why it works** — The authors' explanation for the altitude cases: hypobaric conditions gave "a very demanding training influence which provoked persistent disturbance of the body's homeostasis and subsequent activation of its adaptive processes" (PDF p. 376). For why the LDTE is not seen at sea level, they hedge: if it exists there, the disturbance "will occur at the level of more subtle physiological systems" not detectable by "methods used for assessing the more gross aerobic and anaerobic processes," and "adaptation apparently occurs at deeper cellular and molecular levels (e.g. in the mitochondria and endocrine system)" (Yakovlev 1957, 1974; Viru 1969, 1981; Finogenov 1979), while "disturbance of homeostasis can be detected in the dynamics of a number of psycho-physiological indicators" of the central nervous and neuromuscular systems (PDF pp. 376-377). This is a stated hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.

**When to apply** — When planning or interpreting altitude camps: the book's one quantified case puts the peak about 1 week after a 3-week stay, with changes lasting more than 4 weeks. When judging a heavy endurance block: the authors point to psycho-physiological and neuromuscular indicators as the place a hidden disturbance may show. Eric's baseline: "Modified reactive strength index (mRSI) from daily CMJ testing was deliberately chosen as the daily athlete-readiness marker" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 74, Explicit). Inference: a neuromuscular marker of that kind is the sort of indicator the authors suggest, though they name reaction, precision and tracking tasks, not jump tests.

**When NOT to apply** — The book gives no sample sizes, controls or statistics for any of the three studies, and the skater study has no non-altitude comparison group in the text. The authors say plainly that the LDTE in sea-level distance training is not documented; do not carry the strength-block LDTE timings ([[principle--long-term-delayed-training-effect-from-volume-above-the-optimum]]) over to aerobic work. The LDTE they do report in cyclic sports (middle-distance running, swimming, skating) follows concentrated strength loading and shows in strength-endurance measures, not in aerobic productivity (PDF p. 374). Inference: altitude camps are rare in American collegiate football, so this entry matters mainly for endurance or Olympic-sport programmes.

**Example** — The authors' own: swimmers after 3 weeks at 1700 m improved immediately, peaked about 1 week after returning, and kept the gains for more than 4 weeks (Vaitsekhovsky et al 1974; PDF p. 376 / printed p. 365).

# Distributed and Concentrated Loading Both Work at Medium Qualification; Highly Qualified Athletes Need Concentration

**What it is** — For loading of one primary emphasis, the authors separate "distributed loading," "a relatively uniform distribution of means during an annual cycle," from "concentrated loading," "the concentration of means over definite training stages" (PDF p. 371 / printed p. 360, checked against the page image). Their claim: "Research indicates that both methods are successful in training athletes of medium qualification. The concentrated loading method is appropriate for the training of highly qualified athletes." The evidence named: "distribution of glycolytic work over the annual cycle of highly qualified sprinters raises the loading volume, but does not increase the effectiveness of the loading. However, by concentrating the volume of glycolytic work at certain stages, the work can be executed at a smaller volume but with a more significant improvement in speed-endurance (Kornelyuk, 1980)." "Similar results" are reported for concentrated special strength work in sprinters (Levchenko 1986), middle-distance runners (Sirenko & Zhdanovich 1982), boxers (Filimonov 1979) and jumpers (Mironenko & Antonova 1981). Their conclusion, italicised in the original: "concentration of the volume of uni-directional loading produces more extensive functional changes and greater improvement in the athlete's physical fitness" (same page).

**Why it works** — The authors' reason: "The regular use of training with distributed loading which is dispersed over a prolonged period evokes only a transient functional reaction which does not enhance the development of long-term adaptation. Distributed loading initially can yield some rise in functional level but its training potential soon drops because of rapid adaptation of the body to the training stimuli" (PDF p. 371). No physiological mechanism beyond that is given.

**When to apply** — For advanced athletes whose progress has stalled under an even, year-round spread of one kind of work, concentrate that work into defined stages. The authors list concentration of loading over time among their four organisational principles of special strength training (PDF pp. 370-371). Eric's baseline: "Undulating/non-linear periodization ... waving volume week to week ... is stated as the personally preferred model over strict linear progression" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 106, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q1)). Inference: undulation is about week-to-week waves and concentration is about where a quality's volume sits across the year; the two can coexist, but the book's claim is that an evenly spread quality loses its effect for advanced athletes.

**When NOT to apply** — The book gives no sample sizes, effect sizes or design for any of the cited studies, and gives no definition here of "medium" versus "highly qualified." **Contested across the library:** the vault brief of Issurin (2016) records that he judged the evidence for Verkhoshansky's concentrated-unidirectional model insufficient for broad use in high-performance combat, team-sport and swimming preparation, that the reviewed combat and team-sport studies improved fitness measures but did not report team-practice or competition outcomes, and that he placed the model as better suited to sports dominated by a narrow target quality ([[research-papers/issurin-2016-benefits-and-limitations-of-block-periodized-training-approaches-to-athletes-preparation-a]], brief citing review pp. 6, 11; paraphrase, not Issurin's wording). The Supertraining evidence above comes from sprinters, middle-distance runners, boxers and jumpers, Inference: its boxing study (Filimonov 1979) is a combat-sport case Issurin's review would put in doubt. Inference: for football, where many qualities must be held at once, the claim applies to a single quality inside an otherwise mixed programme rather than to the whole plan. Eric's baseline: "incompatible qualities ... are not both targeted at full intensity in the same block" and "the rest are trained at a retention dose" (line 182, Inferred (transcript + slides)).

**Example** — The authors' own: highly qualified sprinters who concentrated glycolytic work into certain stages did less of it but improved speed-endurance more than when the same kind of work was spread over the year (Kornelyuk 1980; PDF p. 371 / printed p. 360). Inference: in an off-season, glycolytic conditioning could be placed in one or two short stages rather than a little every week; Eric's baseline notes that staff "do program it in the off-season" (line 117, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q19)).

# Within One Session, the Order of Paired Explosive-Strength Means Matters Less Than Their Volume and Contrast

**What it is** — For explosive strength, the authors list "brief combinations of training means" that give "a positive cumulative effect": "short and long jumping exercises"; "barbell exercises and jumping exercises"; "barbell exercises with 30% and 90% of maximum"; "barbell exercises and the plyometric method of developing explosive-strength"; "leaping with kettle-bells and jumping exercises"; "barbell exercises and leaping with kettle-bells" (PDF p. 371 / printed p. 360, checked against the page image). Then: "In a single training session the order of the above means, combined in a systematic complex is fairly unimportant, since the length of time for which the body maintains the after-effect of training stress exceeds the duration of the workout." In italics: "concerning the development of explosive strength, the order of the means is not fundamentally as important as the volume of the loading and the degree of contrast between the different training means." Yet in the next sentence: "Nevertheless, one should still preserve the above order of combining means in each case, since this provides the favourable effect of a brief rise in fitness state, which the first exercise produces as the second one is executed (see Ch 5.3)." Their delivery rule: "employ them sequentially with active rest between each series," and when repeating a series, "vary the exercises to avoid monotony and to maintain the optimal rest interval" (same page).

**Why it works** — The authors' reason: "During this session and the subsequent restoration period, a generalised summation of the after-effects of the entire complex of training influences of different primary emphasis occurs, which produces the same cumulative effect, generally regardless of their sequence" (PDF p. 371). They add that "it is still sensible to apply the recommended scheme of Figure 1.7 to minimise the risk of reducing skill and safety." Figure 1.7 in this edition is a torque diagram in 1.2 (PDF pp. 28-29, text layer). Inference: that cross-reference appears to be wrong and the intended scheme is unclear.

**When to apply** — Building a contrast or complex session for explosive strength from one of the listed pairs: settle the volume and the contrast between the two means first, then keep the listed order as a secondary choice. Inference: when equipment or space forces the order to flip on a given day, the authors' text says little is lost within the session. Eric's baseline: "Training time is deliberately weighted toward high motor-unit-recruitment core/ballistic movements (squats, cleans, jumps, throws, sprints)" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 104, Inferred (transcript + slides)).

**When NOT to apply** — **Contested inside the book.** The same page says order is "fairly unimportant" and then that "one should still preserve the above order." Chapter 5 states fixed orders: "Strength exercises should be done after speed-strength exercises in training" and "Strong-acting plyometric methods should be done after strength exercises, but not before" (PDF p. 301 / printed p. 290, text layer), and 5.2.4 says resistance plus plyometrics works best "provided the resistance movements precede the plyometric activities in individual sessions" (PDF p. 288 / printed p. 277). The novice experiments do support the "order unimportant" view within one workout: "There was no statistically significant difference" between strength-first and speed-strength-first (PDF p. 299 / printed p. 288; [[case-study--novice-strength-and-speed-strength-sequencing-experiments]]). Keep this within-session rule separate from the long-term sequence across stages, where the authors say order does matter: extensive submaximal resistance first, then explosive work, and "The reverse sequence of this loading scheme leads to a negative cumulative effect" (PDF p. 372; [[heuristic--sequential-accumulation-needs-4-6-week-stages-extensive-before-explosive]]). Inference: Issurin's session rules put the dominant or technical task first and add other modalities after it ([[block-periodization--issurin/principle--training-modality-compatibility-in-a-workout]]), a stricter view of order than this passage. The authors say endurance interactions are a different case, where order clearly matters ([[framework--positive-and-negative-sequences-of-endurance-loading]]).

**Example** — The authors list barbell exercises at 30% and 90% of maximum as one positive pairing (PDF p. 371). Inference: a session pairing heavy back squats with light jump squats, done as alternating series with active rest and with exercise variants rotated on repeat days, would follow the rule; the authors give no sets, reps or rest times here.

# Which Endurance Loads Help or Hurt the Load That Follows, and How Long Restoration Takes

**What it is** — The authors first warn that for endurance, research on the interaction of loads of different emphasis is "inadequate" and "often so contradictory that it is very difficult to discern definite trends" (PDF pp. 371-372 / printed pp. 360-361, checked against the page images). The energy-system categories (aerobic, alactic-anaerobic, anaerobic-glycolytic, mixed) "are so conditional that they permit very broad interpretation" and "cannot be strictly applied" to judging a load's emphasis, and "no research has analysed the energy processes occurring while the general activity is undoubtedly aerobic, but the postural muscles are strongly contracted" (PDF p. 372). With that caveat, "current experimental data indicates" (PDF p. 372, checked against the page image):

Positive interaction, where "the previous loading creates favourable conditions for executing the subsequent loading and raises the effect of the training session as a whole":
- "aerobic exercises are done after ATP-CP training"
- "aerobic exercises are done after glycolytic-anaerobic training (in small volume)"
- "glycolytic-anaerobic exercises are done after ATP-CP training."

Negative interaction (Volkov 1975):
- "ATP-CP exercises are done after a large amount of any glycolytic work"
- "glycolytic-anaerobic exercises follow large volumes of aerobic work."

Restoration figures: after "a large volume of aerobic work," restoration of energy resources and endocrine equilibrium "is prolonged by 2-3 days," during which "a small volume of anaerobic loading" is appropriate and "will not negatively influence the restoration of aerobic capacity." "Restoration after moderate volumes of anaerobic loading usually takes 3-8 hours," "very large volumes of anaerobic loading will delay restoration," and "The training effect of ATP-CP workouts deteriorates if the sessions are done without allowing full restoration from the preceding loading" (Nabatnikova 1972; Volkov 1975; Platonov 1980; PDF p. 372). On the next page they add: "the rest pause is actually a training means which is just as important as muscular work" (italics in the original; PDF p. 373 / printed p. 362, checked against the page image).

**Why it works** — The authors' only stated reason is the one above: the earlier load either sets up or spoils the conditions for the later one. They give no physiological mechanism for each pairing. Inference: the pattern reads as "keep the highest-power work fresh and put the longer, lower-power work after it," but that is a summary, not the book's explanation.

**When to apply** — Ordering conditioning within a session or across consecutive days: alactic work before glycolytic or aerobic work; aerobic work after a small glycolytic dose; no alactic speed work after a big glycolytic session; a small anaerobic dose is acceptable in the 2-3 days after a big aerobic session. Eric's baseline: "All three energy systems ... are always active simultaneously; programming targets the adaptation, not the drill label" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 114, Explicit). Inference: the authors' warning that the categories are "so conditional" agrees with that line. Eric's baseline: the football priority stack is "alactic and aerobic capacity/power" with glycolytic conditioning treated as a "worst case scenario" (line 116, Explicit; Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q1)). Inference: the positive pairing "aerobic exercises are done after ATP-CP training" fits that two-system stack in one session.

**When NOT to apply** — The authors themselves call the endurance evidence inadequate and contradictory, give no sample sizes, and cite only the negative list to one author (Volkov 1975). "Large," "small" and "moderate" volumes are not quantified. Inference: the 3-8 hour and 2-3 day figures are general restoration estimates, not limits for a given athlete. The positive list is about order within or between sessions; it is not the same as the long-term sequence across stages ([[framework--three-viewpoints-on-organising-endurance-development]]).

**Example** — Inference: a football off-season day of short acceleration sprints followed by tempo running fits "aerobic exercises are done after ATP-CP training"; reversing it, with a long glycolytic conditioning test before sprint work, fits the negative case "ATP-CP exercises are done after a large amount of any glycolytic work." The authors give no worked session.

# Sequential Stages Need 4-6 Weeks to Count: Extensive Submaximal Resistance First, Then Explosive Work

**What it is** — "Sequential accumulation of the training effect of loading of different primary emphasis has a place in long-term preparation and is achieved by the action of preceding work on subsequent work" (PDF p. 372 / printed p. 361, checked against the page image). For explosive strength, "positive accumulation is achieved by means of a sequence which applies extensive (i.e. high volume) training with submaximal resistance first, followed by explosive training," and "The reverse sequence of this loading scheme leads to a negative cumulative effect." The time rule: "The mechanism of sequential accumulation is appropriate only if the training effect of the previous work has become relatively stable, which takes at least 4-6 weeks. When the strength means of different emphasis are changed over shorter time intervals, for example after 2 weeks, the body cannot differentiate between the specifics of their individual influence and the body's adaptive reaction as mediated by the transient accumulation process. Thus, a change after 2-week stages of use in the order of loading of different emphasis minimally alters the resulting training effect" (same page).

**Why it works** — The authors give a mechanism for the order: "the positive accumulation is achieved by the first loading regime producing a rise in the general energy potential of the neuromuscular system. This creates favourable conditions for further adaptation, facilitated by the subsequent loading" (PDF p. 372). For the 4-6 week rule, their reason is that shorter stages blur into "transient accumulation" (the form defined in [[framework--temporal-and-qualitative-forms-of-the-training-effect]]); no data or study is named for the 4-6 week figure.

**When to apply** — Planning an off-season run of strength and explosive stages: put the high-volume submaximal strength stage first, hold it at least 4-6 weeks, then switch to explosive emphasis. Inference: rotating emphasis every 2 weeks gives a mixed (transient) effect rather than a sequence, so if a coach wants a true sequence the stages should be longer; if a mixed effect is wanted, short rotation is acceptable. Eric's baseline: "The preparatory period is sequenced hypertrophy/work-capacity, then basic strength, then strength-to-power" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 180, Inferred (transcript + slides)). Inference: that order matches the book's extensive-then-explosive sequence.

**When NOT to apply** — The 4-6 week figure is stated without a cited study. The authors' own Fig 6.24 experiment used 3-month stages ([[case-study--barbell-and-depth-jump-sequences-in-high-jumpers]]), so the 4-6 weeks is a minimum, not a tested optimum. The 5.4.4 stage sequence for a jumper starts with jumping exercises before barbell work, then jumps with weights, then depth jumps (Fig 5.18, PDF p. 308; [[heuristic--sequence-jumps-then-barbell-then-depth-jumps-across-stages]]). Inference: that is not a clean contradiction, since its first stage is low-intensity jumping rather than "explosive training," but the two passages do not state the same first step. Eric's baseline: "Undulating/non-linear periodization ... waving volume week to week" is Eric's preferred model (line 106, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q1)). Inference: by this passage, week-to-week waves inside one emphasis are compatible with the rule, but changing the emphasis itself every two weeks would be the "transient" case. Bondarchuk agrees on the direction, from a different premise: stages shorter than the 2-3 months most athletes need to reach sports form cannot build a real base, and 1-3 week rotating stages give only "current" transfer ([[transfer-of-training-in-sports--bondarchuk/heuristic--general-prep-stage-duration-must-match-sports-form-cycle]]). Inference: Issurin's accumulation blocks of up to about six weeks, and shorter ones to refresh ([[block-periodization--issurin/framework--accumulation-mesocycle-duration-and-monitoring]]), concern block length for a mixed set of targets, a different quantity from the 4-6 week stability threshold here.

**Example** — The authors' own direction: extensive submaximal resistance work first, then explosive training; the reverse gives "a negative cumulative effect" (PDF p. 372). Inference: a 6-week high-volume squat and pull stage at moderate loads, then a 4-6 week jump and light-load speed stage, rather than alternating the two emphases every fortnight.
