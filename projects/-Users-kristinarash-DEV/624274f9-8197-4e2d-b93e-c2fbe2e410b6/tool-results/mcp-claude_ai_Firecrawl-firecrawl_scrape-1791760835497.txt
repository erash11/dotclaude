In the meantime, to ensure continued support, we are displaying the site without styles\nand JavaScript.\n\nEffects of rehabilitation interventions on time to return to play and reinjury rate in athletes with acute hamstring strain injuries\n\n\n[Download PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1.pdf)\n\n[Download PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1.pdf)\n\n## Abstract\n\nDespite the high prevalence and recurrence of acute hamstring strain injuries in athletes, the optimal rehabilitation strategy balancing rapid time to return to play (TTRTP) with durable re-injury protection remains unclear. We searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar from inception to January 2026 for English-language randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating exercise-based rehabilitation, adjunctive biological modalities, or other approaches for acute hamstring strains in athletes. TTRTP was the primary outcome; re-injury rate was secondary. Certainty was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE). Models were selected based on heterogeneity and clinical plausibility of pooling. 14 RCTs (693 athletes) were included. A lengthening-focused exercise protocol (L-protocol) based on two trials may shorten TTRTP versus conventional rehabilitation (moderate certainty) without increasing re-injury risk (low certainty). Progressive agility and trunk stabilization (PATS) did not accelerate TTRTP (low certainty); the re-injury estimate was inconclusive (RD -0.35, 95% CI -0.74 to 0.03; I² = 72%; very low certainty; 2 trials), with the confidence interval including both a potential reduction and no effect. For adjunctive therapies, we analyzed Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) as a separate meta-analysis (three trials) and described radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy (rESWT) and low-level laser therapy (LLLT) as single-trial evidence. PRP showed heterogeneous effects (I² = 70%), rESWT modest benefit (one trial), and LLLT no added benefit (one trial). Pain-threshold rehab enhanced strength without delaying TTRTP (moderate certainty; one trial). Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) indicated that the lengthening-focused exercise protocol (L-protocol) TTRTP benefit exceeded the monitoring boundary, suggesting sufficient evidence to rule out random error; however, this does not eliminate clinical heterogeneity or concerns about external validity. Lengthening-focused eccentric exercise may shorten TTRTP. Evidence for PATS and adjunctive modalities remains uncertain, warranting high-quality RCTs. Decisions should prioritize evidence certainty over statistical significance.\n\nClinical trial registration: PROSPERO registration ID CRD420261387775.\n\n### Similar content being viewed by However, the optimal timing, sequencing, and combination of specific modalities with specific exercise protocols remain undefined, primarily due to variability in study design and outcome measures.\n\nSeveral recent systematic reviews have synthesized evidence on hamstring rehabilitation, but none have provided a stratified synthesis that separates exercise-based interventions from adjunctive therapies rather than pooling them, nor interpreted findings through both GRADE and TSA. This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore aimed to evaluate the efficacy of rehabilitation interventions for acute hamstring strain injuries in athletes, including eccentric loading, PATS and adjunctive biological and physical therapies. The primary outcome was time to return to play; secondary outcomes were re-injury rate, pain and functional recovery. Through this stratified synthesis, we sought to guide clinical decision making by evidence certainty rather than statistical significance alone.\n\n## Methods\n\nThis systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement[37](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR37 \"Cochrane Collaboration. Cochrane handbook for systematic reviews of interventions. (2011).\").\n\n### Inclusion criteria\n\nStudies were selected based on the following PICOS criteria. Population: competitive or recreational athletes with acute hamstring strain injuries, diagnosed clinically or by imaging; Intervention: (1) exercise-based rehabilitation programmes (eccentric loading, PATS, stretching); (2) adjunctive biological or physical therapies (PRP, rESWT, LLLT) when combined with structured rehabilitation; (3) stand-alone physical modalities (TECAR, TENS) evaluated as isolated treatments. Stand-alone modalities were included only for narrative synthesis and were not pooled in meta-analyses; Comparison: conventional rehabilitation, placebo, or alternative protocols; Outcomes: TTRTP (primary), re-injury rate, pain, and function (secondary); Study design: published RCTs (English-language only, due to resource constraints; acknowledged in Limitations).\n\n### Exclusion criteria\n\n(1) Unpublished RCTs (including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, cross-sectional studies, observational studies, conference abstracts, case reports or non-randomized controlled trials). One master’s thesis was exceptionally included for narrative synthesis only because it provided unique data on an intervention not covered elsewhere (spinal adjustive therapy); its poor methodological quality (PEDro score 4) is acknowledged in the Limitations and it was not included in any pooled meta-analysis; (2) Studies where participants were not diagnosed with hamstring strain injury; (3) Studies that did not report any training or intervention measures; (4) Studies that did not report at least one of the prespecified outcomes (TTRTP, re-injury rate, pain, or functional outcomes); (5) Studies on chronic tendinopathy, tendon avulsions, or delayed-onset posterior-thigh pain.\n\n### Literature search\n\nThe search was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines[37](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR37 \"Cochrane Collaboration. Cochrane handbook for systematic reviews of interventions. (2011).\"). Two reviewers (JZ and SH) independently performed the search and screened all retrieved records. The protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO in May 2026 (CRD420261387775). The literature search was completed prior to registration, but screening against eligibility criteria and data extraction had not yet commenced. Screening began immediately after registration. The final analytical approach was consistent with the registered protocol, except for the post-hoc disaggregation of physical modalities (PRP, rESWT, LLLT), which is explicitly described in the Results. All other methods (eligibility criteria, outcomes, subgroup categories, analytical models, minimum clinically important difference, GRADE decisions, and TSA analyses) were established before screening began. From database inception to January 2026, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase and Google Scholar databases were searched. The full search strategies for all databases, including controlled vocabulary, free-text terms, Boolean operators, field tags, and limits, are provided in Supplementary Table 1. In addition, three additional records were identified through screening the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews. All studies were retrieved and imported into the citation database (EndNote 21), and duplicates were removed. P. et al. The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised trials. BMJ 343, d5928.                    https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d5928                                     (2011).\"), with assessments conducted at the outcome level. Two reviewers independently evaluated each study across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as “low risk of bias”, “some concerns” or “high risk of bias” following standard RoB 2 guidance. Domain-level justifications are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Judgements for selective reporting were based on available trial registrations or published protocols where accessible; otherwise, the domain was rated as “some concerns” in the absence of a pre-registered protocol. For exercise-based trials, lack of blinding of participants and personnel was not automatically rated as high risk; rather, ratings reflected whether protocol deviations (e.g. differential co-interventions or progression decisions influenced by knowledge of allocation) were judged likely given the trial context, consistent with RoB 2 guidance[38](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR38 \"Higgins, J. P. et al. The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised trials. BMJ 343, d5928.                    https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d5928                                     (2011).\"). In addition, we used the PEDro scale to describe the methodological quality of included studies as a supplementary descriptive measure, not as a primary risk-of-bias tool.\n\n### Data analysis\n\nData from the fourteen studies were independently extracted by two reviewers and recorded in Microsoft Excel 2025 spreadsheets to facilitate the comparison and interpretation of research findings. The extracted data encompassed participant characteristics (age, sex, and type of exercise), sample sizes for each assigned group, administered intervention protocols, and the follow-up duration. A consensus on all extracted data was reached by both authors prior to subsequent analysis. We preferentially extracted intention-to-treat data when available. For the single study reporting medians and IQRs (Hamilton et al.[39](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR39 \"Hamilton, B. et al. Platelet-rich plasma does not enhance return to play in hamstring injuries: a randomised controlled trial. Br. J. Sports Med. 49, 943–950.                    https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094603                                     (2015).\")), the SD was estimated using IQR/1.35. No authors were contacted for missing data, and no imputation was required. Inter-reviewer agreement was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.92). Data analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4. Model selection followed a prespecified strategy: random-effects models were used as the primary approach for all meta-analyses where clinical transitivity was judged adequate, to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included trials, including variations in study populations, intervention protocols, and outcome definitions. Fixed-effects models were used only in sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of findings under an alternative modelling assumption. For TTRTP analyses, we used random-effects models with inverse variance weighting. For re-injury analyses, we used Mantel-Haenszel risk difference with random-effect models as the primary approach, consistent with recommendations for sparse binary data[40](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR40 \"Higgins, J. P. T. Cochrane handbook for systematic reviews of interventions version 5.1 (2019).\"). The random-effects estimator was DerSimonian-Laird, with confidence intervals based on the standard normal approximation. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic; I² > 50% was considered a guide for substantial heterogeneity, but clinical and methodological heterogeneity particularly variations in return-to-play definitions and re-injury surveillance periods was assessed qualitatively before examining statistical heterogeneity. In the analysis evaluating the impact of hamstring eccentric loading and the hamstring strengthening program combined with progressive agility and trunk stabilization on TTRTP, the weighted mean difference (WMD) was employed to calculate the pooled effect size, owing to the utilization of identical measurement criteria. Risk difference (RD) was utilized to evaluate the re-injury rates between the intervention and control groups. Risk ratio (RR) was applied in subgroup analyses to examine the influence of follow-up duration on re-injury rates[41](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR41 \"Richardson, M., Garner, P. & Donegan, S. Interpretation of subgroup analyses in systematic reviews: a tutorial. Clin. Epidemiol. Glob. Health 7, 192–198 (2019).\"). Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Q statistic and the I² index, with the significance level established at _p_ < 0.05. The I² index quantifies heterogeneity, with values of ≤ 25%, > 25% to ≤ 50%, and > 50% indicative of low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.\n\nIn sensitivity analyses, we additionally applied Peto odds ratio, which is more stable with sparse events[42](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR42 \"Sweeting, M. J., Sutton, A. J. & Lambert, P. C. What to add to nothing? Use and avoidance of continuity corrections in meta-analysis of sparse data. Stat. Med. 23, 1351–1375.                    https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.1761                                     (2004).\"), [43](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR43 \"Bradburn, M. J., Deeks, J. J., Berlin, J. A. & Localio, A. R. Much ado about nothing: a comparison of the performance of meta-analytical methods with rare events. Stat. Med. 26, 53–77.                    https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.2528                                     (2007).\"). Studies with zero events in both arms were not estimable and did not contribute to pooled estimates. For outcomes with few studies and events, results were interpreted with emphasis on confidence intervals rather than p-values. Sensitivity analyses examined the influence of effect measure (RD versus OR) and model choice on conclusions. For multi-arm trials, only relevant arms were included: for the three-arm Hamilton trial, we used only the PRP and no-injection groups to avoid double-counting. No missing data required imputation, as all continuous outcomes were reported as means with standard deviations. Outcomes were extracted at the primary TTRTP endpoint prespecified in each trial. The analytical approach followed the PROSPERO-registered protocol, except for the post-hoc disaggregation of physical modalities (PRP, rESWT, LLLT), which is explicitly described in the Results. For the three-arm Hamilton trial (PRP, PPP, and no injection), we included only the PRP and no-injection groups in the meta-analysis. The PPP arm was excluded to avoid double-counting, and the no-injection group was selected as the comparator because it represented rehabilitation alone, consistent with the other PRP trials. No participants were double-counted.\n\n### Synthesis without meta-analysis\n\nFor outcomes and intervention categories that could not be pooled in meta-analysis (pain-threshold rehabilitation, stand-alone physical modalities, stretching exercises, and spinal adjustive therapy), we conducted a narrative synthesis following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline[44](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR44 \"Campbell, M. et al. Synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM) in systematic reviews: reporting guideline. BMJ 368, l6890 (2020).\"). Findings are presented descriptively with effect estimates and confidence intervals transcribed verbatim from the original reports. No additional statistical pooling was performed for these comparisons.\n\n## Sensitivity analyses\n\nTo assess robustness, we compared fixed-effects versus random-effects models, RD versus Peto OR as alternative effect measures, and examined the influence of continuity correction (adding 0.5 to all cells) for zero-event studies. Results were considered robust if direction and significance remained consistent across sensitivity analyses. Fixed-effects models were applied as a sensitivity analysis to examine whether the primary findings were robust to alternative modelling assumptions. Results were considered robust if the direction and magnitude of effect remained consistent across random- and fixed-effects models.\n\n## Trial sequential analysis\n\nTrial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed to quantify the risk of random error in cumulative meta-analyses[45](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR45 \"Wetterslev, J., Thorlund, K., Brok, J. & Gluud, C. Trial sequential analysis may establish when firm evidence is reached in cumulative meta-analysis. J. Clin. Epidemiol. 61, 64–75.                    https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2007.03.013                                     (2008).\"), thereby serving as a robustness check for the primary meta-analytic conclusions. The required information size (RIS) was calculated using α = 0.05, β = 0.20, with control event proportions and effect estimates derived from the included trials; diversity-adjusted RIS was used for TTRTP outcomes to account for heterogeneity, while the re-injury analyses used the control event proportion from the pooled data due to sparse events. RIS values are provided in the figure legends for each TSA plot. The O’Brien-Fleming monitoring boundary was applied for all TSA plots; futility boundaries were not used. Conventional meta-analysis declares statistical significance when the Z-curve crosses ± 1.96 (the conventional boundary for _P_ < 0.05). TSA imposes more stringent O’Brien-Fleming boundaries: crossing the monitoring boundary before reaching RIS indicates sufficient evidence; crossing the conventional 1.96 boundary but not the TSA boundary suggests potential false-positive results due to sparse data[46](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR46 \"Wetterslev, J., Jakobsen, J. C. & Gluud, C. Trial sequential analysis in systematic reviews with meta-analysis. BMC Med. Res. Methodol. 17, 39.                    https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-017-0315-7                                     (2017).\"). TSA was conducted using TSA 0.9.5.10 Beta, with trials entered in chronological order for each meta-analysis.\n\n### Grading of evidence quality using the GRADE approach\n\nTo evaluate the overall certainty of evidence for TTRTP and Re-injury outcomes, we applied the GRADE framework. Two reviewers independently rated the evidence using GRADEpro, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer[47](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR47 \"Guyatt, G. H. et al. GRADE: an emerging consensus on rating quality of evidence and strength of recommendations. BMJ 336, 924–926 (2008).\"), [48](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR48 \"Guyatt, G. et al. GRADE guidelines: 1. Introduction—GRADE evidence profiles and summary of findings tables. J. Clin. Epidemiol. 64, 383–394 (2011).\"). Evidence quality was downgraded for serious limitations in risk of bias (RoB 2), inconsistency (I² > 50%), indirectness, or imprecision. Publication bias was not formally assessed due to the limited number of studies per comparison (≤ 5). Upgrading was not applied as all evidence originated from RCTs, which begin at high certainty. For TTRTP, we prespecified a 7-day MCID. This threshold was established a priori in our PROSPERO protocol, is consistent with sample-size calculations used in prior hamstring rehabilitation trials[39](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR39 \"Hamilton, B. et al. Platelet-rich plasma does not enhance return to play in hamstring injuries: a randomised controlled trial. Br. J. Sports Med. 49, 943–950.                    https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094603                                     (2015).\"), and reflects the clinical judgment that a one-week reduction in RTP time is meaningful in athletic populations. Imprecision was judged by whether the 95% confidence interval crossed this MCID threshold, in addition to examining confidence interval width.\n\n## Results\n\n### Search results\n\nThe search strategy was conducted following PICOS guidelines[49](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR49 \"Methley, A. For TTRTP outcomes, downgrading for imprecision was applied when the 95% confidence interval crossed the MCID threshold of 7 days or when the optimal information size was not met; for re-injury outcomes, imprecision was assessed based on event counts and confidence interval width crossing the null. Inconsistency was downgraded when I² exceeded 50% and the direction of effects varied substantially, and risk of bias downgrades were based on the proportion of information from studies with “some concerns” or “high” RoB 2 ratings exceeding 33.3%. Return to sport particularly return to competition or full performance requires restoration of strength, neuromuscular control, and sport-specific function, which may not be fully captured by TTRTP criteria alone. Throughout our GRADE assessment, we prioritized clinical relevance (whether the 95% CI crossed the 7-day MCID) over nominal statistical significance (whether _p_ < 0.05), consistent with the principle that statistically significant effects may be clinically trivial, and non-significant effects may still be clinically meaningful when estimates are imprecise but suggest potential benefit. Interpretation of the pooled TTRTP findings is further complicated by variation in return-to-play definitions across the included studies. These definitions ranged from return to full training or unrestricted activity to return to match play or competitive sport and return to full sport participation or medical clearance. These thresholds represent different stages of functional recovery and may systematically influence the observed treatment effects. For instance, studies using earlier return-to-training criteria may yield shorter TTRTP estimates than those requiring match-play clearance, independent of the intervention’s true efficacy. This variation should be considered when comparing results across intervention categories and when interpreting the clinical applicability of the pooled estimates. Formal subgroup analysis by TTRTP definition level was not feasible due to the limited number of studies per category, and this constitutes a limitation of the current synthesis. In addition to recovering muscle strength, the mechanisms of protection from eccentric loading appear to involve numerous structural changes. When eccentrically loaded in a muscle lengthened state, the muscle is induced to generate new sarcomeres through the sequential addition of sarcomeres which results in an increase in fascicle length, thereby changing the muscle’s length-tension relationship towards the longer length range of operation, which is especially important for the biceps femoris long head as it experiences maximal musculotendon strain while the hamstrings are activated and lengthened at the terminal swing phase of a sprint[28](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR28 \"Marušič, J., Vatovec, R., Marković, G. & Šarabon, N. Effects of eccentric training at long-muscle length on architectural and functional characteristics of the hamstrings. Scand. J. Med. Sci. Sports. 30, 2130–2142.                    https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13770                                     (2020).\"). This type of architectural remodeling may allow the hamstrings to better absorb eccentric loads, reducing their risk of injury at specific joint angles during high-speed running[62](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR62 \"Andrews, M. H., Shield, A. J., Lichtwark, G. A. & Pincheira, P. A. Hamstring injury mechanisms and eccentric training-induced muscle adaptations: current insights and future directions. Sports Med. 55, 2429–2443.                    https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02291-6                                     (2025).\"). While these mechanisms are biologically plausible, they are derived from physiological studies and should be distinguished from the clinical evidence of reduced TTRTP. The L-protocol is also thought to be less likely than other aggressive eccentric regimes to create premature overloads and/or iatrogenic re-injuries because it utilizes moderate levels of intensity and is guided by pain to progress the program. Though existing data affirms eccentric loading, a few considerations must be borne in mind. The pooled estimate of −28.29 days reflects the weighted average of two trials with differing effect sizes (−23 days in footballers[50](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR50 \"Askling, C. M., Tengvar, M. & Thorstensson, A. Acute hamstring injuries in Swedish elite football: a prospective randomised controlled clinical trial comparing two rehabilitation protocols. Br. J. Sports Med. 47, 953–959.                    https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-092165                                     (2013).\") and − 37 days in sprinters/jumpers[51](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR51 \"Askling, C. M., Tengvar, M., Tarassova, O. & Thorstensson, A. Acute hamstring injuries in Swedish elite sprinters and jumpers: a prospective randomised controlled clinical trial comparing two rehabilitation protocols. Br. J. Sports Med. 48, 532–539.                    https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2013-093214                                     (2014).\")). The L-protocol trials included mostly male elite footballers and sprinters, rendering it uncertain whether findings can be generalized to female athletes, masters’ competitors, or recreational populations[63](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR63 \"Maeo, S. et al. Hamstrings hypertrophy is specific to the training exercise: Nordic hamstring versus lengthened state eccentric training. Med. Sci. Sports Exerc. 56, 1893–1905.                    https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003490                                     (2024).\"). Moreover, Vermeulen et al.[64](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR64 \"Vermeulen, R. et al. Early versus delayed lengthening exercises for acute hamstring injury in male athletes: a randomised controlled clinical trial. Br. J. Sports Med. 56, 792–800.                    https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-103405                                     (2022).\") did not find significant differences in TTRTP from introducing lengthening exercises early versus late (adjusted HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.56 to 1.60), suggesting that the timing of eccentric loading may be less important than once accepted-provided the exercises are ultimately incorporated. Future trials will then need to explore parameters governing optimal dosing (volume, frequency, and progression rate) and explore whether individual factors like injury grade, age, and baseline fascicle architecture modulate treatment response. Moderate-certainty evidence suggests that a lengthening-focused protocol (L-protocol) may expedite TTRTP when this is the clinical priority, although the evidence on re-injury prevention remains low certainty.\n\nAlthough eccentric loading schemes depend on a quick return to activity, rehabilitation techniques that utilize PATS are developed for a unique clinical focus: prevention of re-injury over the long-term. The results of our meta-analysis indicate that PATS programs do not reduce TTRTP significantly compared to control programs (STST or PRES). However, reconciling pooled risk with re-injury, an absolute risk reduction of 35% indicates a clinically meaningful effect. The point estimate (RD −0.35) suggests a potential reduction, but the 95% CI (−0.74 to 0.03) is wide and crosses the null; combined with very low certainty, high heterogeneity (I² = 72%), and inconclusive TSA, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy. The potential prophylactic effect of PATS may be related to its capacity to address biomechanical and neuromuscular deficits, although mediator data directly linking lumbopelvic control improvements to re-injury reduction are not available. Isolated hamstring strengthening, while effective for restoring local muscle force-generating capacity, fails to remediate the broader kinetic chain dysfunctions that contribute to tissue overload during sport-specific movements[65](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR65 \"Buhmann, R., Trajano, G. S., Kerr, G. K. & Shield, A. J. Increased short interval intracortical inhibition in participants with previous hamstring strain injury. Eur. J. Appl. Physiol. 122, 357–369.                    https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-021-04839-6                                     (2022).\"), [66](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR66 \"Summers, S. J., Chalmers, K. J., Wallwork, S. B., Leake, H. B. & Moseley, G. L. Interrogating cortical representations in elite athletes with persistent posterior thigh pain - new targets for intervention? J. Sci. Med. Sport. 24, 135–140.                    https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2020.07.003                                     (2021).\"), [67](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR67 \"Jankaew, A., Jan, Y. K., Yang, T. H., Wu, H. W. & Lin, C. F. Influence of hamstring injuries and vision on posterior chain muscle activation during challenging single-limb balance control among athletes with hamstring strain injuries. J. Musculoskelet. Neuronal Interact. 24, 343–352 (2024).\"). In contrast, PATS incorporates progressive trunk stabilization exercises designed to enhance lumbopelvic control-a factor increasingly recognized as integral to hamstring injury etiology. During the terminal swing phase of high-speed running, the biceps femoris long head undergoes peak musculotendon strain while actively lengthening to decelerate the lower limb. Insufficient lumbopelvic stability may allow excessive anterior pelvic tilt and trunk rotation, increasing the eccentric demand on the hamstrings and predisposing the muscle to overload injury. By improving lumbopelvic control, PATS may reduce compensatory pelvic motion and thereby mitigate eccentric hamstring strain during high-velocity running movements. A narrative review by Badrinath et al.[68](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR68 \"Badrinath, K., Crowther, R. G. & Lovell, G. A. Neuromuscular inhibition, hamstring strain injury, and rehabilitation: a review. J. Postgrad. Med. Educ. Res. 56, 179–184 (2022).\") concluded that athletes returning to sport after conventional rehabilitation exhibit persistent neuromuscular inhibition of both the hamstring and lumbopelvic musculature, manifesting as impaired myoelectric activity in the biceps femoris and altered activation patterns in the gluteal and trunk stabilizers. These residual deficits, undetectable by standard clinical clearance criteria (pain-free palpation, full isometric strength), may explain the alarmingly high early re-injury rates observed in protocols that neglect lumbopelvic retraining. The PATS re-injury analysis illustrates the challenge of interpreting sparse event data. The primary RD analysis was inconclusive, whereas the Peto OR sensitivity analysis suggested a large effect. This discrepancy likely reflects the instability of Peto OR under extreme effect sizes and sparse events, as well as the marked difference in baseline risk between the two studies. Given this uncertainty, the PATS finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.\n\nDespite the compelling evidence, important limitations temper clinical translation. Despite near-universal acceptance of lumbo-pelvic training in elite sport, interventions exploring its effect on hamstring injury rates have not measured stability in any meaningful way, and prospective risk factor studies examining lumbopelvic motion during sprinting remain scarce[69](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR69 \"Shield, A. J. & Bourne, M. N. Hamstring injury prevention practices in elite sport: evidence for eccentric strength vs. lumbo-pelvic training. Sports Med. 48, 513–524.                    https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0819-7                                     (2018).\"). Furthermore, the London International Consensus and Delphi Study on hamstring injuries reported that 91% of experts agreed it is “important to assess, treat and prescribe exercises addressing the whole kinetic chain”, yet consensus on specific exercise selection and progression criteria remains elusive. Future trials must define standardized PATS protocols and evaluate whether individual patient characteristics-including baseline lumbopelvic control, sport-specific biomechanical demands, and injury chronicity-modulate treatment response.\n\nPhysical modalities-including PRP, rESWT, and LLLT are frequently employed as adjuncts to exercise-based rehabilitation in athletes with hamstring strain injuries. However, these passive interventions do not restore eccentric strength, neuromuscular control, or sport-specific functional movement patterns, which are prerequisites for safe return to sport. Their appeal lies in the theoretical potential to modulate pain, accelerate tissue repair, and create a permissive environment for early loading. However, despite decades of clinical application, the evidence supporting their routine use remains inconsistent, and their role in hamstring strain injury management continues to generate considerable debate. Our stratified analyses demonstrate that these modalities should not be considered interchangeable. PRP has garnered particular interest due to its theoretical capacity to deliver concentrated growth factors to the injury site. A systematic review of six RCTs ( _n_ = 277) from 2026 found that PRP reduced TTRTP by a mean difference of −8.6 days (95% CI −3.04 to −0.03, _p_ = 0.045) compared with control, though heterogeneity was high (I² = 94.1%) and re-injury rate did not differ (15% vs. 16%, _p_ = 0.722). Similarly, Liu et al.[70](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR70 \"Liu, M. et al. Effect of platelet-rich plasma injection in hamstring injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis. J. Sport Rehabil. 35, 98–105.                    https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2024-0278                                     (2025).\") demonstrated shorter TTRTP with PRP plus physical therapy versus physical therapy alone (MD −2.29, 95% CI −4.11 to −0.47, _p_ = 0.01). Beyond PRP, Crupnik et al.[59](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR59 \"Crupnik, J. et al. Radial ESWT combined with a specific rehabilitation program (rESWT + RP) is more effective than sham rESWT + RP for acute hamstring muscle complex injury type 3b: a randomized, controlled trial. Br. Med. Bull. 155, ldaf009.                    https://doi.org/10.1093/bmb/ldaf009                                     (2025).\") reported that rESWT added to an 8-week rehabilitation program reduced TTRTP by approximately three days (25.4 ± 3.5 vs. 28.3 ± 4.5 days, _p_ = 0.037) while preserving strength symmetry in the injured leg. While there are positive indicators to support their use, several caveats should be pointed out before recommending their use. This study was unable to conclude whether there are any clinical benefits from stretching programs or physical modality interventions according to Jankaew et al.[71](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR71 \"Jankaew, A., Chen, J. C., Chamnongkich, S. & Lin, C. F. Therapeutic exercises and modalities in athletes with acute hamstring injuries: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Health. 15, 497–511.                    https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381221118085                                     (2023).\"). The LLLT RCT also demonstrated no advantage over exercise, indicating that not all modalities provide equivalent benefits. Furthermore, heterogeneity among PRP preparation (leukocyte-rich, leukocyte-poor, activated, un-activated), injection times and image-guide protocols make interpretation more difficult. Standardized protocols and controlled trials comparing head-to-head the clinical effectiveness and cost effectiveness of the various modalities; if applicable, will further clarify those that are beneficial beyond optimal continued rehabilitation through an exercise programme. Although PRP preparation protocols (leukocyte-rich versus leukocyte-poor) and injection timing varied across the three trials, we considered pooling them clinically appropriate because all evaluated PRP as an adjunct to structured rehabilitation in acute hamstring injuries, shared comparable outcome definitions (time to return to sport), and used similar control conditions (rehabilitation alone or placebo injection). The substantial statistical heterogeneity (I² = 70%) reflects the clinical diversity in PRP formulations and rehabilitation contexts, which limits the precision of the pooled estimate but does not invalidate the overall direction of effect. The clinical heterogeneity across PRP studies, including differences in injection protocols, rehabilitation programmes, and athlete populations, means that the pooled estimate should be viewed as a summary of available evidence rather than a precise effect size applicable to all clinical settings. Compared with recent systematic reviews[10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR10 \"Afonso, J. et al. Effectiveness of conservative interventions after acute hamstrings injuries in athletes: a living systematic review. Sports Med. 53, 615–635.                    https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01783-z                                     (2023).\"), which synthesized evidence on therapeutic exercises and modalities without stratification, our review provides a stratified synthesis that separates exercise-based interventions from adjunctive therapies. Unlike previous meta-analyses that pooled PRP across heterogeneous trials without GRADE or TSA contextualization[70](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR70 \"Liu, M. et al. Effect of platelet-rich plasma injection in hamstring injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis. J. Sport Rehabil. 35, 98–105.                    https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2024-0278                                     (2025).\"), we analyzed PRP separately from other modalities and interpreted findings according to evidence certainty rather than nominal statistical significance. Our findings on PRP are more conservative than those reported in recent meta-analyses[70](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR70 \"Liu, M. et al. Effect of platelet-rich plasma injection in hamstring injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis. J. Sport Rehabil. 35, 98–105.                    https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2024-0278                                     (2025).\"), which generally reported statistically significant effects based on pooled p-values without GRADE or TSA contextualization. This difference likely reflects our stricter eligibility criteria (acute injuries only, excluding tendinopathy), our stratified analytical approach (PRP analyzed separately from rESWT and LLLT), and our use of GRADE and TSA to distinguish suggestive from definitive evidence. Our incremental contribution lies in this stratified, certainty-based interpretation rather than in the identification of new primary studies.\n\nPain threshold rehabilitation appears to be an effective rehabilitation method that is safe. Hickey et al.[58](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR58 \"Hickey, J. T. et al. Pain-free versus pain-threshold rehabilitation following acute hamstring strain injury: a randomized controlled trial. J. Orthop. Sports Phys. Ther. 50, 91–103.                    https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2020.8895                                     (2020).\") did not find any cases of adverse events or re-injuries during rehabilitation and demonstrated a similar decline in fear of movement scores in the two groups, suggesting that the presence of pain would not promote kinesiophobia. The consistency of these findings with the larger body of literature on tendinopathy supports the generalizability of the pain threshold principle for other forms of musculoskeletal injury[72](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR72 \"Silbernagel, K. G., Thomeé, R., Eriksson, B. I. & Karlsson, J. Continued sports activity, using a pain-monitoring model, during rehabilitation in patients with Achilles tendinopathy: a randomized controlled study. Am. J. Sports Med. 35, 897–906.                    https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546506298279                                     (2007).\"), although the evidence for tendinopathy is indirect and should not be extrapolated to acute hamstring strains without caution. The application of this principle requires clinical judgement: pain ≤ 4/10 that resolves within 24 h of exercise is acceptable; however, if pain continues or worsens, then the athlete’s loads should be reduced. The use of pain threshold rehabilitation may reduce functional deficits and decrease the likelihood of future hamstring strains[62](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR62 \"Andrews, M. H., Shield, A. J., Lichtwark, G. A. & Pincheira, P. A. Hamstring injury mechanisms and eccentric training-induced muscle adaptations: current insights and future directions. Sports Med. 55, 2429–2443.                    https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02291-6                                     (2025).\"). Future studies are needed to determine if individual variables, such as injury severity, pain catastrophizing, and psychosocial readiness, affect the optimal pain threshold, and whether recurrent injuries occur after 6 months have passed. Overall, the available evidence supports that pain threshold rehabilitation is a safe, biologically based method for improving tissue adaptation without slowing the recovery process or increasing injury risk.\n\nThe future of HSI rehabilitation lies in precision medicine: leveraging multimodal data streams to stratify athletes by individual risk profiles and tailor interventions dynamically. Wearable sensors and AI may support personalized rehabilitation, but standardized algorithms and sensor-placement protocols are currently lacking[73](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR73 \"Dhahbi, W. & Chamari, K. The algorithmic athlete: a call to standardize assessment of sensor technologies and artificial intelligence. Int. J. Sports Physiol. Perform. 21, 505.                    https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2026-0035                                     (2026).\"). Imaging biomarkers also show promise for objective injury assessment[74](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR74 \"Riem, L. et al. A deep learning algorithm for automatic 3D segmentation and quantification of hamstrings musculotendon injury from MRI. Sci. Rep. 15, 33497.                    https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-16926-1                                     (2025).\"), [75](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR75 \"Sharma, A. et al. Integrating intramuscular fat radiomics with hamstrings-to-quadriceps structure and function ratios to predict future hamstring strain injury. PLOS Digit. Health. 4, e0001144.                    https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0001144                                     (2025).\"), though translation into practice requires standardized protocols and external validation[76](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-70693-1#ref-CR76 \"Dhahbi, W. et al. Application of artificial intelligence for predicting sports injuries and customizing personalized prevention strategies: a scoping review. Bioengineering 13, 692.                    https://doi.org/10.3390/bioengineering13060692                                     (2026).\"). These technologies are promising but remain in development and were not evaluated in this review. Nevertheless, the convergence of risk stratification, wearable monitoring, and AI-enabled analytics heralds a shift towards more personalized, technology-augmented care pathways.\n\n### Limitations\n\nSeveral limitations warrant consideration. Moderate to high heterogeneity (I² up to 72%) arose from variations in interventions, follow-up durations, and outcome definitions. Performance bias was unavoidable due to inability to blind participants and personnel in exercise-based trials. For re-injury outcomes, sparse events constrained precision; despite multiple analytical approaches, statistical power remains limited, with zero-event (LLLT) and single-study (rESWT) estimates precluding definitive conclusions. GRADE certainty for most outcomes ranged from low to very low, with only eccentric loading, LLLT, and pain-threshold rehabilitation on TTRTP reaching moderate certainty. Differences in TTRTP criteria, re-injury surveillance periods (2–24 months), and post-return sport exposure limited comparability; the small number of studies precluded formal subgroup analyses to assess the impact of definitional heterogeneity on pooled estimates, making heterogeneity estimates unreliable and requiring cautious interpretation of single-study and two-trial findings. The pooled L-protocol TTRTP effect should be interpreted as population-specific rather than universally applicable, given the inclusion of elite male athletes and the larger effect observed in sprinters/jumpers. Standardized functional outcomes were largely absent, precluding meta-analysis. Female and recreational athletes were underrepresented. TSA revealed inconclusive evidence for PATS on re-injury and PRP on both outcomes (PRP TTRTP reached RIS but not the O’Brien-Fleming boundary). One included study (Dhlamini et al.) was a master’s thesis with a low PEDro score (4/10; fair); its grey-literature status and poor methodological quality limit its interpretability, and it was therefore included only in narrative synthesis, consistent with our protocol. The English-language restriction may have contributed to language bias. Adherence to prescribed rehabilitation protocols was not uniformly reported across trials, which may contribute to heterogeneity in treatment effects. Adverse events were not systematically reported in most trials, precluding formal safety assessment.\n\n## Conclusions\n\nModerate-certainty evidence based on two trials suggests a lengthening-focused protocol (L-protocol) may shorten TTRTP. PATS shows a non-significant trend toward re-injury reduction (very low certainty), but this finding is preliminary. Among adjunctive therapies, rehab + PRP may offer modest TTRTP acceleration (low certainty), rESWT shows preliminary promise requiring replication, and LLLT does not appear to add benefit. Pain-threshold rehabilitation enhances strength without delaying TTRTP (moderate certainty, single trial). Most evidence remains low or very low certainty. These findings support intervention-specific decision-making based on clinical priorities, while acknowledging that current evidence does not identify a single strategy that reliably optimizes both rapid return and durable protection. Future trials should use standardized injury and return-to-sport definitions, report exposure-adjusted recurrence and adverse events, include sex-balanced samples, and register protocols before recruitment.\n\n## Data availability\n\nAll data supporting the findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis are presented within the main manuscript and its tables.
