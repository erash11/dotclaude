# Verkhoshansky: The Shock Method Is a Forced Shock Stretch of Tensed Muscle, a Different Entity From "Plyometrics", and He Is Not Its "Father"

**What it is** — Section 9.2 is a first-person paper by Verkhoshansky ("Dr. Yuri Verkhoshansky" under the heading, PDF 574 / p.563). He defines the method: "The regime, in which an external resistance is actively overcome after being preceded by a sharp preliminary stretch of the muscles, is most effective for training explosive strength (Verkhoshansky, 1960). This technique is called the Shock Method for development of explosive strength and reactive ability (Verkhoshansky, 1961)." He then says: "in USA the Shock Method is most often called *plyometrics*. This is not correct. The Shock Method and plyometric training are different entities" (PDF 574 / p.563). Under "I am not the father of plyometrics" he says being called "father of plyometrics" "gives me pleasure to hear this, but it is incorrect"; the term "to name my shock method, was invented by my friend, Fred Wilt", a former Olympic runner, NCAA champion and Purdue track coach, with whom he "corresponded ... in the 1960's" (PDF 575 / p.564). He warns that "plyometrics" "could be confused with the term 'pliometric'", the old anatomy term for the "yielding" regime in which "the external load on the muscles is much greater than the tension developed during the contraction, when the muscles are stretching" (footnote 23 cites Faulkner, 2003, J Appl Physiol 95: 455-459, and Faulkner et al., 1997, MSSE 29(5)). His defining feature: "The specific characteristic of my method is expressed in **the shock stretch of the muscle under tension**. This method has a compulsory (forced) character when the neuromuscular system is forced to display a high level of power against a significant external mechanical influence" (PDF 575 / p.564, bold on the page; all quotes checked against the page images).

**Why it works** — His stated reasoning: the shock stretch of strained muscle is "a high powered training influence for the neuromuscular system" because the system is forced, by an external mechanical impact, to produce high power (PDF 575 / p.564). The forcing comes from outside (the fall), not from the athlete's intent. Inference: this is why he separates it from "plyometric training": a jump the athlete starts voluntarily, with no imposed impact, lacks the "compulsory" element he names as the method's specific characteristic. He does not spell out which drills fall on which side.

**When to apply** — When a programme lists "plyometrics", use his test: is there an external impact that sharply stretches muscle already under tension (a depth jump, a drop catch), or only a self-started jump? Only the first is his shock method. This sharpens the 1.6 terminology entry ([[framework--shock-method-and-plyometric-terminology]]), which reports the authors' wider point that Western "plyometrics" drifted to cover ordinary jumping. Eric's baseline: "we incorporate the countermovement jump and the isometric mid-thigh pull to get a dynamic strength index, to determine whether we need more plyometric training or more max-strength training" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 76, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q21)). Inference: when DSI points to "more plyometric training", his distinction asks whether that means shock-method depth jumps or submaximal jumping, which carry different loads.

**When NOT to apply** — The history is his own account, with no source beyond the footnotes on Wilt's books (footnote 22, a bookfinder URL) and the Faulkner terminology papers. The publisher's back cover (PDF 590, not part of 9.2) calls him "the Russian researcher who invented plyometric training"; that is the publisher's wording, which his own text rejects. "Different entities" is asserted, not defined drill by drill, so do not use it to rule a specific drill in or out without the impact test above (Inference). The 1960 and 1961 dates are his self-citations; the 9.2 paper gives no bibliography for them.

**Example** — His own: the depth jump, where landing from a height imposes "a sharp preliminary stretch" on tensed leg extensors before the take-off (PDF 574-575 / pp.563-564).

# How Depth Jumps Began: Heavy Half Squats Hurt His Jumpers' Backs, and Triple-Jump Take-Off Forces Pointed to a Shock Load Instead

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky's first-person account (PDF 574-575 / pp.563-564, checked against the page images). In the mid-1950s he coached high-level jumpers and was "a senior coach in the sprint and jump events in Moscow". He was "one of the first coaches in Russia to use a barbell" for athletes, with a first article in 1957; a main exercise was "the full barbell squat using a weight of 120 kg which gave good results." He then switched to the half squat "with much more barbell weight"; his athletes "could easily execute a half squat with 140-160 kg", but "the next day none of them came to train. They phoned me and told me about pain in the small of the back. I understood that that I had made a serious error in my strength training system" (PDF 574-575; "that that" is on the page). He then recalled his study of triple-jump take-off biodynamics (book published 1961): "the mean magnitude of dynamic effort in the take off for the second jump (step) is about 300-350 kg and lasts only 120 ms. In this instance the effort has a shock type of work when muscles are stretched very sharply and then are very quickly and very powerfully contracted." So he "decided that depth jumping, with landing and takeoff on both legs, would be an effective means of reactive ability improvement. Experiments have confirmed this hypothesis." The method spread through Fred Wilt and Michael Yessis (USA), Peter Tschiene (Germany), Mel Siff (South Africa) and Carmelo Bosco (Italy) (PDF 575 / p.564; the page prints "Carmello").

**Why it works** — His account gives the numbers and the decision: the step take-off effort (300-350 kg in about 120 ms) is "a shock type of work when muscles are stretched very sharply and then are very quickly and very powerfully contracted", so he chose depth jumping. Inference: the implied contrast is that a heavy, slow squat does not reproduce that pattern while a depth jump does, with body mass and fall height supplying the load instead of a bar on the spine; the page does not make the comparison in those words. Inference: the same contrast appears in 5.2.4, where heavy squats are said to train mainly quasi-isometric strength and to place "a large and generally unnecessary load on the spine" ([[claim--heavy-squats-and-light-fast-lifts-miss-the-eccentric-concentric-switch]]).

**When to apply** — As a reasoning pattern: when a loaded exercise is chosen to raise a sport's peak force, compare its force and time with the sport's key action. Inference: his numbers put the step take-off effort at about twice the heaviest half-squat bar load he used (bodyweight aside), delivered in roughly a tenth of a second, so heavier half squats chase the wrong target. Eric's baseline: training time is weighted toward "high motor-unit-recruitment core/ballistic movements (squats, cleans, jumps, throws, sprints)" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 104, Inferred (transcript + slides)). Inference: his story is an argument for keeping jumps and shock work beside the squat in that mix, not for dropping the squat; he kept the full squat.

**When NOT to apply** — This is an anecdote. No athlete count, injury records or experiment details are given, and "Experiments have confirmed this hypothesis" has no data or citation on these pages. "300-350 kg" is a force stated in kilograms (kilogram-force) with no bodymass, so it cannot be turned into multiples of bodyweight here. The back pain followed one session of heavier half squats; the page does not say whether load, range, technique or the jump from 120 kg to 140-160 kg caused it. Do not read the story as evidence that half squats are unsafe in general.

**Example** — The step take-off in the triple jump: about 300-350 kg of mean effort over about 120 ms (PDF 575 / p.564).

# Verkhoshansky's 12-Week Leg Program: Heavy Squats, Then Loaded Jumps and Interval Squats, Then Depth Jumps, Then Box Jumps

**What it is** — "Program to develop explosive strength and reactive ability of the leg muscles" (PDF 578-582 / pp.567-571; every number below was read from the page images, since the table OCR is unreliable). Twelve weeks, two sessions a week for weeks 1-8, then "From the 9th week on, from day 17 - 28, workouts are executed three times a week" (the table numbers these as sessions 17-28). Four means: depth jump; barbell squat; consecutive barbell jumps, either squat jumps with the bar on the shoulders ("thighs are at least parallel to the floor", underlined on the page) or barbell scissor-lunge jumps (Fig 19); and box jumps, "Consecutive jumps onto a box and down using a double leg takeoff", box "0.4 - 0.8 m" (Fig 20). Loads are % of squat 1RM; "8 x 10 (2)" means 2 series of 8 sets of 10 (PDF 578 / p.567). The phases:

| Weeks (sessions) | Content |
|---|---|
| 1-3 (1-6) | Squats only. Heavy days pair 90% or 93-95% (2-4 x 3-5 or 3-5 x 2-3, rest 4-6 min) with 80-85% volume sets (2-3 x 10-12 to 3-5 x 8-10); pyramid days (80% 1 x 10, 90% 1 x 5, 90-95% 1 x 2; or 70% 1 x 12, 80% 1 x 10, 85% 1 x 7) repeated 2-3 times |
| 4-5 (7-10) | Consecutive barbell jumps at 40%, 5 x 8 (2) then 8 x 8 (2), 60 s between sets; squats at 85-90% then 95% "using the interval method", 4-5 x 1-3 |
| 6-8 (11-16) | Depth jumps, 3-4 sets of 10 per session in total, mostly split between 0.75 m and 1.10 m (session 14: 4 x 10 from 0.75 m only), then 6-12 min rest, then barbell jumps at 40%, 6-10 sets of 8-10, set rest falling from 60 s to 30 s to 10 s |
| 9-11 (17-25) | Three sessions a week: two depth-jump days (4 x 10 from 1.10 m or 0.75 m, then barbell jumps 8-10 x 10 in 2-4 series) and one box-jump day (0.4-0.5 m 4 x 40 (2); 0.5-0.6 m 6 x 40 (2); 0.7-0.8 m 5 x 50 (3)) |
| 12 (26-28) | Barbell jumps 10 x 10 (3-4), 10 s between sets, twice; box jumps 0.7-0.8 m 6 x 50 (3), 2 min between sets |

Footnote 24: after the last 85% squat rep "it is necessary to do 2-3 additional repetitions" (PDF 579 / p.568). Footnote 25 defines the interval method (see [[framework--interval-resistance-training-and-cluster-sets]], Also in 9.2). The rules that follow the table are in [[heuristic--keep-the-order-of-means-and-reset-jump-load-to-40-percent-of-current-squat-max]].

**Recorded as printed (Inference: likely misprints):** sessions 12 and 13 list "Depth Jumps (0.10 m.)" as the second height, where every other session pairs 0.75 m with 1.10 m (PDF 580 / p.569); session 11 prints "8 x 10 (6)", six series, where every other barbell-jump row has 2 to 4 (PDF 580); the text says "day 17 - 28" for what the table calls training sessions (PDF 578).

**Why it works** — His stated principle, printed after the table: "gradually increasing the specificity and intensity of the training load", so "each training load prepares the organism for effective execution the following loads" (PDF 582 / p.571). Inference: the order (maximal strength first, then light loaded jumps, then depth jumps, then high-contact box jumps) matches the strength-first sequences reported in 5.3 and 6.9.1 ([[heuristic--sequence-jumps-then-barbell-then-depth-jumps-across-stages]], [[case-study--barbell-and-depth-jump-sequences-in-high-jumpers]]). The drop heights match the 0.75-1.15 m range of 4.2.3 ([[claim--optimal-depth-jump-drop-height-for-power-vs-force]]), with 1.10 m, not 1.15 m, as the high one.

**When to apply** — As a template for an off-season reactive block for jumpers or other leg-dominant power athletes who already squat heavily, keeping the phase order and the 40% barbell-jump load. Eric's baseline: "Prilepin's chart (total reps and reps-per-set by %1RM) is the default guide for daily rep/set assignment within a training block" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 107, Inferred/Explicit (shared team material)). Inference: the weeks 1-3 squat rows (for example 3-5 x 2-3 at 93-95%, 2 x 8 + 2-3 at 85%) can be checked against that chart before use.

**When NOT to apply** — No outcome data, population or sport is given for the program; it is a prescription. The contact counts are large: 10 x 10 barbell jumps in 3-4 series is 300-400 loaded jumps in a session, and 6 x 50 box jumps in 3 series is 900 contacts, which, Inference, sits awkwardly beside his own rule that "It is the quality of the shock method, not the quantity that is important" (PDF 577 / p.566, item 7). Inference: the barbell and box jumps are submaximal rebound work, not shock-method depth jumps, which stay at 4 x 10 or less per session, within his item 2 cap (PDF 577). His conclusions restrict the shock method to "experienced top level athletes" (PDF 588 / p.577), so this is not a youth or novice program ([[principle--reserve-the-shock-method-for-experienced-top-level-athletes-not-youth]]). In-season football weeks do not fit a three-session-a-week reactive block (Inference).

**Example** — Session 16 (week 8): depth jumps 0.75 m 2 x 10 and 1.10 m 2 x 10, 6-8 min rest, then barbell jumps at 40% 8 x 10 in 2-3 series, 30 s between sets and 8-12 min between series (PDF 581 / p.570).

# Running a Reactive Block: Keep the Means in Order, Work Every Set at Maximum Intensity, Re-Set Jump Load to 40% of the Current Squat Max, and Rest Actively Between Series

**What it is** — Under "Important additional information about the program", after the 12-week leg program, Verkhoshansky gives these rules (PDF 582 / p.571, checked against the page image):
1. Scope: "This program is directed to the improvement of leg muscles. For the muscles of hands and arms, shoulder-girdle and trunk, parallel strength programs should be used."
2. Order: "the principle of gradually increasing the specificity and intensity of the training load used in the program. Therefore, re-arrangement of the means (changing their location in program) is not recommended because each training load prepares the organism for effective execution the following loads".
3. Effort: "each portion of the work in one set is executed with maximum intensity".
4. Jump load: "the weight used in the jumps should be increased when the maximum strength of legs is increased and should be equal to 40% of maximum in the barbell squat".
5. Rest between series: "the character of the rest pause between series has great significance for preservation of the specific work-capacity of players during the training session. A small volume of work with low to moderate intensity executed by the same groups of muscles during the rest pause is effective."

**Why it works** — His stated reasons are in rules 2 and 5: each load prepares the next, and active rest by the same muscles preserves "specific work-capacity". Rule 4 has no stated reason. Inference: tying the jump load to the current squat maximum keeps the relative load constant as strength rises in weeks 1-5, so the loaded jumps stay at the same point on the force-velocity curve. Inference: rule 2 is the conjugate-sequence idea applied inside one block ([[framework--conjugate-sequence-system-of-training-means]]).

**When to apply** — When running his program or one built on it. Re-test or re-estimate the squat maximum after the heavy squat weeks and reset the 40% bar before the jump phases. Keep the phases in his order rather than shuffling sessions to fit a calendar. Fill the 8-15 min rests between series with light work by the same muscles rather than sitting. Eric's baseline: "Velocity-based 1RM estimation ... is preferred over live 1RM attempts" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 67, Legacy (interview 2026-09-28, Q8, Q10)). Inference: an estimated squat maximum is a workable base for the 40% rule; the book assumes a measured 1RM (PDF 578 / p.567).

**When NOT to apply** — No study, population or outcome is given for any rule. "Maximum intensity" for each set sits oddly beside sets of 10 jumps at 10 s rest in weeks 8-12; Inference: it most plausibly means maximal effort within each jump, not a maximal load. Rule 5 calls the athletes "players", although the program is for leg power generally; the page does not name a sport. The 40% figure is for the consecutive barbell jumps in this program; in the starting-acceleration program the loaded vertical jump is "30-50%" in the list and "50-60%" in the table (PDF 583-585), so 40% is not a universal jump load. Do not extend rule 2 to forbid any change: it is "not recommended", not banned.

**Example** — An athlete whose squat rises from 150 kg to 160 kg over weeks 1-3 moves the barbell-jump bar from 60 kg to 64 kg before session 7 (Inference: worked example of rule 4; the book gives no numbers).

# Verkhoshansky's 6-Week Starting-Acceleration Program: Squats, Loaded and Kettlebell Jumps, Depth Jumps, Multiple Bounds, Stadium Jumps and Short Hills

**What it is** — "Program for Perfecting Starting Acceleration" (PDF 583-586 / pp.572-575; numbers read from the page images, since the table OCR scrambles the rows). It "is executed two times a week for six weeks for a total of 12 training sessions" with seven means (PDF 583-584):
1. Barbell squats at "80-90% of maximum for development of maximum strength and for toning the central nervous system".
2. Vertical jumps with the bar on the shoulders (Fig 21) "using a weight of 30-50% of maximum", for explosive strength.
3. Kettlebell squat jumps (Fig 22), 16, 24 or 32 kg "chosen depending upon the athlete", for reactive ability; a dumbbell will do if no kettlebell is available.
4. Depth jumps, for explosive strength and reactive ability.
5. Six-fold and ten-fold "leg-to-leg bounds from place executed with maximum effort", for "repeated explosive effort and anaerobic capacity".
6. Stadium jumps up stairs, "for development of maximal anaerobic capacity", "2-3 steps at a time with an active take-off in an upward and forward direction".
7. Uphill running, 15-25 m, "at a low rate with emphasis on a forward take-off and active swing movements of the leg. Running with half bent legs like dribbling is a huge mistake: it lowers the specificity of the training influence of this exercise."

The sessions: 1, 2 and 4 are squats only (85-90% 2-3 x 5-6; then pyramids); 3 and 7 pair bar jumps with six-fold bounds; 5 pairs depth jumps (0.75 m, 2 x 10) with ten-fold bounds; 6 pairs kettlebell squat jumps 3 x 8 with ten-fold bounds; 7, 9 and 11 end with uphill runs 1 x 5-6 after 15 min rest; 8, 10 and 12 are depth jumps 4 x 10 (0.75 m in session 8, 1.10 m in 10 and 12) then stadium jumps 4 x 10 in 2-3 series; 9 and 11 pair squats at 93-95% 2 x 2-3 with depth jumps from 1.10 m, 2 x 10 (PDF 584-586 / pp.573-575). Rests are long: 4-6 min between sets and 8-12 min between series.

**Recorded as printed (Inference: likely errors):** the exercise list gives the bar jumps at "30-50% of maximum" (PDF 583), but sessions 3 and 7 prescribe "Vertical jumps with bar (50-60%)" (PDF 584-585). Session 2 gives "(93-95%)" for "1 x 12" (PDF 584 / p.573), while the same pyramid in the leg program puts 1 x 2 at the top load (PDF 579 / p.568). The book does not reconcile either.

**Why it works** — His stated purposes are per means (list above): maximal strength and CNS "toning" from squats, explosive strength from loaded jumps, reactive ability from kettlebell and depth jumps, repeated explosive effort and anaerobic capacity from bounds and stairs, and a specific forward take-off from hills. Inference: the program moves from mostly squats (sessions 1-4) toward depth jumps, stairs and hills (sessions 8-12), the same rising specificity as the leg program.

**When to apply** — A short pre-season block for athletes whose event starts from rest (sprint starts, football get-off), who already squat and jump. The hill cue is concrete: forward take-off and active leg swing, not a shuffling, bent-leg run. Eric's baseline: "0-10m is read as an acceleration score (reflecting maximal strength/power/slow stretch-shortening-cycle development)" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 94, Legacy (interview 2026-09-28, Q8)). Inference: that reading matches his mix of heavy squats and loaded jumps for starting acceleration; a 0-10 m split before and after the six weeks would test it.

**When NOT to apply** — No outcome data, sport, level or test is given. Stadium jumps and bounds are aimed at "anaerobic capacity", which is not a starting-acceleration quality; Inference: they serve repeat-effort ability, and could be cut for an athlete who only needs single starts. Depth jumps from 1.10 m in four sessions limit this to athletes who meet his "experienced top level" condition ([[principle--reserve-the-shock-method-for-experienced-top-level-athletes-not-youth]]). Do not quote the bar-jump load without the 30-50% and 50-60% conflict.

**Example** — Session 9: squats at 93-95% 2 x 2-3 paired with depth jumps from 1.10 m 2 x 10, the pair repeated twice with 10-12 min between series; then 15 min rest and 5-6 uphill runs of 15-25 m with 4-6 min between (PDF 585 / p.574).

# Cue Maximal Jump Height, Not Knee Angle; When the Dip Must Be Standard, Set It With Plinths Instead of Instructions

**What it is** — Two notes in the starting-acceleration program (PDF 583 / p.572, checked against the page image):
- Vertical jumps with the bar on the shoulders: "In this exercise, as in the depth jump, the athlete must be motivated to attain the maximum jump height without thinking about the knee angle at the end of 'yielding' (pliometric) phase." Footnote 26 adds the execution: bar on the back of the shoulders, feet "about hip-width apart", "Quickly squat down and jump straight up as high as you can. Stop and shake your legs (you can rack the bar if desired)", and "the previous pliometric phase must be executed in the way that assures the consecutive best jump height automatically, thanks to the athlete's correct understanding of the motor concepts involved in this exercise."
- Kettlebell squat jumps, footnote 27: consecutive vertical jumps "standing on two high parallel benches or plinths. During execution of this exercise, the kettlebell must come close to the floor in the low down position, but should not touch the floor. In this way, one can ensure a standard leg flexion angle, determined by the height of the plinths." Fig 22 shows three frames: standing on two stands with the bell between the feet, squatting with the bell near the floor between the stands, and jumping up (an up-down arrow beside the figure).

**Why it works** — His stated reason for the first: the yielding phase should organise itself "automatically" around the goal of best height, through "correct understanding of the motor concepts" (footnote 26). For the second, his stated reason is that the plinths "ensure a standard leg flexion angle" (footnote 27). Inference: the set-up means the athlete need not think about depth, and both remove joint-angle attention from the athlete during the jump; one leaves depth to the goal, the other builds it into the set-up.

**When to apply** — When coaching loaded or depth jumps, give the outcome (jump as high as possible, reach a marker) rather than a knee-angle instruction. When a fixed squat depth matters (for comparing sets, or for a weaker athlete who would sink too deep), use a physical stop such as plinths or a box under the bell. This matches the 9.2 depth-jump technique note to reach "some height, indicated by a marker" (PDF 576 / p.565; see [[heuristic--depth-jump-execution-cues-landing-gaze-breath]]).

**When NOT to apply** — He gives no study; the cue is a coaching opinion. It does not override the leg program's squat-jump instruction that thighs go "at least parallel to the floor" (PDF 578 / p.567), which is a depth instruction for a different exercise. Inference: an athlete who lands with knees collapsing inward still needs a technique correction; "don't think about the knee angle" is about depth at the end of the dip, not about knee alignment. The plinth set-up adds a fall risk with a loaded bell, which he does not discuss.

**Example** — His Fig 22: kettlebell squat jumps between two plinths, the bell stopping just short of the floor each rep so every rep reaches the same knee flexion (PDF 583 / p.572).

# Verkhoshansky's 6-Week Ball-Throwing Speed Program: Heavy (80%) Then Light (40%) Throws Against a Cable Apparatus, Sized From Maximal Effort in the Start Position

**What it is** — "Program for improvement of ball throwing speed" (PDF 586-588 / pp.575-577, read from the page images). It combines "light and heavy weight throwing using a special training apparatus" (Fig 23, two drawings: an athlete beside a tall vertical guide carrying a weight, and an athlete in the throwing position holding a ball attached by a horizontal cable to a gridded frame; the drawings do not show how the parts connect). Load setting: "measure the maximum effort that can be displayed by the player in the starting position for the ball throw. The light weight is equal 40% and the heavy one, 80% of this value. For example, if the maximum effort is 20 kg, the training weights will be equal to 8 kg and 18 kg respectively" (PDF 586 / p.575). Method: "the repeat-serial method. One series includes the heavy and the light weight with the first exercise executed with the heavy weight"; "a series of 1x4 with heavy weight and then 2x7 with light weight" is one set of 4 heavy then 2 sets of 7 light; 1-2 min between sets, series repeated "several times" with 4-6 min between; when maximal effort rises, "the training weights should be increased accordingly" (PDF 586). The schedule (PDF 587-588 / pp.576-577) runs five days a week for weeks 1-5 and four days in week 6. Heavy sets rise from 1 x 4 to 1 x 6; light work ranges from 1 x 8 to 4 x 8; series per session rise from 2 (weeks 1-3) to 3 (week 4) and 4 (week 5, days 1-2), then return to 2 (week 5 days 3-5, week 6), with several days mixing two series formats.

**Recorded as printed:** 80% of 20 kg is 16 kg, not the printed 18 kg (Inference: arithmetic or typographic error; 40% of 20 kg = 8 kg is right). Week 1 day 5 prints "1 x 4 with heavy weight and 2x3 with light weight" (PDF 587 / p.576), out of line with the 2 x 7 to 4 x 8 light sets around it (Inference: possible misprint for 2 x 8).

**Why it works** — He gives no mechanism on these pages beyond the heavy-first order. Inference: it is a heavy-light contrast series of the kind the authors classify among contrast methods in 7.1 ([[framework--contrast-methods-load-release-pneumatics-heavy-light-set]]), and loads set from effort in the throwing start position keep the resistance specific to that joint configuration. The 5.2.3 implement findings warn that too heavy an implement can harm technique ([[claim--implement-mass-for-speed-strength-keep-technique-and-size-per-athlete]]); the 80% heavy load here is a cable resistance, not a heavier ball.

**When to apply** — An off-season block for throwing athletes (the text says "players"; the sport is not named) with access to a cable or pulley that can be set from a measured isometric effort in the throwing start position. Keep heavy first in each series, set loads from a fresh maximum, and re-test. Plan around the expected loss of throwing feel ([[heuristic--warn-throwers-the-feel-of-the-ball-fades-and-allow-2-3-weeks-to-restore]]).

**When NOT to apply** — No outcome, population or throwing-speed data is given. The 18 kg example contradicts the 80% rule; use the rule, not the example. The apparatus is drawn, not specified (pulley ratio, handle, load path), so the 40%/80% loads cannot be copied to a different machine without re-measuring. Five days a week for six weeks with throwing technique degraded from week 3 (PDF 588 / p.577) does not fit an in-season period.

**Example** — Week 4, day 3: 1 x 4 heavy and 2 x 8 light, the series repeated 3 times, then 1 x 4 heavy and 3 x 8 light (PDF 587 / p.576).

# A Throwing-Strength Block Temporarily Costs "the Feeling of the Ball": Warn the Players, Keep Technical Goals Out of It, Then Rest the Arm a Week and Allow 2-3 Weeks

**What it is** — "Important additional information about the program" after the ball-throwing program (PDF 588 / p.577, checked against the page image):
1. "It is possible that players will lose 'the feeling of the ball' (the feel of the throw) when the strength program is used (especially beginning the 3rd week). This is a natural result of the strength training. The coach must explain the cause of this phenomenon to players. He must also tell them that it is a temporary phenomenon and that the 'feeling of throwing' will come back when the program is completed."
2. "The low ability to throw the ball as a result of the strength training should be kept in mind when technical-tactical mastery is an aim of training."
3. "The weeks following the strength program play an important role in the training process. First of all, the load on the muscles of the throwing arm must be excluded during the first week. Then the power of throwing should be increased gradually. As a rule, 2-3 weeks is enough for the restoration of the feeling of throwing."

**Why it works** — He calls the loss "a natural result of the strength training" and gives no mechanism. Inference: this is the pattern the book describes as the long-term delayed training effect, where voluminous strength loading depresses speed-strength preparedness during the block and the gain appears after it ([[principle--long-term-delayed-training-effect-from-volume-above-the-optimum]]); 5.3.1 also reports that heavy strength volume can reduce explosiveness and technique ([[claim--excess-heavy-strength-volume-impairs-speed-strength-and-technique]]).

**When to apply** — Before any concentrated strength or contrast block for throwers or other skill-speed athletes: tell athletes and sport coaches in advance that feel may drop from about week 3 and why; do not schedule technical-tactical goals that depend on throwing feel inside the block; after it, a week with no throwing-arm load, then a gradual build over 2-3 weeks. Eric's baseline: the department's game week ends "lifting and hard practice ... 72 hours before a Saturday game" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 186, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q15)). Inference: a 2-3 week restoration window is far longer than an in-season week allows, so blocks like this belong off-season.

**When NOT to apply** — The timings (week 3 onset, one week off, 2-3 weeks to restore) are his rule of thumb for this program, with no data, sport or athlete numbers. They should not be transferred to a different strength program without checking. "Feeling" is subjective and he gives no way to measure it. Inference: a drop in throwing feel that persists past 2-3 weeks, or comes with pain, is a reason to look for overload or injury, not a sign to wait longer.

**Example** — His own: after the six-week heavy-light throwing program, no throwing-arm loading in week 7, then gradually rising throwing power, with feel back by about weeks 8-9 (PDF 588 / p.577; week numbers counted by this entry).

# Reserve the Shock Method for Experienced Top-Level Athletes; High, High-Volume Depth Jumps in Young Athletes Are "Very Injurious"

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky's first two conclusions (PDF 588 / p.577, checked against the page image): "In many countries I was able to see that young athletes were using the depth jump from great heights and with high volume. This is not correct. It is very injurious." And: "The particular virtue of the shock method is its capability for significantly influencing the training effect on man's neuromuscular system. Therefore, this method should be used only by experienced top level athletes. As for young athletes, there are many other effective exercises that they should use." Conclusion 3 follows: "It is necessary to emphasize that it is impossible, in any circumstance, to overrate the potential of the shock method." Inference: read literally, conclusion 3 says the method's potential cannot be exaggerated; read with the next paragraph ("only one of the many ways of special strength training and is not meant to replace the other ones", PDF 589 / p.578), it more likely means the potential must not be overrated. The page does not settle which; quote it as printed.

**Why it works** — His reason is the strength of the stimulus: because the method influences the neuromuscular system so strongly, "this method should be used only by experienced top level athletes" (PDF 588). The 9.2 depth-jump recommendations add the preconditions: "special preliminary preparation such as a significant volume of barbell exercises and traditional jumping, short sprints and skipping", normal jumping technique and "the correct lumbar-pelvic rhythm" first, and "Great caution" with one-legged, direction-changing or complex variants (PDF 577 / p.566, items 1, 8 and 9).

**When to apply** — Deciding whether an athlete gets true depth jumps (drops from height with a maximal rebound). Eric's baseline: the Vermeil hierarchy, "work capacity, then strength, then power, then speed, then sport skill, each level a required foundation for the next", is the department's programming "compass" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 101, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q1)). Inference: his rule places the shock method at the top of the power level, after a strength base; freshmen and low-training-age athletes get submaximal jumps, hops and loaded jumps instead.

**When NOT to apply** — "Young", "great heights", "high volume" and "experienced top level" are not defined; no injury data is shown. His rule is narrower than it sounds when set beside 5.2.4, where the authors argue that plyometric injury risk comes from prescription, overload and overuse, rather than from the method ([[claim--plyometric-injury-risk-comes-from-prescription-not-the-method]]), and that the 1.5-2 x bodymass squat prerequisite applies to maximal depth jumps, not to submaximal plyometrics ([[claim--squat-1-5-to-2-bodymass-rule-applies-to-maximal-depth-jumps-only]]). These are not the same claim: his conclusion is about the shock method (maximal depth jumps), theirs about plyometrics broadly. Recorded as a difference in scope, not a conflict. Do not use his conclusion to bar youth from all jumping.

**Example** — His own observation: young athletes in "many countries" doing depth jumps "from great heights and with high volume", which he calls "very injurious" (PDF 588 / p.577).

# The Shock Method Is One Means of Special Strength Training, Not a Replacement, and Gets a Definite Place Set by Evaluation and the Stage of the Year

**What it is** — The closing paragraph of 9.2 (PDF 589 / p.578, the last page with text, checked against the page image): "The shock method is only one of the many ways of special strength training and is not meant to replace the other ones. The shock method should assume a definite place in the training system. This is determined by a full evaluation of the athlete's preparation and timed to coincide specifically with a definite stage of the yearly training cycle. The motor specifics of the particular sport, the objective of the training, the level of athlete's preparedness and competition calendar must all be taken into account." Earlier in 9.2 he names the stage: "Depth jumps occupy a fundamental place in the second half of the preparatory period of the yearly cycle", with a maintenance role in the competition period, "once every 10-14 days, but no later the 5-6 days before competition" (PDF 577 / p.566, item 5; "no later the" is on the page).

**Why it works** — He gives no mechanism in this paragraph. Inference: it follows from his other claims in the paper: the method's stimulus is strong ([[principle--reserve-the-shock-method-for-experienced-top-level-athletes-not-youth]]), each load in a program "prepares the organism" for the next (PDF 582 / p.571), so the shock method has to come after the means that prepare for it, not instead of them.

**When to apply** — Annual planning. Decide the shock method's slot from four inputs he names (sport's motor specifics, training objective, preparedness, competition calendar), with the late preparatory period as his default. Eric's baseline: "Off-season strength work prioritizes relative strength and technique first; already-strong athletes shift more time toward strength-speed/speed-strength work. In-season, the stated rule is "maintain intensity, reduce volume"" (`docs/method-baseline.md` line 105, Inferred (transcript + shared slides)). Inference: his placement, depth jumps late in the preparatory period and sparse in season, fits that line.

**When NOT to apply** — The paragraph is a general position with no criteria for the "full evaluation". It does not say which other special-strength means the shock method should sit beside. His in-season cut-off of 5-6 days before competition differs from the 10 days in 5.2.4 for the same depth-jump rule ([[heuristic--depth-jump-weekly-frequency-and-seasonal-placement]], Also in 9.2); quote both.

**Example** — His 12-week leg program places depth jumps only from week 6, after five weeks of heavy squats, loaded jumps and interval squats (PDF 578-580 / pp.567-569; see [[case-study--twelve-week-leg-program-squats-then-barbell-jumps-then-depth-and-box-jumps]]).
