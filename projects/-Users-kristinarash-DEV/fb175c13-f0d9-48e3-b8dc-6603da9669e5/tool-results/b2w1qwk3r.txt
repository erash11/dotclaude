######## claim--strength-hypertrophy-dilutes-mitochondria-and-speeds-endurance-fatigue.md

# Strength-Training Hypertrophy Without Matching Mitochondrial Growth Lowers Mitochondrial Density and Brings Faster Fatigue Under Endurance Loads

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky (9.1 §1.4, PDF 528 / p.517, checked against the page image): "In strength training (in contrast to endurance training) reworking of the contractile fibers takes place in the skeletal muscles without a corresponding increase in synthesis of the mitochondrial proteins and with adequate increases in the vascularisation of the muscle tissues. As a result, there is expressed hypertrophy, an increase in muscle mass of the tissues and a decrease in specific density in the mitochondria (Wenger et al, 292, MacDougall et al., 241, Tesch, Lindeberg, 273). This leads to a decreased supply of oxygen in the muscle tissues, a decrease in the functional power of the mitochondrial system and as a result, to increased production of lactate and as a consequence, fast fatigue in endurance loads (Tesch, Lindeberg, 273, MacDougall et al., 239, Meerson, Pshennikova, 78, Pshennikova, 99)."

**Why it works** — The chain on the page: contractile protein grows, mitochondrial protein does not keep pace, so the same mitochondria are spread through more muscle; less oxygen is used per unit of tissue, more lactate is made, and fatigue comes sooner in endurance work. Translation note: the page prints "with adequate increases in the vascularisation," but the "decreased supply of oxygen" that follows only makes sense if vascularisation does not keep up. Inference: probably "without adequate increases."

**When to apply** — When a strength phase precedes or overlaps a conditioning target in an athlete who needs repeated efforts: plan for the hypertrophy to cost some local oxidative capacity unless conditioning work is kept in. Eric's review: the baseline treats this kind of pairing as a programming constraint: "Compatible qualities are trained together; the rest are trained at a retention dose." (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 182, Inferred (transcript + slides)). Inference: this page gives one mechanism for why heavy hypertrophy work and endurance work compete.

**When NOT to apply** — The effect is dilution from hypertrophy; it does not apply to strength gains made with little size gain. Within this book the authors elsewhere cite research that strength training raises haemoglobin and myoglobin and helps distance sports ([[claim--strength-training-raises-haemoglobin-myoglobin-and-aids-distance-sport]], 4.4). The two are not the same claim: 4.4 is about strength training's net effect on distance performance, 9.1 is about mitochondrial density after "expressed hypertrophy." Record the difference; it is not marked `contested`. The cited studies (MacDougall, Tesch and Lindeberg, Wenger) are named but their data are not shown here.

**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own contrast, same page and the next: strength training gives "expressed hypertrophy" and lower mitochondrial density, whereas "training for endurance is not combined with general hypertrophy of the skeletal muscles and an increase in the myofibril mass" but with "an expressed increase in the number and size of the mitochondria" (PDF 528-529 / p.517-518).

######## claim--strength-training-adds-connective-tissue-that-can-impair-fine-control.md

# Strength Training Also Builds the Connective Tissue Around Muscle Fibres, and Too Much of It Can Make Muscles Thick and Less Fit for Fine, Varied Movement

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky, citing Yakovlev (144) for each point (9.1 §1.4, PDF 528 / p.517, checked against the page image): "In a loaded muscle, there is an especially strong break-up of proteins in the isometric regime, and at the time of rest, their synthesis is more significantly displayed. But, besides the synthesis of the contractile muscle fibers there is intense protein synthesis of the connective tissue surrounding the muscle fibers. For muscle work, these excessively developed connective tissue layers not only are not needed, but are even harmful: the muscles become thick, unsuitable for different fine, varied and precise variations in their activity." The same paragraph gives a non-contractile change in the other direction: "Strength training leads mainly to a significant increase in the content of the elastic myostromins in the muscles, structural proteins taking part in muscle relaxation," which "serves to more fully and quickly relax the muscles after their contraction." Footnote 10 (PDF 528) glosses myostromin as "a protein found in muscle stroma, the supporting framework of muscle."

**Why it works** — The page gives the observation and its consequence, not a mechanism. Inference: the link to the isometric regime suggests long, high-tension holds are the case where the connective-tissue response is largest, since that is where Yakovlev reports the strongest protein breakdown and later synthesis.

**When to apply** — Inference: in sports where fine, fast and varied muscle control matters (throwing, striking, skill positions), weigh large volumes of heavy isometric or slow high-tension work against that need, and keep speed and skill work alongside it.

**When NOT to apply** — One citation, no data, no dose: the page does not say how much training produces "excessively developed" connective tissue, nor how the "fine, varied and precise" loss was measured. The myostromin point cuts the other way (better relaxation after strength training), so the page does not support a blanket warning against strength work. Rated `untested`. Connective tissue around the fibres also transmits force and protects against strain injury (see [[principle--condition-tendons-ligaments-and-bone-not-only-muscle]]); this claim is about excess, not about connective tissue as such.

**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own picture of the end state: muscles that "become thick, unsuitable for different fine, varied and precise variations in their activity" (PDF 528 / p.517).

######## claim--endurance-training-builds-mitochondrial-protein-without-hypertrophy.md

# Endurance Training Builds Mitochondrial Protein in Both Slow and Fast Fibres, Without Skeletal-Muscle Hypertrophy

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky (9.1 §1.4, PDF 528-529 / p.517-518, checked against the page images): "training for endurance is not combined with general hypertrophy of the skeletal muscles and an increase in the myofibril mass. In place of this, there is a small increase in the cell cytoplasm and an expressed increase in the number and size of the mitochondria" (Hollman & Hettinger; Saltin & Gollnick), with higher activity of "a number of mitochondrial as well as cytoplasmic enzymes." "In adaptation to work requiring endurance, hypertrophy in the muscles is not observed." As a result the muscles better "utilize pyruvate and the fatty acids," and "Endurance loads lead to an increase in the synthesis of mitochondrial proteins, to a significantly greater extent than the proteins of enzyme glycolisis and glycogenolysis (Wenger et al.,292)" (PDF 529 / p.518). "Synthesis of the mitochondrial proteins increases not only in the oxidative slow twitch muscle fibers, but also in the fast twitch fibers (Baldwin et al., 150, Yakovlev, 144)." The form of endurance that develops "is specific, corresponding to the character of the training loads (general endurance, endurance for long term work, speed and strength endurance and so on)" (Yakovlev, 137, 144; PDF 529 / p.518).

**Why it works** — Three factors, which he says "are not exclusive from one another and can be looked at as components of a single mechanism" (PDF 529-530 / p.518-519): "1. Accumulation of the recruited metabolites acting on the genetics of the cells as specific inductors of specific protein synthesis; 2. The most significant increase in degradation is of those proteins that do not appear as the decisive ones in the development of endurance; 3. The total depressed protein synthesis, on the background of which the effect of the inductors is realized and that calls forth synthesis of only proteins, is more noted in adaptation to long term muscle activity." Item 3 is garbled in translation. Inference: probably "synthesis of only certain proteins," meaning overall synthesis is held down and only the induced proteins are made. He adds that "Stronger degradation of the contractile proteins is especially expressed after long term execution of exercises for endurance" (PDF 526 / p.515), which is "one of the reasons why the strengthened adaptive protein synthesis in training for endurance does not bring about an increase in the myofibrils" (PDF 527 / p.516).

**When to apply** — When an athlete must gain aerobic capacity without gaining mass (weight-class athletes, distance athletes), or when explaining why long aerobic work does not build strength. Eric's review: "For speed-power athletes, the aerobic system's main value is inter-effort recovery (regenerating ATP between bouts), not distance capacity" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 123, Inferred (transcript)). Inference: because fast fibres also gain mitochondria by this account, aerobic work can raise the fatigue resistance of the fibres a power athlete relies on, which is consistent with that view.

**When NOT to apply** — The heart is the exception on the same page: in heart muscle there is "expressed hypertrophy." The printed sentence reads "hypertrophy in the cells of the mitochondria as a consequence of the increased size of the myofibrils" (PDF 529 / p.518). Inference: probably "the cells of the myocardium," since the sentence contrasts heart with skeletal muscle and says mitochondrial levels do not change. The claim is about adaptations from endurance training as such; it does not say mixed programmes cannot gain both. Literature summary with no data shown, so `supported`.

**Example** — Verkhoshansky's tissue survey (PDF 529 / p.518): in endurance training "stronger protein synthesis is also noted in other tissues, mainly, the heart muscle tissues," and in "the brain tissues, which includes synthesis of mitochondrial proteins (Viru, 33)."

######## framework--two-levels-of-the-constructive-effect-of-adaptation.md

# The Constructive Effect of Adaptation Has Three Bases and Works at Two Levels: Session Supercompensation and the Long-Term Delayed Training Effect

**What it is** — In 9.1 section 1.5, "Essentials of the constructive effects of the adaptational process," Verkhoshansky (9.1 §1.5, PDF 530 / p.519, checked against the page image): "The foundation for the development of the adaptational process in sports activity is the strengthened synthesis of structural and enzymatic proteins in the actively functioning cells." Strengthened protein synthesis "appears as a constructive effect of the adaptational process." "The basis for the constructive effect of adaptation lies in three phenomena (Yakovlev, 144, Viru, 32, Viru, Kirge, 35, Verkhoshansky, Viru, 286):
- specificity of protein synthesis in the after work period, conditioned by the character of the work executed, its intensity and means of energy provision,
- supercompensation of the ingredients, that are used up during the time of work (substrates serving as sources of energy and different structural and enzymatic proteins),
- a positive correlation between the catabolic and anabolic processes, brought about by the muscular work."

From these he names "two levels of the constructive effect." "One of them is related to the well known phase of restitution that is inherent in the phenomenon of supercompensation of products and energy used up in external work (Yakovlev, 139, 144, Viru, 289). The other level is related to the long stages of training. It is expressed in the long and steady increase in the functional indices after their long decrease as a result of using concentrated training loads, is known as the long term delayed training effect (Verkhoshansky, 23, 24, 25, 280, 281, 282, 283)" (PDF 530 / p.519).

**Why it works** — The short level rests on restitution: the phase after work "is characterized not only by increased metabolism but also a higher turnover speed of the muscle proteins which ensures the renewal of the molecular composition of the actin - myosin complex," clearing "'physiologically exhausted' structural 'footprints'" (Viru, 289, 291). "The program of biochemical changes ... determine the catabolic processes taking place in the muscles during the time of work. This program is realized during the time of rest" (PDF 530 / p.519). Inference: the long level is the same process summed over a concentrated block; the page says only that it "is related to the long stages of training" (see [[principle--long-term-delayed-training-effect-from-volume-above-the-optimum]]).

**When to apply** — Inference: use the two levels to keep two time scales apart when judging a plan. A session or microcycle is judged by restitution over hours to days; a concentrated block is judged by the delayed rise weeks after the loading, not by how the athlete performs during it.

**When NOT to apply** — Rated `contested` because the book contradicts itself on the second phenomenon. Here supercompensation covers "different structural and enzymatic proteins." In 1.16 the authors prefer "adaptive reconstruction," and printed p.88 states "No supercompensation has yet been observed to occur among any of the other biochemicals" (as quoted in [[claim--adaptive-reconstruction-vs-supercompensation]], PDF pp. 94-99). Same claim, same book. The page shows no data for either level. The link between the two levels (that the long-term effect is built from session-level restitution) is not stated on the page.

**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own definition of the long level: "the long and steady increase in the functional indices after their long decrease as a result of using concentrated training loads" (PDF 530 / p.519), the pattern drawn as strategy C in Fig 4 (see [[framework--three-adaptation-strategies-low-uniform-high-and-concentrated-fig-4]]).

######## claim--clearing-metabolites-during-rest-blunts-adaptive-protein-synthesis.md

# Restoration Procedures That Speed Metabolite Removal Remove the Signal for Protein Synthesis, So Rest Is Mainly for Synthesis, Not Readiness

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky (9.1 §1.5, PDF 531 / p.520, checked against the page image) ties the work-rest ratio to protein synthesis: "The after work activation of the nucleic acids and protein synthesis plays an important role in the formation of the structural 'footprints' in the specific response to the adaptation systems (Meerson, 76). This issue has a direct relationship to the extremely important practical problem - the ratio between work and rest." He then names "the prevailing presentation in practice and in the literature, of 'restoration' of the athlete after the training loads." "The problem is that the accelerated extraction from the body of the metabolites due to the use of different types of 'restorative' procedures, (that are widely recommended and used in sports practice) leads to a disturbance in protein synthesis and as a result, to a decrease in training effect of the loads and the specifics of its direction (Verkhoshansky, 24, 25)." "Thus, the rest period (or interval of time between the previous and following training session) is necessary not only for achieving a state of readiness (which to a significant extent, is evaluated subjectively) for the following portion of the training loads, but mainly for realization of the after work specific protein synthesis (Verkhoshansky, 25, 26, 27)." His conclusion: "the metabolite dispersal from the body during the rest intervals, is in principle, not expedient because it liquidates the results of the previous load influence" (PDF 531 / p.520).

**Why it works** — The mechanism is his inducer model: metabolites formed during work are "the basic inductors of protein synthesis in the muscles" (PDF 531 / p.520; see [[claim--metabolites-induce-and-hormones-amplify-adaptive-protein-synthesis]]). Clear them early and the specific signal is gone. He adds that the after-effects, "structural 'footprints', traces," are what link one session to the next and "are maintained in the tissues, organs and systems of the athlete for several tens of hours" (PDF 531 / p.520).

**When to apply** — When deciding whether to use "restorative" procedures straight after a session whose adaptation you want; the page names no specific procedure. Inference: flushing-type means such as massage, hydrotherapy or active recovery aimed at clearing metabolites are the obvious cases. Eric's review: "Recovery capacity must be "earned" by training hard enough first; recovery technology (Theragun, Normatec, cryo) is treated as useful but not a substitute for managing training stress and dose correctly." (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 151, Inferred (transcript)), and "It's handled through training-load design alone. We're not tracking anything objective." (line 153, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q23)). Inference: Verkhoshansky's position supports managing recovery through load design rather than routine post-session modalities.

**When NOT to apply** — Rated `contested`: the book contradicts itself on the same claim. In 6.9.3 the authors report that swimmers using restoration procedures systematically during voluminous loading gained 24.2% in absolute strength and 18.9% in strength-endurance, against 7.7% and 4.9% without them (Kuchnyev 1977; Platonov 1980; PDF 368 / p.357, as quoted in [[heuristic--routine-artificial-restoration-can-blunt-adaptation]]). In 8.6 Talyshev's sauna and hydrotherapy straight after the workout left next-day work capacity "below its initial level," which fits 9.1, but the same therapy 6-9 hours later produced next-day "significant increase (supercompensation)" (PDF 462 / p.451, as quoted in [[case-study--talyshev-sauna-and-hydrotherapy-timing-0-3-6-9-hours]]). 9.1 calls metabolite dispersal "in principle, not expedient" with no qualifier for timing or dose. Inference: the two can be partly reconciled if procedures delayed until synthesis has run are allowed, but 9.1 does not say so. The page gives no data of its own, only his books (refs 24-27). Inference: "several tens of hours" is not a protocol; it gives no window per quality.

**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own statement of the priority: the rest interval is "mainly for realization of the after work specific protein synthesis," and only secondarily for readiness, which "to a significant extent, is evaluated subjectively" (PDF 531 / p.520).

######## heuristic--start-the-next-session-as-the-previous-sessions-protein-synthesis-ends.md

# Start the Next Session When the Previous Session's Protein Synthesis Ends or Begins to Fall, Not Sooner and Not Much Later

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky, citing Viru (289, 290, 291) (9.1 §1.5, PDF 532 / p.521, checked against the page image): "it is important to give attention to the need for determining the best time (moment) for the repeat work, in relation to the time of development of the post training protein synthesis. In other words, each following training session (or training load portion) must be started only at the moment when the protein synthesis, ensured by the preceding training session, is finished, or when the intensity of this protein syntheses start to decrease. If the following training session is carried out sooner (or too late), the main part of the positive influence of the preceding training session will not by realized" (printed so; the sense is "be realized"). He presents this, with the case against restoration procedures and complex loads, as grounds to "review the obsolete principles of training load organization in microcycle coming from the conception of Periodization of Training, introduced by L.Matveev, and working out contemporary models of programming the microcycle" (PDF 532 / p.521).

**Why it works** — The rest interval exists "mainly for realization of the after work specific protein synthesis" (PDF 531 / p.520; see [[claim--clearing-metabolites-during-rest-blunts-adaptive-protein-synthesis]]). Load again while synthesis is still rising and the new breakdown cuts it short; wait until long after it has ended and the after-effects that link sessions, which last "several tens of hours" (PDF 531), have faded.

**When to apply** — When spacing sessions that load the same structures or quality, such as two heavy lower-body days or two speed-endurance sessions in a week. Inference: Fig 5 on PDF 533 (animal stress data, not training) shows protein synthesis peaking at about 24 and falling back toward baseline by 120-168 on its time axis (see [[claim--single-stress-depresses-then-raises-protein-synthesis-above-baseline-fig-5]]); if that axis is hours, it would put the next hard session for the same tissues somewhere after the first day and well before a week. The page itself gives no hours.

**When NOT to apply** — No time windows, no measures and no data are given, and protein synthesis cannot be read in the field, so the rule needs a proxy that the page does not supply. It also says nothing about sessions aimed at different qualities, which load different proteins (see [[claim--training-direction-sets-which-proteins-are-synthesised-table-1]]). Rated `untested`. Inference: in an in-season football week the game fixes most of the spacing, so the rule matters most in the off-season.

**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own failure case: a session "carried out sooner (or too late)" than the end or decline of the previous session's synthesis loses "the main part of the positive influence of the preceding training session" (PDF 532 / p.521).

######## framework--periodization-as-additive-chain-vs-programming-as-hierarchical-whole.md

# Periodization Treats Training as a Chain of Microcycles; Programming Treats It as a Hierarchical Whole Whose Key Unit Is the Great Adaptive Cycle

**What it is** — Footnote 14 (9.1 §1.5, PDF 532-533 / p.521-522, checked against the page images; the footnote runs from the foot of PDF 532 onto the foot of PDF 533 and is unsigned) explains the two conceptions the main text names when it calls for "contemporary models of programming the microcycle, based on the conception of Programming of training (Verkhoshansky, 27)" (PDF 532). "The difference between the two conceptions Periodization and Programming is based on the different 'vision' of the training process and different approach to its planning."
- Periodization: "the training process is seen as 'the chain of the microcycles', that is, an additive formation, 'the whole' which is 'the sum of its parts'. The microcycle represents its base structural element." The plan is "'constructed' from these microcycles, as from the 'bricks' of the different calibre, situated in an undulant tier on the axle of the time toward the date of the competition." On competition day "we were unable to predict the level of the athlete's performance, but we must assure its constant (persistence, incessant) increase and we must try to obtain the maximal 'angle' of this increase" (PDF 532).
- Programming: "the training process is seen as the hierarchical system of training influences on the organism, organised over time, which can only be diversified within the microcycles. So, the training process is the 'whole' which 'is greater than the sum of its parts'. Each change in the contents and in the sequence of microcycles changes 'the whole', provoking the phenomenon of 'emergence' (new properties arising in systems as a result of the interactions at an elemental level). So, the most important temporal measure of the training process is not the microcycle, but the Great Adaptive Cycle. A microcycle represents the working forms of the organization of the temporary parts of the general volume of the loads (necessary to reach the planned final result); the tier of which is determined by the general strategy of their distribution over time" (PDF 533).

**Why it works** — The argument rests on interaction: if what a microcycle does depends on what came before and after it (as the after-effects and delayed effects in 9.1 imply), then microcycles cannot be designed one at a time and stacked. Inference: this is the same non-additivity point the authors make with their "algebraic relations" for training order in Chapter 4 (see [[framework--non-commutative-non-associative-training-order]]).

**When to apply** — When building an annual or off-season plan: set the overall distribution of the load volume needed for the target result first, and let the microcycles follow from it, rather than picking standard microcycle templates per period and laying them end to end. Eric's review: Eric's stated model is "waving volume week to week (e.g., medium-high, medium, high, low over four weeks)" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 106, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q1)). Inference: that is a week-level pattern repeated over a block, closer to the microcycle-as-brick view this footnote criticises, though a four-week wave can also be read as a planned whole.

**When NOT to apply** — The footnote states a view; it gives no evidence that programming plans beat periodization plans. It does not define the "Great Adaptive Cycle" here (no length, no structure on these pages). Its author is not named, and it reads as a later editorial note explaining Verkhoshansky's position; treat it as his school's view. Its picture of periodization is the critic's version; the periodisation entries in this library carry the reply (see [[claim--periodisation-controversy-verkhoshansky-vs-zheljazkov]]). Rated `untested`.

**Example** — The footnote's own image of the old approach (PDF 532 / p.521): microcycles as "bricks" of different calibre laid "in an undulant tier on the axle of the time toward the date of the competition," with the aim of the steepest possible rise.

######## claim--single-stress-depresses-then-raises-protein-synthesis-above-baseline-fig-5.md

# After a Single Stress, Protein Synthesis First Falls, Then Rises Well Above Baseline for Much Longer Than It Was Depressed (Meerson, Fig 5)

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky opens the "consequent (second) phenomenon of the constructed effect of adaptation," supercompensation (9.1 §1.5, PDF 533 / p.522, checked against the page image). "If during the time of muscular work the processes of breakdown and oxidation dominate, that is, processes necessary for energy provision, then during the time of rest, restoration of that which was used up is required. The program of synthesis taking place during the time of rest is determined by the type of break-up reaction which took place during the time of work. In this way, any kind of break-up reaction brings about productive resynthesis reactions in the body (rule of V.Engelgard). In this way, in normal functioning biosystems the restorative process is always potentially greater and supersedes the processes of breakdown (the phenomena of supercompensation)." Then the evidence: "a onetime stress influence of the catabolic phase is followed by an anabolic phase, that is, generalized activation of nucleic acid and protein synthesis" (Meerson, 76, 77), and in "a one time emotional-painful stress type influence (trials on animals)" Figure 5 shows protein synthesis after the stress. "It has been established that the stage of post stress activation of the biosynthesis of RNA and protein, is first of all many times longer than the stage of biosynthesis depression and secondly, it is characteristic to see a substantial increase from the initial level" (PDF 533 / p.522).

Figure 5 ("Dynamics of the protein synthesis in myocardium (1), brain (2) and liver (3) after the stress (Meerson, 1986)," PDF 533, values read from the image): all three curves drop at once to a trough at the "8" mark (about 64 for myocardium, 61 for brain, 57 for liver), cross 100 before the "24" mark, and peak at 24 (about 163, 158 and 181). By 48 they are near 137, 141 and 127; by 120 near 115, 118 and 107; by 168 near 107, 110 and 100. The axes carry no labels. Inference: 100 is the pre-stress level, and 8, 24, 48, 120 and 168 are hours (168 hours is one week); the time axis has breaks after 24, 48 and 120.

**Why it works** — The page explains it by the genetic apparatus: the anabolic phase "can potentiate realization of the interrelationships between the functions and the genetic apparatus," so "the structural consequences are formed in the systems, specifically answering to the adaptation to a definite factor" (PDF 533 / p.522). The paragraph closes at the top of PDF 534 / p.523 (checked against the page image): this stage gives "a sufficiently long period of preparation during which the organs and systems can respond to the physiological loads faster," with changes "much greater than can be produced in conditions of sharp loads in the absence of an increased fund of prepared ribosomes" (Meerson, 76). The sentence introducing Fig 5 is garbled in translation ("right after the catabolic effect in all the studied organs, the laws of development in the activation of biosynthesis and an increase in content RNA which in turn, leads to the biosynthesis of protein"). Inference: it means that after the catabolic effect all the organs studied showed activated biosynthesis and higher RNA content, which then drove protein synthesis.

**When to apply** — As the shape to expect after a single hard stimulus: a short dip, then a long overshoot. Inference: it supports spacing repeat loads for the same tissues so they land on the overshoot, not the dip (see [[heuristic--start-the-next-session-as-the-previous-sessions-protein-synthesis-ends]]).

**When NOT to apply** — The data are from animals under "emotional-painful" stress, in heart, brain and liver, not skeletal muscle after training; the time course cannot be carried over to a training session without testing.
