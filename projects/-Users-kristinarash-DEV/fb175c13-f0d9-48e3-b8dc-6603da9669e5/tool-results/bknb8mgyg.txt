    14	# Long-Term Adaptation Runs on Two Linked Functions: Energy Provision for External Work and Plastic Provision for Repair (Fig 1)
    15	
    16	**What it is** — The Addendum (9.1) is Yuri Verkhoshansky's own paper, translated by Michael Yessis (byline, PDF 515 / p.504). In the introduction (9.1 Introduction, PDF 517 / p.506, checked against the page image) he writes: "The 'long term' adaptation mechanism is comprised of the energy and plastic providing functions of the body together with their mutual conditioning effect." The energy function is the "joining of the metabolic processes" that releases enough energy for the needed level of activity. "The plastic provision is a process of repair of the functional, energy forming, transport and support structures of the cells. It is realized through protein biosynthesis." He then takes a systems view from Bauer and Kasnacheev: "the state of the biological system is determined by interaction between two main energy flows: the first is used to attain the maximal effect of 'external work' (energy ensuring function), and the second flow is used to attain restitution of the energy ensuring macro- and micro structures (plastic provision function)" (PDF 517 / p.506). Figure 1 (V.P. Kasnacheev, 1980; PDF 517, read from the image) plots the relative energy share to external work (axis I) against the share to restitution (axis II). A shaded band labelled "Normal ratio" sits between two lines labelled "Limit of compensation." Curve a oscillates inside the band ("the normal correlation"); curves b and c leave it, b running out along the restitution side and c shooting up the external-work side ("the compensational limits").
    17	
    18	**Why it works** — Verkhoshansky's account: at rest, with no "adaptogenic factor," the two processes fluctuate "within normal limits of physiological fluctuations (see Figure 1 graph a)." When the factor is present, "the amplitude of oscillation in the alternation of the energetic and plastic providing functions increases. However, it is only up to a certain physiological limit after which there inevitably is a disturbance in the adaptational mechanism" (PDF 517-518 / p.506-507). He adds that "in separate stages one or another process can dominate, and even in micro intervals of time, a balance between them is maintained" (PDF 518 / p.507). Footnote 2 (PDF 517, unsigned glossary note) gives Bauer's "principle of the permanent non-equilibrium of living matter" and his two "general biological lows" (printed so; the sense is laws): systems develop by increasing their external working effect, and because they are "constantly working and destructing," they must restore themselves from the environment.
    19	
    20	**When to apply** — As a frame for reading any session: part of the response spends energy on the work, part rebuilds the structures used. Inference: this is the frame Verkhoshansky uses later in 9.1 to argue that the rest interval belongs to plastic provision (see [[claim--clearing-metabolites-during-rest-blunts-adaptive-protein-synthesis]]) and to draw the energetic and plastic phases of his three adaptation strategies (see [[framework--three-adaptation-strategies-low-uniform-high-and-concentrated-fig-4]]).
    21	
    22	**When NOT to apply** — Figure 1 is a schematic with no units, no data and no stated sample; it comes from Kasnacheev's general biology, not from sport studies. The text does not say how far a training load can push the oscillation before the "disturbance" begins, so the model cannot set a dose. The Bauer footnote is a glossary note, not Verkhoshansky's argument, and Bauer's "non-equilibrium" biology (1935) is cited as theory. Treat the frame as a way of thinking, not a measured result.
    23	
    24	**Example** — Fig 1 itself (PDF 517 / p.506): curve a stays inside the "Normal ratio" band, while b and c cross the "Limit of compensation" lines, the two ways the balance can fail.

######## framework--viru-general-adaptation-mechanism-specific-homeostatic-vs-general-fig-2.md

    14	# Small, Familiar Loads Stay Within Specific Homeostatic Reactions; Larger or Longer Loads Switch On the General (Stress) Adaptation Mechanism (Viru, Fig 2)
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Verkhoshansky, using Viru's scheme (9.1 Introduction, PDF 518-519 / p.507-508, checked against the page images): "Management of the adaptation process is done by the CNS (Figure 2)." Its first result is "the activation of specific homeostatic reactions." "When the strength (level) of the irritants is not high and the changes in the external and internal environment is not great, and in the presence of the previously worked out adaptation to a given action, the adaptational effect is limited to the specifics of the homeostatic reactions." As the strength and duration of the influence rise, the cells' own energy and plastic capabilities "require support from the side of general mobilization of the energy and plastic reserves of the body. This means mobilization of the non-specific component of adaptation or (according to Viru) inclusion of the general adaptation mechanism." That activation "appears as the basic sign of the stress state." Figure 2 ("The scheme of the mechanism of general adaptation (Viru, 1981)", PDF 518, read from the image) runs Stressor, then CNS, then two branches: "Specific homeostatic" and "General Adaptation." General adaptation splits into "Mobilization of energy reserve and energy provisioning functions," "Mobilisation of plastic resources and adaptive synthesis of enzymatic and structural proteins" and "Activation of defenses faculties (immunoactivities, etc..)," with "Ensuring the work of ionic pumps" feeding back to the specific reactions and "Ensuring the transition to the long term adaptation" linked back by a dashed line.
    17	
    18	**Why it works** — The hormonal division of labour he gives, citing Viru (32, 289) and Yakovlev (144): "In mobilization of the energy resources of the body and in the energy provision function, predominant importance is given to the sympatho-adrenal system," acting through "hormonal (adrenaline) and mediatory (noradrenaline) links." "Mobilization of the plastic reserve of the body under stress is a function of the hypophyseal-adrenocortical system," tied to "the creation of a free amino acid reserve" for adaptive protein synthesis (PDF 519 / p.508). In the adapted body "there is increased power of the mechanism of the hormonal - humoral regulation," which "increases the maximum reaction of the body to limit loads, that ensures maximum mobilization of its functional capabilities" (PDF 519 / p.508).
    19	
    20	**When to apply** — Inference: use the two branches to judge what a session asks for. A familiar, moderate session in a trained athlete stays in the specific branch and mostly maintains; a new, heavier or longer load recruits the general stress response, which is what funds new structure. This frames why Verkhoshansky ties progressive adaptation to loads that "bring about systematic disturbance of body homeostasis" (PDF 521 / p.510; see [[claim--compensatory-adaptation-is-temporary-and-precedes-stable-adaptation]]).
    21	
    22	**When NOT to apply** — The page gives no thresholds: "not high" and "not great" are not quantified, so the scheme cannot tell a coach where one branch ends. It is a cited model (Viru 1981), shown without data. The stress-state framing follows Selye (cited on PDF 516), so the same caution applies as in the 8.5 overtraining entry: "stress" here is a whole-body hormonal state, not only fatigue. Inference: modern work adds local signalling (mechanical tension, for example) that this hormone-centred scheme does not show.
    23	
    24	**Example** — Fig 2 (PDF 518 / p.507): the stressor reaches the CNS, which can answer through the specific homeostatic box alone or also through the three general-adaptation boxes, one of which leads on to "Ensuring the transition to the long term adaptation."

######## claim--acute-adaptation-runs-at-the-limit-and-long-term-adaptation-needs-protein-synthesis.md
    13	
    14	# Acute Adaptation Works at the Body's Limits; Only Repeated Bouts That Switch On Protein Synthesis Build Long-Term Adaptation
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Verkhoshansky, citing Kusnetzov, Meerson, Haydarliu and Viru (9.1 Introduction, PDF 516 / p.505, checked against the page image): "there are two stages in development of the adaptational process: the beginning stage - 'acute' (short term), incomplete adaptation, and the following stage - 'long term', completed adaptation." The acute stage starts at once and so "could be realized only on the basis of the earlier formed physiological mechanisms." "The most important trait of this stage of adaptation is that the activity of the body takes place on the limits of its physiological abilities, during almost full mobilization of the functional reserves, which are still far from the full measure needed to ensure the necessary adaptational effect." The long-term stage "appears gradually as a result of long or multiple repetitions" and "is developed on the basis of multiple realizations of 'acute' adaptation," until the body "acquires a new quality - a level of functional abilities that is greater than earlier." He calls the transition "the main moment in the adaptational process" (PDF 516 / p.505). Section 1.1 restates it for sport: "Short term adaptation transitions gradually into long term on the basis of multiple short term and systematic training effects on the body (training days, training sessions)" (9.1 §1.1, PDF 521 / p.510).
    17	
    18	**Why it works** — The bridge is protein synthesis: "an increase in the function of organs and systems naturally entail the activation of the synthesis of nucleic acid and cell proteins that form these organs and systems," producing structural changes that "appear as a quality material base on which the formation and transition from 'short' to 'long term' adaptation is realized" (PDF 516 / p.505). In 1.2 he ties this to the reserve: "The realization of CAR to the limits that it can be withstood, activates the genetic apparatus of inner cellular regulation," leading to structural changes "ensuring the transition from short term adaptation to long term (Meerson, 76-77, Viru, 32,33)" (9.1 §1.2, PDF 522 / p.511). He lists the transfer "through activation of the genetic apparatus of the cells and the induction of adaptive protein synthesis" among the findings of the adaptation literature (PDF 516 / p.505).
    19	
    20	**When to apply** — When explaining why one hard session changes little and a block changes a lot: a single bout spends reserves, and the structure is built only by repeated bouts. Inference: a coach judging a block by the first week's response is reading the acute stage, which by this account is still "far from the full measure."
    21	
    22	**When NOT to apply** — The section is a literature summary with citations and no data shown. It says nothing about how many repetitions, at what spacing, move an athlete from acute to long-term adaptation; for timing rules see [[heuristic--start-the-next-session-as-the-previous-sessions-protein-synthesis-ends]]. `supported`, not `proven`: the two-stage outline matches mainstream adaptation physiology, but this text gives the argument, not the evidence.
    23	
    24	**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own wording of the sport case: "training days, training sessions" are the "multiple short term and systematic training effects" through which short-term adaptation "transitions gradually into long term" (PDF 521 / p.510).

    14	# Training Theory as a Hierarchy: Laws of Adaptation, Then Laws of Sports Mastery, Then Principles, Then the Time Forms of Training (Fig 3)
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Verkhoshansky opens 9.1 with the "multi year Process of Achieving Sports Mastery (PASM)," which "was developed on a base of definite laws. Athletes can achieve the heights of sports mastery by different pathways and expend different amounts of time doing this. However, the path taken by each athlete will have general traits, inherent to all athletes," set by "genetically imposed abilities of the body to accommodate" (9.1 Introduction, PDF 515 / p.504, checked against the page image). "The general laws of PASM are first of all determined by the laws of development of the adaptational process related to sports activity (Figure 3)" (PDF 519 / p.508). Figure 3 ("The principal structure of the fundamentals of the Theory and Methodology of Sport Training," PDF 520 / p.509, read from the image; no source is given) stacks: "The laws of adaptation"; "General laws of PASM"; two branches, "The laws of the morphological-functional specialization" and "The laws of the technical and tactical mastery perfectioning"; between them "Principles to develop the training process"; then "The forms of the temporal organization of training," which divide into "Yearly cycle," "Large training cycle," "Micro-cycle," "Training day" and "Training seance" (printed so).
    17	
    18	**Why it works** — His argument: "The general laws of the PASM reflect the necessary structure of multiyear training in an objective manner, including the order of development of all of its components and the principles of their interactions." The principles are "joined with the objective display of the practical realization of the laws of adaptation of the body to loaded muscular activity, to determine the organizational-temporal form of constructing sports training" (PDF 520 / p.509). Other drivers of mastery, "socio-economic conditions, effectiveness of the theoretical concept of training, professional mastery of the coach, the individual psycho-physiological personality traits of the athlete and so on," are, "even with the indubitable significance of these factors, ... secondary. They can be looked at (and realized) under the process of PASM, but do not determine the principle mechanisms of its development" (PDF 519-520 / p.508-509).
    19	
    20	**When to apply** — As an order of questions when building or auditing a plan: what adaptation is wanted, what the sport's specialisation and technical demands require, which principles follow, and only then how to lay out years, blocks, weeks, days and sessions. Inference: the figure puts the calendar last, which is the reverse of building a plan by filling a template of weeks.
    21	
    22	**When NOT to apply** — Verkhoshansky states that "It has been proven that the unique correct path to the formulation of effective practical principles of sports training is through the clarification of the laws of human body adaptation" (PDF 519 / p.508), but the page cites his own and colleagues' work, not a test of that claim; it is his position, rated `untested` here. The figure has no source line and no data. Inference: for a team-sport staff, the factors he calls "secondary" (coach skill, the athlete's personality, budgets and schedules) often set the real limits, so the hierarchy describes the logic of a plan, not what most constrains it.
    23	
    24	**Example** — Fig 3 (PDF 520 / p.509): the five time forms at the bottom, from "Yearly cycle" down to the single "Training seance," all hang from "The forms of the temporal organization of training," which hangs from the principles and, above them, the laws of adaptation.

######## claim--car-volume-is-capped-and-shrinks-as-mastery-nears-the-genetic-limit.md

    14	# The Current Adaptation Reserve Has a Ceiling Set by Two Factors, and It Shrinks as an Athlete Nears the Genetic Limit
    15	
    16	**What it is** — In 9.1 section 1.2, "Current adaptation reserves of the body," Verkhoshansky describes the compensatory phase as one of "intense mobilization of the energy and plastic reserves of the body. This phase cannot be long." After it, "the body recovers its functions up to a level which significantly surpasses the initial" (9.1 §1.2, PDF 521 / p.510, checked against the page image). "This is the beginning hypothesis of the current adaptation reserve (CAR), which the body always has available for extreme situations that may arise requiring maximum functional loading (Verkhoshansky, 1985- 24, 1988 - 282, 1993 - 27)." Two new points follow. First, "The volume of CAR has a definite limit, which is conditional (as determined from specialized studies) based on two factors: the adaptation (functional) reserve of the sympathetic-adrenal and hypophysial - adrenocorticoid systems (Viru, 32), and the absolute level of adaptation (morpho-functional) restructuring, which has already been realized in the body at the present time" (PDF 521-522 / p.510-511). Second, "With an increase in sport mastery, that is, according to the degree of approaching the genetically conditioned limit of adaptation capabilities, the volume of CAR decreases (Verkhoshansky, 24, 27, 282)" (PDF 522 / p.511).
    17	
    18	**Why it works** — Verkhoshansky's mechanism: the first factor is hormonal, the same two systems that fund energy and plastic mobilisation under stress (see [[framework--viru-general-adaptation-mechanism-specific-homeostatic-vs-general-fig-2]]). The second is how much restructuring has already happened. Inference: the more an athlete has already adapted, the less distance is left to the genetic limit, so each block can buy less.
    19	
    20	**When to apply** — Inference: when an advanced athlete's gains from a block are smaller than a younger athlete's from the same block, this account says that is expected, not a programming failure. It also argues for spending the reserve with care in elite athletes, since there is less of it.
    21	
    22	**When NOT to apply** — Verkhoshansky calls CAR a "hypothesis" ("the beginning hypothesis") and the "specialized studies" that set its two limits are not shown; no measure of CAR is given, so it cannot be tested or tracked from this page. The genetic-limit reading rests on his own books (refs 24, 27, 282). Rated `untested`; the existing 6.8.1 entry on dosing within CAR is rated `supported` on the authors' fuller Chapter 6 account.
    23	
    24	**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own evidence that a reserve exists is indirect: "the presence of the adaptation reserve in the body is seen in cases of accidents (wrecks)" (Yakovlev, Prokop, Beckman, Letunov, Garkavi; PDF 522 / p.511). He gives no sport example on these pages.

    14	# Matveev's Phases of Sports Form Came From Prokop's Adaptation-Energy Phases, Without the Physiology
    15	
    16	**What it is** — In 9.1 section 1.2, Verkhoshansky notes that "even at the end of the 1950's, the concept of adaptation energy (adaptation ability) disbursement during stress, together with its possible restoration, was used to explain the dynamics of sports achievements during training. Three phases were determined: 1. Phase of adaptation (increase in the athlete's abilities); 2. Phase of the highest sports workability; 3. Phase of readaptation (Prokop, 258)" (9.1 §1.2, PDF 522 / p.511, checked against the page image). Footnote 3 on the same page (unsigned) adds: "This idea was subsequently adopted by L.Matveev, who interpreted these phases as the phases of formation, stabilization and loss of sports form without any kind physiological explanation (Matveev L.P. 'The problem of periodization of sports training'. Moscow, 'Physical Culture and Sport', 1965)."
    17	
    18	**Why it works** — The point of the footnote: the three-phase shape of classical periodisation (build form, hold it, lose it) began as a description of spending and restoring a limited adaptation energy under stress. Inference: by this account the phases were the visible trace of a reserve being used up and rebuilt, and Matveev kept the trace while dropping the reserve, which is the cause Verkhoshansky builds his own CAR model on (see [[claim--car-volume-is-capped-and-shrinks-as-mastery-nears-the-genetic-limit]]).
    19	
    20	**When to apply** — When reading or teaching the history of periodisation, and when a "loss of sports form" phase is treated as a fixed calendar step rather than as the result of a spent reserve. Inference: the footnote gives a reason to plan the "readaptation" phase by the state of the athlete, not by date.
    21	
    22	**When NOT to apply** — The footnote is a one-line attribution with no evidence and no page reference into Matveev's book, and it is not signed; it reads as Verkhoshansky's side in a long dispute with Matveev (see [[claim--periodisation-controversy-verkhoshansky-vs-zheljazkov]]). Matveev's own account of where the phases came from is not given. Rated `untested`. Inference: whether Matveev offered "any kind" of physiological explanation would need his 1965 book, which is not in this library.
    23	
    24	**Example** — The three Prokop phases on PDF 522 / p.511 set against Matveev's "formation, stabilization and loss of sports form" in footnote 3 on the same page.

######## framework--three-adaptation-strategies-low-uniform-high-and-concentrated-fig-4.md

    14	# Three Adaptation Strategies: Moderate Loads, High Loads Spread Evenly, High Loads Concentrated (Fig 4)
    15	
    16	**What it is** — In 9.1 section 1.3, "The adaptation strategy," Verkhoshansky defines strategy as "a form and means of realizing the laws of adaptation in practical sports conditions" and gives "three variants of adaptational strategy that have been studied and written up (figure 4)" (Verkhoshansky, 24; Verkhoshansky & Viru, 28, 286) (9.1 §1.3, PDF 522-524 / p.511-513, checked against the page images):
    17	- **A, low-middle loads.** "If moderate volume and intensity training loads are used (in relation to the current state of the athlete) then for specialized workability (W) it is characteristic to have moderate and relatively balanced development." "Full exhaustion of CAR does not take place and the adaptational process is extended over time. This type of strategy is for beginning and mid level athletes." Afterwards W can go three ways: "a decrease (if there is a decrease in the strength of the training influences), stabilization (if so called maintenance loads are used) or further increases (if the strength of the training influences are increased)" (PDF 523 / p.512).
    18	- **B, high loads distributed evenly**, "the traditional variant of organization of high level athlete training." It "ensures periodic short term disturbance of the constant internal body environment"; the process is "on a more intense level and its duration has a definite limit." Afterwards only two ways: "a decrease (even if the volume and intensity of the loads increases)" or "stabilization (if maintenance loads are used)" (PDF 523-524 / p.512-513).
    19	- **C, high loads concentrated in a short period.** "The beginning stage of training is distinguished by systematic long term and deep disturbance of body homeostasis due mainly to the mobilization of the energy resources," seen as a "steady decrease in specialized workability (W)." Then, "as a result of mainly mobilization of the plastic resources," comes "a sharply expressed increase," to "a significant increase above the initial level and even the level achieved in the first two variants." Afterwards: "a decrease (if once again concentrated load volumes are used)" or "continued stabilization (if maintenance loads are used)" (PDF 524 / p.513).
    20	
    21	Figure 4 (PDF 523 / p.512, values read from the image): panel A has no numeric scale. In B, W% rises from 100 to about 140 by week 10 and levels near 150 by week 20. In C, W% falls to about 80 near week 5, crosses 100 near week 9, and levels near 155 by about weeks 17-20. Each panel ends in tails a and b (A also c) after week 20. Each panel also carries hatched down-arrows and open up-arrows, with no legend (see "Why it works").
    22	
    23	**Why it works** — Verkhoshansky's mechanism is the energy-plastic alternation (see [[framework--bauer-kasnacheev-energy-and-plastic-flows-fig-1]]): in A "consecutive alternation of moderate activation of the energetic (solid line) and plastic (dotted line) reserves"; in B "the expenditure of the energetic resources (solid lines) is compensated for by the mobilization of the plastic resources (dash lines)"; in C a long energy-dominated phase is followed by a plastic-dominated phase (PDF 523-524). Figure note: the text refers to solid, dotted and dash lines, but the printed Fig 4 shows one curve per panel with hatched and open arrows and no line legend. Inference: the down-arrows stand for energetic mobilisation and the up-arrows for plastic mobilisation.
    24	
    25	**When to apply** — Use A for beginners and mid-level athletes, where CAR need not be exhausted. Use C only where the athlete is highly qualified, the block can be protected from competition, and a planned dip in W is acceptable. In every variant, plan what follows the block: maintenance loads hold the level, and in B even more load does not raise it.
    26	
    27	**When NOT to apply** — Rated `contested` because the C-over-B claim is the same claim the existing 6.8.1 entry carries, which Issurin (2016) contests for team, combat and swimming sports (see [[framework--distributed-vs-concentrated-loading-two-forms-of-compensatory-adaptation]]). Fig 4 is a schematic: there is no sample, sport or test named for W, and A has no scale. Inference: the planned dip of about 20% in C would collide with a competition schedule in a field sport. Eric's review: Eric's stated preference differs from both B and C: "waving volume week to week (e.g., medium-high, medium, high, low over four weeks) is stated as the personally preferred model over strict linear progression" (`docs/method-baseline.md`, line 106, Explicit (interview 2026-09-28, Q1)). Inference: that is a difference in approach to record, not a conflict over the same claim.
    28	
    29	**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own contrast in Fig 4 (PDF 523 / p.512): over the same 20 weeks, B rises steadily to about 150%, while C first drops to about 80% and then finishes near 155%.

######## claim--metabolites-induce-and-hormones-amplify-adaptive-protein-synthesis.md
    13	
    14	# What Breaks Down During Work Decides What Is Built After It: Metabolites Induce Adaptive Protein Synthesis, Hormones Amplify It
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Verkhoshansky, citing Yakovlev and Viru throughout (9.1 §1.4, PDF 524 / p.513, checked against the page image): "Synthesis of proteins is provided for by the genetic program coordinated in the nucleus of the cell. Its realization activates the action of the inductors." "The basic inductors appear to be the cell metabolites (products of substance exchange in the cells) and hormones, reaching the cells via the blood." "Metabolites determine the specific character of the protein synthesis; synthesized are those proteins which are primarily destroyed, and likewise enzymes catalyzing biochemical reactions, lying in the base of the corresponding functions." "Hormones as a rule strengthen adaptive protein synthesis, inducted by metabolites." His summary: "the catabolic processes that take place in the muscles during the time of work determine the specific character of the following biosynthesis of the substances destroyed during muscular activity, that is, the specificity of the anabolic processes that take place in the post work period" (PDF 524 / p.513). He repeats it later in 1.4: "Metabolites accumulated at the time of work, acts as inductors of adaptive protein synthesis. Hormones actively secreted in these conditions strengthen protein synthesis, specifically directed by metabolites, and assures the supplying of this process by 'constructive materials'" (PDF 528 / p.517).
    17	
    18	**Why it works** — Two supporting pieces on PDF 527 / p.516. Gene repression: the muscle fibre carries the genes for all its proteins, but "part of the genetics can be 'repressed'"; under "trophic influence of the nervous system or some kind of change in the conditions, the existing suppression can be lifted and then the muscle can synthesize the proteins, which before this, were not capable of being synthesized" (Yakovlev, 144). Glucocorticoids: they mobilise protein, create "a reserve of free amino acids for ensuring the plastic functions," and move amino acids from one tissue to another, so "the plastic reserves of the cells can be exhausted in some tissues because of the stronger slow of plastic materials" (printed so; Inference: probably "outflow") "and in others as a consequence of more intense use of free resources in the anabolic processes" (Viru). Footnote 9 (PDF 528, quoting Viru 1995, ch. 8) gives the proposed signal: "low molecular weight products of protein degradation" acting on transcription, with specific activation if they "are protein specific fragments."
    19	
    20	**When to apply** — Verkhoshansky draws the programming rule from it (PDF 532 / p.521): "Training can be considered effective when the loads bring about induction of adaptive protein synthesis in the desired direction. The criteria for such loads should (or can) be the accumulation of metabolites or the changes in the hormonal ensemble, necessary for specific induction of the adaptive synthesis of protein and for its strengthening (Viru, 289)." Inference: pick the session's means so the breakdown it causes is the breakdown of the structures you want rebuilt.
    21	
    22	**When NOT to apply** — The section is a review of cited work (Viru, Yakovlev, Booth, Poortemans and others) with no data on the page. Inference: modern muscle biology also gives a large role to mechanical signalling, which this metabolite-and-hormone account does not mention, so treat it as one part of the mechanism. The page names no practical measure of "the accumulation of metabolites" or "the hormonal ensemble," so the effective-load criterion cannot be applied directly in a weight room.
    23	
    24	**Example** — Verkhoshansky's own strength case (PDF 528 / p.517): hypertrophy from strength training "is based on the strengthened protein synthesis ... and is combined with an increase in the protein content, in mainly the myofibrils," and "the anabolic acting androgenic hormones strengthen this aggregate change (Viru, 33)."

    14	# Each Training Direction Builds Its Own Proteins: Speed the Sarcoplasmic Reticulum, Strength the Contractile Proteins, Endurance the Mitochondria (Table 1)
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Verkhoshansky (9.1 §1.4, PDF 525 / p.514, checked against the page image): "The specific character of the biochemical adaptation in the process of training depends to a significant degree, on the essentials of the training loads used (Yakovlev, 140)." Table 1, "Specific influence of different regimes of muscle work on the adaptive synthesis of proteins (According to A. Viru, 1995)," read from the image:
    17	
    18	| Primary direction of the training influence | The specific character of the adaptational protein synthesis |
    19	|---|---|
    20	| Development of speed | Synthesis of proteins in the sarcoplasmatic reticulum |
    21	| Development of strength | Synthesis of contractile proteins |
    22	| Development of endurance | Synthesis of mitochondrial proteins |
    23	| Development of speed endurance | Synthesis of proteins with buffering properties, synthesis of isoenzymes resistant to lower pH |
    24	
    25	The text below the table restates it by exercise type: "In strength exercises, the myofibril protein synthesis appears to be foremost. In short term exercises of maximum effort there is synthesis of sarcoplasmatic reticulum; in submaximal exercises tied in with insufficient satisfaction of the needed oxygen, accumulation of non-oxidized metabolites, and a change in pH to the acid side, there is synthesis of glycogen and protein enzymes, that have great buffering capabilities and in long duration exercises based on aerobic energy exchange, there is synthesis of mitochondrial proteins" (PDF 525 / p.514).
    26	
    27	**Why it works** — The mechanism is local metabolites acting as specific inducers (see [[claim--metabolites-induce-and-hormones-amplify-adaptive-protein-synthesis]]): "It is most certain that the specificity is determined by the local accumulation of metabolites, acting as inductors of synthesis of a limited number of proteins" (Viru, 289; PDF 526 / p.515). The result: "the muscles acquire the functional properties corresponding to (adequate for) the work regime," either faster anaerobic ATP resynthesis and contraction, more respiratory ATP resynthesis and endurance, or more contractile protein and strength, "or in the end result, to some degree of development of all of these qualities" (PDF 526 / p.515). Even new mitochondria take the load's character: "the ability of mitochondria to oxidize pyruvic acid in training for endurance is two times higher than in speed training (Yakovlev, 144)" (PDF 526 / p.515).
    28	
    29	**When to apply** — Choose the session's dominant regime by the protein you want built. Inference: for a football lineman a heavy strength day (contractile protein) and a repeat-sprint day with short rest (buffering capacity) aim at different machinery, which supports keeping each session's main emphasis clear.
    30	
    31	**When NOT to apply** — Table 1 is a four-row summary from Viru's 1995 book, with no data, loads or durations; it names a primary product, not the only one. The speed row says speed training builds sarcoplasmic reticulum, but the next page also has speed training increasing "muscle mass and thickness of the fibers," myofibril protein, "sarcoplasmatic proteins and myoglobin" (PDF 526 / p.515), so the rows overlap in practice. The "two times" pyruvate figure is a single citation without units or method. `supported`, not `proven`, under this book's rule for findings shown without data.
    32	
    33	**Example** — The book's own pair (PDF 526 / p.515): under endurance exercise, fibre thickness "practically does not change" while mitochondrial number and area rise significantly; under speed exercise, fibre thickness always rises and sarcoplasmic reticulum proteins rise "substantially," while mitochondria rise "less than under the influence of exercises for endurance."
