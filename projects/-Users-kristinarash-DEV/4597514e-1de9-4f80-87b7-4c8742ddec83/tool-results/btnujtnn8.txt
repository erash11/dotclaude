Lack of evidence for far transfer of action capacity training

Given the lack of evidence for a consistent relationship between action capacities
and NFL performance, it is not surprising to find that there is little evidence to
support the idea that improving action capacity through training (e.g., training
“agility” by practicing the 20-yard shuttle) results in improved in-game football
performance (i.e., an increase in a RB’s average yards per carry). What we are
looking at here is transfer of training. Transfer of training can be defined as the
gain (or loss) in the capability for performance of one task (termed the criterion
task) as a result of practice on some other task (termed the transfer task) (Schmidt,
1982; Gray, 2019). For example, in the example given above, playing RB in a NFL
game is the criterion task, while running the shuttle in practice is the transfer task.
There are three key characteristics of transfer: direction, magnitude, and distance.
The direction of transfer can be either positive (performance is better on the
criterion task after training with the transfer task) or negative (training with the
transfer task actually leads to a deterioration in performance on the criterion task).
Magnitude refers to the size of the training effect, which can be small or large.
Finally, distance refers to the separation between the transfer and criterion task in

terms of the physical and psychological demands. Near transfer of training
involves transfer and criterion tasks that are similar (or often nearly identical) in

terms of the perceptual-motor responses requited, while far transfer involves
transfer and criterion tasks that are relatively dissimilar. In our example, if

practicing the 20-yard shuttle improved your time on the 60-yard shuttle or
running through an agility ladder, that is evidence of near transfer (both tasks

involve avoiding stationary objects placed on the gtound), while if it improved a

would be evidence of far transfer.

Researchers have shown that while near transfert is almost always positive
and very large in magnitude, far transfer is much more elusive (Schmidt & Young,
1986). When the criterion task is highly similar to the transfer task (1e., near
transfer), a substantial improvement on the criterion task is observed. Football
decision-makers should take this into consideration when evaluating any new
training technology, equipment, program, or practice activity. The goal is to make
the player better on game day, not better at the training exercise. The reality is
that far transfer of training is much more difficult to produce, as research has
shown that it only occurs for criterion and transfer tasks that are highly similar in
terms of the perceptual-motor demands (see the concept of representativeness
discussed in chapters 2 and 5). This has been borne out in research examining
transfer of training in American football. There have been many studies
demonstrating near transfer of training of action capacity with football players.
For example, strength training (e.g., doing bench presses) improves performance
on the strength tests conducted in the gym (i.e., your 1 rep max for the bench
press). “Agility training” (e.g., practicing changing direction by running through
a course of cones) results in improved times on similar change of direction tests
(e.g., the shuttle tests). But critically, to our knowledge, there have been no
published studies that have demonstrated far transfer of training (1e.,
improvements in performance in the game) for any of these types of training.

Task decomposition and prescriptive instruction are not required to develop “the fundamentals”

As discussed above, a long-held belief in American football (and most other
sports) is that, before an athlete can be skillful in the game, it is necessary that the
basic fundamental skills (¢.g., catching, throwing, running) be mastered first.
Typically, this involves task decomposition (pulling the fundamentals out of the
game) and prescriptive coaching (the coach giving the athlete the ideal solution
through direct instruction and corrective feedback). There is a growing body of
research in other sports providing evidence that is inconsistent with these ideas.
In a study published in 2023, Deuker et al. compared prescriptive
instruction of fundamental passing, shooting, and dribbling skills in soccer to a
constraints-led approach (CLA) that focused on playing small-sided games. In this

on the inside of your foot”) and were given corrective feedback when they
deviated from this. A second group of players learned through playing small-sided
and conditioned games (SSCG). In a SSCG, the number of players and size of
the playing area are deliberately reduced to encourage particular actions (e.g,,
passing the ball). This typically also involves manipulating other task constraints

7. Prescriptive coaching can encourage an internal focus of attention

As first proposed by Gaby Wulf and colleagues in 1998, there are two broad
categories that can be used to describe the types of instructions used by coaches
to direct an athlete’s attention during the execution of a sports skill. An infernal
focus of attention instruction directs a performer's attention inwards to the
movement or position of their body (e.g., the position of their hands, amount of
flexion in their knee), while an external focus of attention instruction directs it
outwards towards the effect one’s movement has on the environment (¢.g., the
direction the ball goes when it leaves a QB’s hand) or something else in the
external environment, such as the position of the safety (S). From the first study
in 1998 until 2021, there had been a total of 73 studies with 1,824 participants
that demonstrated superior performance for external focus of attention
instructions and 40 studies with 1,274 participants that have shown better motor
learning (Chua et al., 2021), Furthermore, it has been shown that the benefits of
adopting an external focus are both “sticky” (you still see them in retention tests
24 hours after training) and transferable (they result in better performance for
tasks in which the participant was not trained),
Not surprisingly, when a coach is telling you to “bend your knees more,”
“hit the ball with the inside of your foot,” or “snap your wrist when you throw”
(.e., they are using prescriptive coaching), this is highly likely to induce an internal
focus (on the knees, foot, or wrist) in the athlete. As a specific example, Porter
and colleagues compared the effect of different types of attention instruction on
agility performance (2010). In this study, participants were required to run

2. Prescriptive coaching can lead to less adaptable and less creative athletes

Research has shown that prescriptive coaching can lead to athletes that are less
adaptable, that is, less able to find effective movement solutions in response to
changing constraints. As an example, Lee and colleagues (2014) compared two
types of instruction for coaching the forehand stroke in youth tennis players. The
traditionally coached group was given instructions that prescribed the one correct
technique. One of the instructions given to players in this group was “move the
racquet low to high by turning the shoulders to the side and contacting the ball
in front of the forward foot.” The other group was given an analogy-based
instruction: “hit the ball like the shape of a rainbow.” In the traditional group, the
coach prescribes a movement solution to the athlete by making specific reference
to your body movements and positions (i.e., a specific technique)—“turn your
shoulders” and “in front of your forward foot.” In the analogy group, the coach
is guiding the athlete to find a more authentic solution that has the key
characteristics they are looking for without telling the athlete the specifics of how
that is to be achieved (i.e., the technique in terms of the specific body movements
and positions). Instead, the coach is trying to encourage the athlete’s movement
solution to have a certain form by appealing to an analogy—a metaphor for the
pattern of movement that does not explicitly refer to its specifics. When I tell you
to move “like a rainbow,” the key feature that comes to mind for most people is
an arch in the path. Instead of trying to give you the exact solution to the problem,
we are using an analogy to convey its key features.

example, some participants used more shoulder motion, while others used more
wrist movement. Before the training, both groups showed four clusters, or, in
other words, they used four different movement solutions for executing a
forehand, After training, the group that was given traditional prescriptive
instructions, not surprisingly, became more homogenous—they now exhibited

3. Prescriptive coaching can lead to a reduced sense of autonomy & lower intrinsic

One of the most popular, well-supported theories in sports science is related to
motivation. Specifically, Se/{Determination Theory (SDT), which is a meta-theory

interpersonal relationships. Competence reflects a belief in one’s abilities and
capacity to control outcomes. Finally, avfonomy represents the desire to express

In a paper by Ian Renshaw and colleagues (2012), the authors describe

motivation. Think about the typical drill-based activity, like running around
cones, punching air, or hitting a ball off a tee. For most athletes, these drills can

4. Prescriptive coaching can increase the risk of injury

In 2004, Roger James proposed an interesting twist on the traditional
explanations for sports injuries. The “variability-overuse hypothesis” starts with
the same basic concept as most theories: Repeated application of load eventually
leads to tissue breakdown and injury. Meaning, don’t do it too much. But then it
adds a new twist: this breakdown will occur when there is not enough variability
in movement to allow for adaptation. So, the problem is not that you are not
doing it the one, correct way. In fact, trying to do that—trying to have a highly
consistent, repeatable movement—is likely to increase the chance of injury, not
decrease it. Why might this be the case? Think about making a tackle. If we
performed this movement the same way every time, we would stress the same
muscles, tissues, and bones (and the entire human movement system) in the same
way every time. Allowing for slight variations in the technique used, in terms of
direction, location, magnitude, and frequency of the muscle activity involved,
creates a broader distribution of stressors, which reduces the chance of injury.

To test this idea, Orangi and colleagues (2021a) examined whether
coaching methods that promoted variability helped to reduce risk factors for ACL
injuries in soccer players as compared to traditional, prescriptive coaching. Sixty-
six novice soccer players were split into three groups that trained for 12 weeks. A
traditional, prescriptive instruction group was trained using repetitive, corrective
practice designed to promote the “correct” techniques for shooting and passing.
So, in other words, the coach was trying to minimize the variability in skill
execution. A CLA group was encouraged to explore movement solutions using
constraint manipulations, including the use of small-sided and conditioned
games. Finally, a differential learning group practiced with random perturbations

29
===== p30 =====
to the practice conditions, including things like which side of the foot they had
to kick with, hand positions, and type of ball. To assess the injury tisk, all
participants were asked to perform a change of direction test in which they had
to run at full speed to a marker and then cut at 45 degrees. During this test, the
researchers used motion tracking to measure variables that have been shown to
be risk factors for ACL injuries in previous research (including things like trunk,
hip, and knee flexion angle and peak ground reaction force). What was found?
For both the CLA and differential learning groups, all the measures changed in
the positive direction (and by significantly more than for the traditional training
group). For example, from pre- to post-training, the knee flexion angle increased
and the magnitude of ground reaction force decreased. These results support the
idea that adding variability and exploration to practice serves to reduce the risk
factors for injury.

relatively uncommon) staff role in Sports organizations that we believe can help

Skill Acquisition or Enhancement)

Summary: The SAS will be tesponsible for assessing and supporting the design
of football practice environments where learning, skill evolution, and on-field
performance are maximized. By understanding and applying contemporary skill
acquisition theory to coaching and training practices (ie., underpinned by an
ecological dynamics framework), SAS will partner with various organizational
departments to promote a transdisciplinary player development approach
throughout the entire football organization. An emphasis will be placed on the
