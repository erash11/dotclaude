          Hew-Butler, T. Training impulse and session ratings of perceived exertion as perceived by athletes and observed by coaches during
          pre-season football camp. J Strength Cond Res XX(X): 000–000, 2025—Quantification of internal training load (TL), through
          wearable technology, represents an emerging metric for TL management in sport. The accuracy of subjective vs. objective
          assessments of TL, as perceived by athletes and observed by coaches, has yet to be evaluated during long-duration football
          practices (.2 hours). The purpose of this study was to evaluate relationships among objective heart rate (HR) and subjective ratings
          of perceived exertion (RPE) assessment of TL in collegiate American football athletes, and their coaches, during preseason camp.
          Twenty-four athletes (first-year, n 5 6; returning, n 5 18) and 2 coaches (football coach [FBC], n 5 1; strength and conditioning
          coach [SCC], n 5 1) participated. Athletes wore chest strap HR monitors during the first 6 days of preseason football camp for an
          objective TL assessment using training impulse (TRIMP: average exercise HR 3 duration of practice). Subjective TL was assessed
          by both athletes (perceived) and coaches (observed) using a session ratings of perceived exertion (sRPE; 0–10 rating scale; 10 5
          maximum effort). The average practice duration was 142.3 6 35.5 minutes, with 139 total observations across 6 practices.
          Significant (alpha level p # 0.05) relationships were noted between TRIMP and sRPE from the athletes (r 5 0.62), FBC (r 5 0.68) and
          SCC (r 5 0.49). When subdivided into first-year and returning groups, relationships were stronger between TRIMP and sRPE with
          first-year athletes (athlete r 5 0.68; p 5 0.001; FBC r 5 0.79; p 5 0.001; SCC r 5 0.64; p 5 0.001) than for returning athletes (athlete
          r 5 0.60; p 5 0.001; FB coach r 5 0.70; p 5 0.001; SCC r 5 0.40: p 5 0.001). These data suggest that subjective athlete
          perceptions and coach observations of TL are in low-moderate agreement, indicating these are not interchangeable, during long-
          duration preseason football camp.

                                                                                       Researchers have found sRPE methodology to be an in-
                                                                                   expensive, efficient, and reliable way to measure internal TL in
                                                                                   place of expensive and more invasive HR monitoring (7,15,21).
                                                                                   There are moderately strong relationships between objective and
                                                                                   subjective measurements of internal load in Canadian football
                                                                                   (7), basketball (30), and soccer (21) players with correlation
                                                                                   coefficients ranging between 0.68 and 0.85. The results from this
                                                                                   study found a slightly lower correlation coefficient in our NCAA
                                                                                   DII American football players (r 5 0.62), but still in reasonable
                                                                                   agreement with previous studies. A possible explanation for the
                                                                                   lower correlation coefficient documented in this study is that the
                                                                                   sRPE load ratings included other physiological stressors
                                                                                   (i.e., ambient environment, and psychological stress) imposed on
                                                                                   the athletes during preseason camp that might not have been
                                                                                   captured in previous studies that were observed over an entire
                                                                                   football season (7) and over multiple weeks of preseason camp
                                                                                   (22). In addition, to further explain the differences in correlations
                                                                                   between sports, there are specific factors to the sport of football
                                                                                   that could influence players perception of load. First, football
                                                                                   players train with large amounts of protective equipment and the
                                                                                   added equipment could explain the difference between this study
                                                                                   and other outdoor field sports such as soccer (9). In addition, it is
                                                                                   possible that the intense collisions in football could influence
                                                                                   player perception of the load experienced compared with other
                                                                                   sports such as basketball (7,40).
