Introduction
Groin injuries are extremely common in football, accounting for 18% of all injuries (Mosler
et al., 2018). In addition, the AL muscle is reported to be involved in two-thirds (Mosler
et al., 2018) of all cases and 17% of these are classified as grade 3 injuries (rupture or
avulsion) (Serner et al., 2018). For grade 3 injuries, both conservative and surgical manage­
ment have provided solid RTPlay outcomes. Conservative management potentially pro­
vides shorter RTPlay times (10–12 weeks) (Dimitrakopoulou et al., 2008; Ueblacker et al.,
2016) and is better suited for small tendon retraction lengths (<2 cm), but carries the risk
of uncontrolled tendon healing, weakness and residual pain (Best et al., 2020). In contrast,
surgical management results in longer RTPlay times (12–16 weeks) (Best et al., 2020), but is
better suited to high-performance athletes that have suffered larger tendon retraction
lengths (>2 cm) and/or damage to other structures such as the pyramidalis muscle, the
anterior pubic ligament, and in some cases the pectineus muscle (Lempainen et al., 2021).
   The purpose of this case report is to describe how a professional footballer successfully
progressed through an RTPerf pathway following a proximal AL tendon reattachment. This
report will use the previously cited RTPerf pathway, which has 11 clear phases (Figure 1)
with clear progressions and objective criteria to help inform a shared decision-making

Discussion
Case presentation
This previously healthy 23-year-old professional footballer was playing centre-back in
a game when he attempted to clear a ball with his left foot. He immediately experienced
an audible “pop” and pain in the adductor region of the kicking limb. The injury mechan­
ism was established as abduction, extension and external rotation of the hip prior to
kicking the ball. This particular movement places a high eccentric load on the AL
(Charnock et al., 2009), with this particular mechanism accounting for 29% of all AL
ruptures (Serner et al., 2018). After attempting the clearance, the player was unable to
continue and was immediately removed from the field for clinical evaluation.
   From the outset, the player presented with common signs and symptoms of a proximal
AL tendon rupture. Initially, clinical assessment revealed a palpable defect/dip, swelling,
and reproduction of pain in the region of the adductor origin on outer range resistance
testing (Abate et al., 2023). Within 72 hours, the player underwent Magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI), which revealed a full-thickness avulsion of the AL origin and fibrocartilage
footprint. The tendon and fibrocartilage retracted distally 3.9 cm. Accompanying this
report, the player had developed an antalgic gait and gross swelling in the adductor
region. Compared to pre-injury metrics, he was significantly impaired in hip range of
movement (flexion, abduction and internal rotation), and strength output (resisted
adduction) at 0°, 45° and 90° of hip flexion. A shared decision-making process was
undertaken by key stakeholders (doctors, orthopaedic surgeon, physiotherapists, and
coaches), with the player (Dijkstra et al., 2017) electing for a proximal AL tendon reattach­
ment with a RTPlay goal of 12–16 weeks (Best et al., 2020).



Surgical management and acute phase
Five days post-injury the player underwent surgery, where a 3–5 cm incision was made 3
cm distal to the pubic tubercle over the adductor region. The retracted AL tendon was
located and reattached to the pubic tubercle with 2 suture anchors. The subcutaneous
tissue was then closed with absorbable sutures and the skin was closed with non-
absorbable sutures (Lempainen et al., 2021). Immediately after surgery, appropriate
nutritional support, wound care, rest, and the use of cryotherapy were priorities. At 7
days postoperatively, the player was able to stop using crutches as he was able to adopt
a normal gait pattern and started a modified conditioning programme in the gym for the
upper body and the contralateral limb.

Gym phase 1 - early loading and normal movement
During weeks 3–4 the player completed Gym Phase 1, regaining confidence in normal
movements such as squats and basic lunge patterns, first in the pool and then on land. In
the gym, regaining hip range of motion was prioritized along with the introduction of
adductor exercises using ball squeezes at 0°, 45° and 80–90° of hip flexion (Thorborg,
2023). These isometric exercises are believed to be beneficial for tendon loading and pain
reduction while promoting function (Hölmich et al., 1999). Prophylactic core stability
exercises were also introduced including basic plank positions, standing squat positions
and single leg (SL) balance-type exercises. Throughout this and subsequent phases, the
player used a numeric pain rating scale (0–10) to rate their pain, with 0 being no pain and
10 being the worst perceived pain. A score of 2 or less was considered acceptable for
exercise progression (Thorborg, 2023). Complementary to this, a global confidence rating
was used by the player to guide progression and ensure ongoing communication
between the player and the IDT (0% being no confidence and 100% being complete
confidence, with 80% required for progression) (Mitchell & Gimpel, 2024).


Gym phase 2 – reconditioning pathways
Once the player had demonstrated the basic movement patterns of Gym Phase 1, they
began Gym Phase 2, which focused on reconditioning pathways (Figure 1). This phase
lasted two and a half weeks as the player progressed through the various reconditioning
pathways in preparation for the return to running profiling in Gym Phase 3. Crucially,
adductor rehabilitation was a priority because of the surgical reattachment and its role in
football-related movements such as kicking and change of direction activities. Adductor
exercises included concentric and eccentric banded adduction, abduction, hip flexion,
and an introduction of multiplane combinations (Serner et al., 2020). Similarly, short lever
Copenhagen and standing slide board exercises were performed as pain and confidence
allowed (Serner et al., 2014). These, and the player’s overall reconditioning, were sup­
ported by exposure to the other pathways of this phase (Figure 1).
   The running preparation pathway included exercises such as wall drives, marching
patterns and low-level sled pushes. Player engagement and confidence was enhanced by
the indoor ball skills pathway, which used a stepwise approach to grading ball exercises
from seated foot contacts to 1-2 m passes, in this case. The plyometric pathway developed
the players’ ability to absorb and produce force through the stretch-shortening cycle
(SSC). This started in the pool with first double leg, and then SL exercises (squat jumps, to
countermovement to drop jump techniques), and progressed on a soft surface and,
towards the end of the phase, the normal gym floor (Mitchell et al., 2023).
   As with all the exercises in this phase, linear actions were performed first, followed by
the introduction of multidirectional patterns to gradually load the adductor region. The
injury specifics pathway inevitably included adductor progressions but also the develop­
ment of lumbo-pelvic control. Emphasis was placed on gluteal activation, avoidance of
anterior pelvic tilt and control of trunk stability to optimize load distribution through the
hip and groin region (King et al., 2018). Having progressed along the reconditioning
pathways of Gym Phase 2, the player was profiled in the return to running criteria (Gym
Phase 3) at weeks 6.5–7 to determine his readiness to run.

Gym phase 3 – return to running `criteria checklist´
From the RTPerf pathway (Figure 1), Gym Phase 3 specifically profiles the player’s ability to
begin running and the introduction of sport-specific actions in Grass Phase 1. Minimum
standards surrounding the theme of return to running have been previously documented
(Mitchell & Gimpel, 2024) and were sufficiently met by this player during weeks 6.5–7. For
example, the player completed low-intensity running preparation sessions consisting of
A-skips, B-skips, butt kicks, and ankle bounds etc., in addition to an antigravity treadmill
session at 95% of the player’s body weight. The SSC was further evaluated by plyometric
profiling, which included a hop battery (profiled by distance achieved) and force plate
jumps (profiled by eccentric, concentric and performance variables such as jump height
and reactive strength index) (Mitchell et al., 2021, 2022). Player confidence, movement
proficiency and achieving 80% of pre-injury metrics were sufficient as 100% recovery of

SSC qualities is not possible at this point in the process. Injury specific criteria such as
adductor squeeze and hip range of motion were all within 10% of pre-injury metrics
(Figure 2), which was further supported by players’ pain levels not exceeding 2/10. At 7
weeks post-surgery, an IDT decision based on the available date allowed the player to
progress to Grass Phase 1.


Grass phase 1 – reloading and technical reintroduction
At 7 weeks, the player began Grass Phase 1 (reloading and technical reintroduction),
which included the reintroduction of running, low-intensity sport-specific skills and the
completion of power profiling in the gym (Figure 1). Using live data monitoring (Global
Positioning System [GPS]), speeds were limited to <65% of a player’s maximum and
included a combination of pitch length runs (100 m), box-to-box runs (70 m) and football
specific dribbling circuits such as the Hoff Drill (Mitchell et al., 2023). Similarly, accelera­
tions (ACCs) and decelerations (DECs) were introduced (<65% of his maximal intensity)
but were confined to small spaces (≤4 m) to limit any exposure to high-speed running
(HSR) or high-intensity ACCs and DECs (Harper et al., 2022). In the gym, the player
continued to develop his power qualities and was profiled through the hop battery (SL
horizontal and triple hop techniques) and force plate (SL countermovement and drop)
jumps. Specific to this injury, underlying strength (adductor/abductor ratio and outer
range adductor strength, obtained from isometric profiling) (Mosler et al., 2017; Serner
et al., 2020), and dynamic power (SL medial and lateral hop techniques) were targeted in
conjunction with kicking actions using a resistance band. The normalization of these,
together with a meticulous progression of kicking loads (2 m to 10 m distances) on the
grass, improved player confidence, motivation and skill retraining.


Grass phase 2 – high speed running and agility drills
At 8 weeks, the player progressed to Grass Phase 2, which involved completing HSR and
intensive agility drills, including exercises with reactive decision-making. Running speeds
were increased to <85% of the player’s maximum speed, and the intensity of ACCs and
DECs was further increased by expanding the drill distances (Figure 1) (Harper et al., 2022).
The kicking distance was systematically increased from 10 m to 40 m while closely mon­
itoring the clinical signs, symptoms, and players’ ratings of self-confidence. In the gym,
reactive strength was profiled using the SL drop jump reactive strength index. Additionally,
the adductor strength was specifically targeted for recovery from this injury. The minimum
standards that needed to be met before entering Grass Phase 3 were an inner range
adductor/abductor strength ratio of 1.2 (±0.2) N (Mosler et al., 2017), and an outer range
isometric adductor strength >3.0 (±0.7) N·m/kg (Serner et al., 2020) in addition to other
sports-specific grass and gym-based metrics (Table 1) (Mitchell & Gimpel, 2024).


Grass phase 3 – maximum speed and positional drills
From weeks 9–10, the player completed the maximum speed and positional drills of Grass
Phase 3. Drills were often enhanced by the support of a technical coach or additional players
to replicate exercises that were chaotic and highly variable by design. Defending drills

Comparative outcomes
At 10 weeks, the IDT decision permitted the player to return to team training (Grass
Phase 4) and was reintroduced to passing drills, phases of play and small (4 vs 4),
medium (5 vs 5 to 8 vs 8) and large practice games (9 vs 9 to 11 vs 11). The player
was also exposed to peak running speeds (97%) and peak ACCs and DECs inten­
sities (98% and 99%, respectively) in training situations, helping him to replicate
match demands. Subsequently, at 12 weeks, he completed 45 min in a U23 match
without any complications (Grass Phase 5). Further match exposure was increased
over the next 2 weeks, allowing the player to participate fully in the regular match
schedule from 14 weeks (Grass Phase 6). Complementing these grass progressions
was the continuity of gym-based strength, power and reactive strength exercises,
helping to mitigate the risk of future injury (Lauersen et al., 2014).


Conclusion/Clinical implication
This case report demonstrates a successful RTPerf for a professional footballer who
sustained a rupture of the proximal AL tendon. Following an acute injury, the player
was effectively managed through surgical reattachment and adherence to a progressive
rehabilitation pathway. During the process, the player followed the RTPerf pathway, which
required him to meet phase-based clinical, psychological, and sports-specific criteria. The
RTPerf pathway was underpinned by the IDT’s shared decision-making, which aimed to
ensure a positive outcome and successful RTPerf.
