 "evidenceSummary": "Retrospective correlational analysis of every regular-season game of the 2022 NFL season (n = 271 games, 32 teams, one canceled game excluded), using public schedule data. Each team's relative rest (its rest interval minus its opponent's, averaged over the season) was correlated with season winning percentage. Season relative rest correlated positively with winning percentage (r = 0.338, p = 0.029); playoff teams and winning teams had higher average relative rest than non-playoff and losing teams (about 0.2 vs -0.15 days, p = 0.022 for both comparisons), but winning percentage did not differ across the high, middle and low relative-rest tertiles.",
 "screeningLimitations": "One season of team-level, ecological data cannot show that rest causes wins, and no individual player, load, injury or performance data were analysed. The authors describe the work as preliminary.",
 "synthesisCaution": "Staff could read this as proof that schedule rest drives team success, when it is a single-season association between rest difference and winning percentage.",

  "abstract": "The authors examined whether season-average relative rest (the difference in days of rest between a team and its opponent in each game) relates to winning percentage across the 2022 NFL regular season. A significant positive correlation was found, and teams that won more often or made the playoffs had higher average relative rest.",
  "tldr": "In the 2022 NFL season, teams that had more rest than their opponents, on average, won a larger share of games. The link is a single-season correlation, so it describes schedule context rather than a cause.",
  "methods": "All 271 regular-season games of 2022 were analysed from public schedule data. Rest intervals were calculated per team per game, relative rest was averaged over the season, and it was correlated with winning percentage. Teams were split into thirds by relative rest and compared with ANOVA, and winning and playoff teams were compared with independent t-tests.",
  "findings": "Season relative rest correlated positively with winning percentage (r = 0.338, p = 0.029), accounting for about 11.4% of the variance. Winning teams (mean relative rest 0.183) and playoff teams (0.206) had higher relative rest than losing teams (-0.175) and non-playoff teams (-0.153), both p = 0.022, with moderate effect sizes (d about 0.71). Winning percentage did not differ between the high, middle and low relative-rest groups (p = 0.133).",
  "limitations": "Only one season was studied, so results may not hold in other years. The analysis is team-level and correlational. It did not include travel, time-zone change, sleep, training load, injuries or individual players. The three-group comparison was not significant, and the authors note the result rests on the correlation and the two group comparisons.",
  "practicalImplications": "Relative rest across a season was associated with winning. Coaches could look at rest disadvantage when planning load management in the weeks a team has less rest than its opponent, as the authors suggest. The study does not show that managing rest changes results.",
  "athleteDev": "Use relative rest as one schedule factor when planning training load and recovery in weeks when a team has less rest than its opponent. Do not treat the finding as evidence that a specific load plan improves performance, because the study did not measure training or athlete outcomes.",
  "rtp": "This study cannot inform return to sport. It has no injury, rehabilitation, or individual athlete return data, and its team-level results should not be applied to decisions about a single athlete."
