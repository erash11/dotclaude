## Summary

The speaker, a physical therapy professor and the sport science manager for U.S. Figure Skating, describes replacing pen-and-paper jump logs with a single hip-worn motion sensor. The first goal was to count training workload, with performance and technique feedback second, because elite athletes reject being told what to do. Three case studies show the sensor used to guide a return after puberty, to set a daily jump limit, and to test whether a skater could realistically land a triple axel by a deadline. The talk matters for athlete monitoring because it ties a device output to individual rep limits, and it shows that adoption depended on who drove the project.

## Key claims

- Vertical loading on a pop jump was found to match that of triple and quad jumps, so early monitoring counted every jump equally [01:32].
- Self-logged jump counts were unreliable: athletes skipped pop jumps and counted combinations as one [02:34].
- Priority one was tracking workload, not judging performance; telling an Olympic athlete what to do risks losing them [03:06].
- A four-sensor setup was first tested on one top athlete, and the speaker says no earlier system had captured the rotation of a triple lutz without saturating [04:38].
- Sensors on the full ice surface avoided the marker-system limit of jumping inside one circle [05:08].
- A single sensor on the hip, smaller than a smartwatch, gives immediate app feedback on jump type, revolutions, rotation speed, air time, height and timing of peak angular velocity; some outputs are estimates and loop jumps are hardest [06:40], [07:10], [07:41].
- Case 1 (post-puberty return): triple axel work was paused, rotation rose over months, peaked around May, dipped in July after a vaccination, and recovered by August [09:44], [10:16], [10:46].
- Time to peak rotation moved earlier toward a point just over halfway through the jump as she improved [11:16].
- The second jump in a combination shows angular velocity like a triple axel, so it carries heavy rotational landing load; coaches were told to track triple-triples with triple axels and quads [12:18], [12:48].
- After tracking those together, the athlete had no further hip pain flare-ups [13:50].
- Case 2: the athlete underestimated her daily jumps; rotation fell across a two-hour session, and the concern was residual fatigue the next day [14:20], [14:51].
- A 75-jump daily ceiling was set, with two recovery days required if she chose to exceed it; the ceiling was raised after progressive exposure with no fatigue [15:23], [15:54], [16:24].
- Case 3: after about four months of data the team judged a triple axel by the target date unrealistic because her rotation appeared capped at the triple; she returned the sensor [17:25], [18:25].
- Adoption pattern: coach-driven succeeded, athlete-driven succeeded, practitioner-driven did not [18:55], [19:26].
- The work was funded by the USOPC and developed with the sensor company in about a year [19:57], [20:58].

## Numbers and definitions

- Triple axel rotation needed for a woman: about 1900 to 1950 degrees per second; the speaker estimates the Russians at 2000 to 2100 (estimate) [09:14].
- Case 1 athlete rotated about 1750 degrees per second on return in February; reached the 1900s in March [09:14], [10:16].
- Setup time: about 5 minutes for sensors versus an hour or more for markers; 11 sensors about 10 minutes versus 30 to 45 minutes for a marker system [05:08], [06:09].
- Case 2: thought she did 60 to 70 jumps a day; actual close to 100 [14:20]. Limit 75; beyond it, two days around 40 jumps [15:23], [15:54]. Later limit 85 (caption is garbled around "high low days are 85") [16:24].
- Five of the six single skaters at the games wore the sensor [08:13].
- Residual fatigue: still fatigued the next day [14:51].

## Methods and frameworks named

- Wearable IMU (single hip sensor, plus a four-sensor and an 11-sensor test) with app-based jump detection and an algorithm refined with more data [07:41].
- Peak angular velocity and time to peak, combined, as the two key measures [18:25].
- Individual thresholds and a progressive increase in allowed volume, compared by the speaker to progressive overload [15:54].
- Comparison of one athlete's trend against athletes who can land triple axels [17:55].

## Limits and open questions

- Three case studies, no group statistics, no accuracy figures against video.
- The speaker says the old jump-height finding (doubles versus triples) probably needs retesting with today's quads [02:03].
- Internal tension: the talk begins with "all jumps are equal" and later says not all jumps are equal [01:32], [13:19].
- The "no flare-ups" result is one athlete without a control [13:50].
- The ceiling-effect judgement in Case 3 is the speaker's belief; the athlete rejected it [17:25].
- The speaker is the sport science manager for the governing body, with an institutional interest.
- Caption problems: sensor company transcribed as "40 motion sports" and "4d sports"/"4d motion" [04:08], [06:40], [20:58]; "cheetah jump" [07:10] is probably garbled; "pear skaters" is pairs [26:18].

## Q&A

- Spins: not measured; loading is jump based; a boot sensor is planned for the next phase [22:06].
- Arms overhead: has data but keeps it private; the speaker says it reduces inertia and helps young skaters but is harder after puberty [23:10], [23:40], [24:10].
- Baseball pitch counts and volleyball jump counts are being explored with a single sensor [24:45], [25:16].
- Pairs skating: hip sensor is not workable, other locations are being discussed [26:18].

## Summary

Eager argues that football analytics has used its three data families (play-by-play, hand-charted data, and player tracking) mostly one at a time, and that combining them gives better player evaluation. He shows three cases from Pro Football Focus (PFF): an expected pressure rate for pass rushers, a "bite distance" measure for linebackers on play action, and a speed-adjusted receiver grade for deep routes. In each case the hand-charted grade supplies the target and tracking data supplies a measure that is steadier from year to year or tracks athletic testing. The Q&A covers second-order effects, trade-offs and model context.

## Key claims

- Football analytics has advanced from a few public writers to many data sources, but each source is mostly used alone, and combining them would improve player evaluation [01:33].
- PFF charts every NFL and college game by hand, producing player grades and event labels. The NFL's tracking data comes from chips in shoulder pads and the ball [02:08, 02:40].
- For pass rushers, PFF's charted pressure and win-rate data served as training targets, and tracking data supplied the predictors, in two models (pressure and win rate) [06:16].
- The most important predictor of a pressure was the defender's vertical distance covered in the first second. The model was an XGBoost ensemble of decision trees [06:47].
- Charted position labels (nose tackle, edge defender) also entered the model, so it carries some information on player type [07:18].
- Each play yields a pressure probability, not only a yes or no [07:48].
- Pressure is treated as the process that leads to sacks. PFF pressure rate and expected pressure rate together predict sack rate better than either alone, and tracking data also adds power when predicting a player's own pressure rate [08:19, 08:49].
- Expected pressure rate tracks combine and pro-day athleticism measures better than charted data does, which gives a time-varying athleticism measure in place of one combine number [09:19, 09:50].
- Coverage grades are unstable year to year. Linebacker "bite distance under expected" (BDU), the vertical displacement on play action adjusted for situation, is more predictive year to year than the coverage grade [10:20, 11:22, 12:23].
- Eager's reasoning for BDU: biting on a fake leaves the middle of the field open, the most valuable area to throw [10:51, 11:22].
- A receiver model using mean, median and max speed over a play found speed to be the most important variable for a positive PFF grade on go and corner routes [13:25, 13:55].
- The resulting speed-adjusted grade is steadier year to year than the deep-pass grade and correlates with the 40-yard dash, with exceptions such as Landry (slow 40, good receiver) and Kupp (faster on field than his timed run) [13:55, 14:25, 14:56].
- Tracking data's promise may not be reached without combining it with charting and play-by-play [15:27].

## Numbers and definitions

- Sacks take about two points on average from an offense [05:45].
- Sack rate predicting itself year to year: about 0.24 (R squared), given with "I think" and "or something" [08:19].
- BDU is described as three or four times more predictive year to year (R squared) than the coverage grade. The captions read "about four times three or four times", so the exact multiple is garbled [11:53, 12:23].
- Kupp's pro-day 40 time: 4.62 (caption "cooper cup"; the speaker was unsure of the year and said 2017) [14:56].
- Pressure: charted when a rusher moves the quarterback off his spot or gets a sack. Win rate: charted as whether the rusher won the rep. The two are correlated but can differ [05:15, 05:45].
- PFF grades are discrete steps of half a point from negative two (caption wording) [26:36].
- First-second speed was measured in the vertical (y) direction and as straight-line distance [06:47].

## Methods and frameworks named

- Charted grades as labels, tracking features as predictors (pressure, win rate, receiver grade) [06:16, 13:25].
- XGBoost classification for pressure [06:47].
- Adjusting linebacker displacement for situation (run tendencies, down and distance) and averaging over time [11:22, 11:53].
- Year-to-year R squared as the test of whether a new measure is better than the existing grade [08:19, 12:23].

## Limits and open questions

- The slides are not visible. Most R squared values, sample sizes, seasons and the number of plays are not in the captions, so only the 0.24 and the "three or four times" claims can be quoted.
- The speaker works for PFF and presents PFF tools as results, so this is an institutional and commercial view.
- The 40-yard dash question (correlation of about 0.75 raised by a questioner) concerns career averages versus one-time combine results, not within-player prediction, and the exchange is partly unclear [22:31, 23:31].
- Eager agreed in Q&A that BDU says nothing alone about whether a defender's passivity is worth what it costs against the run [18:58, 19:28].
- The linebacker examples are individual cases (Warner, Kuechly, Hewitt, Cunningham, White, David as heard in the captions); no group-level validation is shown beyond the R squared claim [11:53, 18:58, 24:31].
- Names in the captions may be misspelled (Kuechly appears as "luke lee"; Okereke as "okariki").

## Q&A

- An economist-style questioner asked about second-order effects (tired linemen, scheme and play-calling changes). Eager agreed, noting football has many columns but few rows, and that the game changes quickly, with play action as an example [16:28, 16:58, 17:28].
- Is the linebacker metric the right objective, since it measures the mean rather than variance? Eager is studying the trade-off (a good run defender may accept some middle-field plays) and wants analytics to quantify it [18:28, 18:58, 19:28].
- Do the receiver rankings match popular opinion? Eager discussed Tyreek Hill and Antonio Brown (caption spelling "tyree kill") and argued the measure captures controlled speed usable for football plays [20:28, 20:59, 21:30].
- Clarifying the 40-yard R squared table: it uses careers, because players test only once [22:31, 23:01, 23:31].
- Do defensive backs or linebackers who cushion create bias? Eager suggested team-level view of the two inside linebackers, because one may bite while the other covers [24:01, 24:31, 25:02].
- Why do context variables such as yards to go matter in the grade model? PFF does not ask graders to do context math, and the model partly adjusts for it [26:03, 26:36, 27:07].

## Summary

A five-person NFL panel (two current or former club executives, a club research director, a data-vendor executive, and an ESPN moderator) discusses how data should feed roster construction. The panelists spend most of the hour on four questions: how to set up an organization so data is actually used, how much positional value should drive the draft and free agency, whether trading down is a good bet, and how to think about quarterbacks probabilistically. The recurring argument is that data helps most when it is built into the scouting process from the start, when a team uses its own models and its own history rather than a league-wide rule, and when decisions are made before need and job pressure distort grades. Several panelists stress that NFL job security shortens decision horizons.

## Summary

They discuss how a hard salary cap shapes roster building: planning a contention window around core players, structuring contracts so they stay tradeable, buying or selling at the deadline, and balancing analytics against leadership and culture.
