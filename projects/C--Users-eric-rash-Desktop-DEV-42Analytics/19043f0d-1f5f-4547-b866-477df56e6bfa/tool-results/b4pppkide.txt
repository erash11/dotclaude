# A failed attempt to prove that curveballs are more dangerous than fastballs

**What it is** — Glenn Fleisig says ASMI had advised for years not to throw curveballs until the growth plates close ("don't throw curve balls until you're old enough to shave") and ran studies to prove it (AgouMNTeyo4 [38:40], [39:11]). The result went the other way:
- Lab: in three biomechanics studies (two in Birmingham, one in Connecticut, some by other groups), elbow torque in a curveball was the same as or a little less than in a fastball (AgouMNTeyo4 [39:11], [39:41]).
- Field: in the 10-year cohort of about 500 youth pitchers, those who started throwing curveballs younger were not the ones who got hurt (AgouMNTeyo4 [40:12]).
- Conclusion: "we have not proven that the curve ball is dangerous. We have proven pitching too much is dangerous" (AgouMNTeyo4 [40:12], [40:42]).

In Q&A he is careful: failing to prove danger is not proving safety, so young pitchers should still learn a fastball and a changeup first and add a curveball "when you're ready", because the purpose of off-speed pitches is to disturb the batter's timing (AgouMNTeyo4 [52:31], [53:02]). On pitch type, his group has studied fastballs, curveballs, changeups and sliders: fastballs, curveballs and sliders are stressful, changeups less so; split-fingered fastballs were not studied (AgouMNTeyo4 [53:02]).

**Why it works** — Fleisig's measure of stress is elbow torque calculated from motion capture: kinematics (positions and angles) from cameras and kinetics (forces) calculated by equation, torque of about 100 N·m at the cocked position for a fastball (AgouMNTeyo4 [11:46], [12:17], [15:27]). Inference: if torque is the right proxy for ligament load, a pitch with equal torque should carry equal ligament risk; the speaker does not test that proxy in this talk (he says the link from arm stress to injury is unproven, AgouMNTeyo4 [58:38]).

**When to apply** — As a worked example of a prior belief tested by two designs (lab mechanics and field cohort) and reported when it failed. Also when a coaching rule rests on tradition: ask what the stress measure and the field outcome actually show.

Eric's baseline: Applied Performance's mission is to help the team "answer questions, ask better questions, and discover new ones" rather than hand down answers unilaterally (Explicit, Playbook p.6). Inference: Fleisig reversing a position his own center held is the posture that mission describes.
**When NOT to apply** — Baseball pitching in youth to professional levels; the torque comparison does not transfer to any football skill. The speaker does not show the torque numbers for the three curveball studies or the cohort statistics, so the "same or less" result is as stated. "Not proven dangerous" is not "safe", as he says himself (AgouMNTeyo4 [52:31]). The cohort is the same one that shows the 100-innings result, and the tested pitches did not include the split-finger. A rule about when to introduce a pitch is a coaching and medical decision; do not remove an age guideline on this result alone.

**Example** — The speaker's own contrast: ASMI's advice before the studies against its results (AgouMNTeyo4 [39:11], [40:42]).

# Adding the model improves game prediction over traditional metrics, but the Vegas line stays best

**What it is** — The speaker, Ben Jenkins, tests how well the model predicts games on out-of-sample data, against a Vegas line benchmark. He reports that the Vegas line alone "is obviously the best, has the highest win prediction"; adding the deep RL Shapley values improves over the traditional metrics, and adding the synergy effects improves accuracy further, getting closer to the Vegas line (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42], [12:12]). He concludes the learned values "capture real forward-looking predictive signals" (JjeFktc4QLM [12:12]). The accuracy figures are shown only on a chart and are not stated in the captions, so no effect size can be cited here (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42]). The validation sums the players' Shapley values on the court to predict games, which a judge questioned (JjeFktc4QLM [13:13], [13:44]).

**Why it works** — The speaker gives no mechanism beyond the value being learned from outcomes and including interaction. Inference: a benchmark that already prices public information (the Vegas line) is a hard test, so falling short of it is the expected result for a player-value model rather than a failure.

**When to apply** — As a model for how to validate a player-value metric: hold out data, compare against an existing predictive benchmark, and add the new metric to it rather than replacing it. Inference: a football grade or load metric could be tested the same way against a simple baseline on held-out games.

**When NOT to apply** — Confidence is supported only for the design: out-of-sample comparison against named benchmarks is reported, but no numbers, no uncertainty, no data split and no number of games appear in the captions. The data are about five seasons of NBA play-by-play (JjeFktc4QLM [16:21]). Do not read this as beating the market: the Vegas line was best (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42]). Do not assume a similar gain for a football metric.

**Example** — The speaker's own ladder: Vegas line alone, then adding the model's values, then adding synergy, each step closer to the Vegas line (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42], [12:12]).

# Faster Pitches Put More Stress on the Elbow, Youth and Professional

**What it is** — Dr. Eric McNally's group at Henry Ford uses a wearable sleeve that detects stress and torque at the medial elbow, "right where the UCL is" (z_6M5r7031A [04:09]). He reports that, "study after study," the faster the pitch, the more elbow stress, and that this is pitch speed, not arm speed (z_6M5r7031A [04:39]). He says it holds for a 12-year-old youth pitcher and for a professional, and that it has been borne out in "numerous" independent studies from his group (z_6M5r7031A [04:39] to [05:10]). He contrasts it with older advice to avoid curveballs and off-speed pitches and "stick with straight fastballs" (z_6M5r7031A [04:39]).

Related statements from the talk:
- Arm slot: studies of slot and release point exist, and "so far" slot has not shown increased elbow stress; this is the group's next stage (z_6M5r7031A [22:33]).
- Device limit: the sleeve relies on a throwing motion, so it cannot measure elbow stress in hitters (z_6M5r7031A [21:23]).
- Trend: average fastball velocity is climbing year over year, which he offers as one of two reasons the injury persists (z_6M5r7031A [04:09]).

**Why it works** — The speaker gives no mechanism. Inference: a faster release plausibly needs more force through the arm, so more load at the elbow is plausible, but the talk does not say so.

**When to apply** — As background when a throwing sport (baseball, softball) asks whether to monitor throwing stress or velocity. Eric's baseline: a test or technology that "doesn't answer a question anyone has, or a direct need" has no place (Explicit, interview 2026-09-28, Q3). Inference: ask first what decision a sleeve or velocity number would change, then check validation (the talk does not describe any validation of the sleeve).

**When NOT to apply** — The talk gives no effect sizes, sample sizes or ranges for these studies. The studies are the speaker's group's own, summarized aloud. The sleeve's validity is not discussed in the talk. It covers pitching only; he says UCL injury is essentially one position of one sport (z_6M5r7031A [21:54]). The slot finding is a preliminary "so far." Do not use it as a velocity limit for any athlete.

**Example** — The speaker's own contrast: the old coaching rule was to avoid curveballs and change-ups because of elbow stress, while his group finds high-velocity fastballs also impart greater stress, in a 12-year-old and in a professional (z_6M5r7031A [04:39] to [05:10]).

**Related** — [[claim--fatigue-breaks-the-velocity-elbow-stress-link]] (the same sleeve under fatigue). [[claim--youth-specialization-survey-and-early-surgery]] (the second cause the speaker gives for the injury).
