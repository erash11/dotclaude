## Summary

The CEO of an athlete-management and analytics company argues that injuries drive wins, and that most teams use load data wrongly. Using base salary as a stand-in for performance, he links lost salary from injuries to wins in the NBA and NFL. He then presents team-level charts where less load, not more, coincided with more injuries, and argues that common thresholds (a one standard deviation change, the 1.5 acute to chronic ratio) fail when checked against a team's own data. He says risk models need pooled, anonymized data and specific questions about pathology, timing and mechanism. Many results are shown only as charts, and the speaker's company is the source.

## Key claims

- Earlier attempts to link injuries to wins failed because counts of injuries or days lost assume every athlete matters equally [01:36], [02:06].
- Base salary is used as a stand-in for contribution because the speaker believes the best talent is paid the most in a salary cap market [02:38].
- Days lost are priced by deducting a day of salary per missed day, then correlated with wins [03:08].
- The speaker says 63 percent of NBA injury spending is on avoidable injuries (ruptures, strains and sprains) [03:39].
- In the NBA, health alone is said to decide about nine wins a year [04:10].
- Injury rates are rising across leagues while time lost shrinks, and prevention spending is not working [05:10].
- More data has meant more decisions but not better ones; people are disillusioned, as with fitness bands [05:40], [06:11].
- A soccer team's higher exposure to high-speed running and explosive efforts coincided with no injuries, and lower exposure with more; the team had been holding athletes back [06:42], [07:13], [07:43].
- Two NFL examples showed more risk with doing less, and for one team the degree of change mattered, not the total [08:13], [08:43].
- Another NFL chart showed the reverse for accelerations: above 200 in a week, injuries were frequent [08:43].
- The common baseball view that a small change in range of motion sharply raises risk is challenged; the speaker says it varies by person, and an increase in hip internal rotation was linked to elbow ligament strain more than the opposite [09:13], [09:43], [10:13].
- A published acute to chronic ratio threshold did not fit any of four rugby teams in one league [10:13], [10:43].
- Each team and each person has a different load signature, including phenotypes that break from too much load, too little, or both [14:15], [14:46].
- Risk tolerance should change with roster and opponent [13:14].
- Good injury models need specific questions about pathology, season phase, practice versus game, and injury mechanism [16:48], [17:18], [17:49].
- The speaker says no one is truly doing injury prediction with machine learning [15:46], [16:17].
- Teams should keep a few core metrics and review them yearly rather than manage a hundred variables [18:51].
- He claims the company helps teams cut days lost to injury by 42 percent [19:21].

## Summary

The speaker argues that how analysts frame a finding decides whether it is adopted, and tests that idea against four common complaints about the rise of the three-point shot: that the league has become a jump shooting league, that long shots cause long rebounds and fast breaks, that the mid-range game is lost, and that defenses can control three-point percentage. In each case he grants a grain of truth, shows what the data says, and offers a replacement story. He closes with the causes he sees behind the rise in threes and calls his account a theory to be improved. He says his work for the team is private, so the talk avoids team specifics.

## Key claims

- The meta point: statistics have to be tied to a story and a mechanism to be accepted, because fans, coaches and front-office people hold strong prior beliefs [01:30], [02:00], [02:30].
- The goal of framing is to give people permission to accept a new narrative [03:00].
- Ten of the last twelve NBA finalists ranked in the top ten in three-point rate that season [04:00].
- The league has always taken many jump shots: the share of shots from beyond ten feet was the same in 1999-2000 and last year, so the change is in distance, not in jump shooting [04:32].
- Taking more long jump shots can be described as settling or as using the geometry of the court, and the description changes how it is heard [06:03].
- Long misses do produce longer rebounds, but they do not produce more fast breaks than other misses [06:35].
- Shots rebounded from around the rim lead to fast breaks more often than rebounded above-the-break threes [07:07].
- His explanation is that a three usually follows good execution and balanced spacing, so a defender is already back; this is his own account [08:10].
- The mid-range share has fallen, but the self-created (unassisted) mid-range shots by star players have held roughly steady [08:43], [09:44].
- Among high-usage players, long two-pointers rose as a share of made shots over about fifteen years [10:44].
- Unassisted above-the-break threes by star players have risen sharply in four years, driven by a small number of players [11:16], [11:47].
- Baseball analogies hold for rate stats and efficiency, but basketball has no batting order, and a possession has a third outcome besides made or missed: not shooting [13:18], [13:48], [14:18].
- A well-contested shot often means the player did not shoot at all [15:18].
- Defensive three-point percentage looks like a coin flip at the individual level, and the speaker says it is not necessarily luck [16:18], [16:48].
- Defenders can affect a shot in known ways: distance to the shooter, avoiding fouls on a jump shooter, and late-clock or end-game contests [16:48], [17:18], [17:48].
- Defending the three therefore means avoiding it, since a three-point shot at lower than a certain accuracy is not taken [18:19].
- Four factors behind more threes: player skill development, teams permitting players to shoot from farther, downsizing, and strategies that move where the ball is caught [18:50], [19:20], [19:50].
- Power forwards' three-point rates have risen in four years and teams more often play four or five shooters [20:20].
- He calls all this a theory and invites better ones, aiming to be "less wrong" [21:22].

## Numbers and definitions

- Jump shot (his definition): a shot from outside ten feet. Share was 55% in 1999-2000 and 55% last year [04:32].
- Shots from outside fifteen feet: began at 42%, peaked at 48%, about 46% now [04:32], [05:02].
- Mid-range (his definition): outside the paint, inside the three-point arc [08:43].
- High-usage player (his definition, "arbitrary"): usage of 27% or higher [10:44].
- Share of made shots that are long twos for that group: from 7% to 11.5%, which he calls over a 50% increase, over fifteen years [10:44].
- Extra unassisted threes last year versus the 2000 rate: about 340, of which two players accounted for 308 [11:47]. He said he may have done the math wrong.
- Rim-area rebound versus above-the-break three fast-break likelihood: one and a half to two times, over 15 to 20 years of data [07:07].
- Four feet away on a catch-and-shoot: about 34%; ten feet away: about 40% [16:48], [17:18].
- Fouling a jump shooter turns a 35% shot into 75 or 80% [17:18].
- A 34% three is a 51% effective shot [18:19].
- Caption problem: "worth 50% less" at twenty feet [19:50] reads unclearly; do not rely on it.
- Ten of the last twelve finalists in the top ten for three-point rate [04:00].

## Methods and frameworks named

- Shot-location data, described as reliable from about 1999-2000 [04:32].
- Assisted versus unassisted split of shots, and density plots of assisted share by location [09:44], [10:14].
- Usage-based grouping of star players [10:44].
- A graph by another writer (caption spelling "Hest Becker") treating individual defensive three-point percentage against a coin-flip pattern [16:18].
- Baseball's batting average on balls in play as an analogy for what the defense controls [15:18].

## Limits and open questions

- The speaker said his team's work is proprietary and the talk draws on public work from his earlier site [01:00].
- The mechanism for the fast-break finding is his hypothesis, not tested in the talk [08:10].
- The coin-flip claim rests on one graph by another writer, with no detail shown; the speaker did not claim luck, only similarity [16:48].
- Star-player trends rest on a small group of players, and he said two players drive the unassisted three increase [11:47].
- The talk is built around framing, and most numbers are shown on slides the captions do not describe.

## Q&A

- A question about how teams measure the chains of actions that lead to a shot and how advanced teams are; the speaker declined to answer [21:52], [22:25], [22:55].
- A question whether star shots are still inefficient; he said shot clock and the need for someone to be open limit alternatives, and that there is probably a ceiling on how many players have the skill to space the floor [23:25], [23:56]. The question text is garbled.

## Summary

Four sports executives and a Wharton moderator talk about how cognitive bias affects front-office decisions. They agree that people are poor decision makers and discuss luck versus process, anchoring, loss aversion, regret, group think and owner influence. Practical tools they describe include writing a thesis and rereading it, reversing a trade, treating each decision as independent, and asking scouts to explain their weighting. They debate blending quantitative models with subjective scouting, how to judge scouts, and whether team chemistry causes winning. The panel is mostly anecdote from the speakers' own experience.

## Key claims

- A panelist said people are very poor at decisions and that even knowing about biases such as anchoring and loss aversion does not remove them. [02:34]
- Each decision should be treated as independent of earlier ones; a prior assumption about a college or region can mislead. [06:39]
- Good outcomes involve luck, and a right process can still produce a bad outcome. [07:41]

## Summary

The speaker, a sleep scientist who says she works with athletes and teams (and refers to a lab at Henry Ford), argues that sleep and circadian rhythm are the most ignored performance factor and touch every other one. She presents three things: why sleep matters (reaction time, judgment, injury, hormones, pain, glucose), how much athletes need (9 to 10 hours, based on extension studies), and how the body clock affects performance (travel across time zones, evening games, and individual chronotype). Her take-home points are that deficits accumulate, that regular sleep matters more than sleep before a game, and that circadian timing is an unexplored, manageable advantage. A long Q&A covers quality, individual need, naps and clock shifting.

## Key claims

- Sleep and recovery are integral to performance and the most ignored aspect; sleep and circadian rhythm affect diet, hydration, training, cognition, mental preparation and coaching [00:00], [00:32].
- The function of sleep is not known, but the effects of too little are [01:03].
- Getting six to seven hours instead of eight doubles or triples reaction time [01:33].
- Deficits accumulate over days, and three days of recovery sleep do not bring people to the level of those who sleep nine hours regularly [02:05], [02:36].
- Sleep regularly, not only before the game; athletes tend to sleep poorly just before games [03:07].
- Sleep loss impairs the prefrontal cortex, which handles judgment and decision-making, while emotional brain activity rises; fouls increase [03:37], [04:08].
- Hours of sleep was the strongest predictor of injury in a study of high school athletes followed for about 22 months [04:38], [05:08].
- Growth hormone is released in deep sleep and testosterone while sleeping; five lost hours lowered testosterone [05:40], [06:12].
- Sleep loss raises hormones that break down muscle [06:42].
- Less sleep increases pain sensitivity [06:42], [07:12].
- Sleep deprivation reduced glucose tolerance in college students [07:12], [07:42].
- Impairment from lost sleep was compared to impairment from drinking [07:42], [08:12].
- Adults need seven to eight hours but athletes need nine to ten [08:42].
- In Stanford sleep-extension studies (six weeks, ten hours in bed), basketball players sprinted faster and shot better, were less sleepy, and had better mood [09:12], [09:42], [10:13].
- Football players improved in the 40-yard dash and shuttle; swimmers improved turn times; tennis players improved serve accuracy [10:44], [11:14].
- Sleepiness depends on a homeostatic drive (time awake) and on a circadian alerting signal, with an afternoon dip [11:45], [12:18], [13:19].
- Coordination, reaction time, cardiovascular efficiency and strength tend to peak in the evening [13:49].
- In forty years of evening NFL games, West Coast teams beat the spread and won twice as often against East Coast teams; there was no difference in daytime games [14:19], [14:49].
- In baseball data, East Coast teams lost their home advantage when returning home, and crossing three time zones raised the chance of losing the first game [15:50], [16:20].
- Chronotype (night owl, morning lark) shifts the time of peak performance, and teams of mostly late chronotypes peak later than teams of early ones [16:51], [18:55], [21:00].
- Peak performance occurred six to seven hours after waking for early and intermediate chronotypes, and eleven or more hours after for late [20:30].
- Take-homes: elite sport is a 24-hour commitment, deficits are cumulative, sleep touches all performance factors, and circadian advantage is unexplored [22:03], [22:33], [23:03].
- She suggests sleep loss was once masked by amphetamines and steroids in sport [23:03].

## Numbers and definitions

- Study design: seven days at reduced time in bed (the captions give "7 five versus 3 hours", garbled), then three days of nine hours [02:05], [02:36].
- Injury: sleeping under eight hours raised injury likelihood by "1.7%" in the captions [05:08]. This may be garbled (a ratio versus a percent); do not rely on it.
- Testosterone: down 10 to 12% after five hours of lost sleep (caption "increased... decreased" is inconsistent) [06:12]. Normal aging loss: 1 to 2% per year; she says a 20-year-old would look 31 [06:12].
- Glucose tolerance: reduced by 40% after a week or ten days [07:42].
- Alcohol comparison: six hours of sleep like two to three beers; four hours like about six beers [07:42], [08:12].
- Stanford extension: ten hours in bed for six weeks; basketball free throws and three-pointers each up 9% [09:12], [09:42].
- Football 40-yard dash improvement is garbled in the captions ("1 point1 2 seconds") [10:44]; swimmers added "five kicks" [11:14].
- NFL: West Coast evening teams won and beat the spread "twice" as often, forty years of data [14:19].
- Baseball: first-game loss "60% or more" after crossing three time zones; home field advantage 3.9% [15:50], [16:20]. The 60% caption is unclear.
- Chronotype peaks: early, noon to 2; intermediate, mid-afternoon; late, 7 to 8 in the evening [18:55]. The group-level peak "between four and seven" has no am or pm in the captions [18:24].

- Twelve teams were used in the team chronotype analysis [20:30].

- Stanford sleep-extension studies using actigraphs and sleep diaries, measuring performance at the end of six weeks [09:12].
- Forty years of NFL evening-game results compared to the Las Vegas spread, with controls for team quality and travel [14:19], [14:49].

## Summary

Brandon Payne, who says he owns Accelerate Basketball, describes a ten-point training philosophy built over roughly eight or nine years and used with Stephen Curry. He says the philosophy came first and technology was added where it fit without slowing sessions. The argument is that a less explosive player can close the gap by training the brain along with the body, and that drills double as readiness assessments. Conditioning is redefined as keeping decision accuracy and speed when tired, tested by repeating a light-based decision drill after simulated quarters. The talk is a practitioner description with video clips. No data, sample sizes or outcomes are shown.

## Key claims

- Technology was added to a philosophy built in 2008 and 2009, not the other way round. [02:10]
- Curry would not top an NFL-style combine of explosive athletes, so the gap to more athletic players has to be closed another way. [05:44]
- Technology should not replace the player's own work, and overloading a player with it makes them shut it out. [08:20]
- The same philosophy is scaled from age three or four up to NBA players. Drill speed and implementation change, but the elements stay the same. [09:53]
- The tennis-ball and dribbling overload drill is an assessment, not a ball-handling drill. The coach watches for held breath, shoulders rocking (core not engaged) and knees buckling in. [10:55] [11:56]
- If any of those signs appear, the speaker concludes the brain-body link is not ready for accelerated learning that day. [12:26]
