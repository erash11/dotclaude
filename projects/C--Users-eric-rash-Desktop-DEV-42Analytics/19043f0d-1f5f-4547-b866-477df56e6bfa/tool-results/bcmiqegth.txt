## Summary

The speakers argue that the published link between the acute chronic workload ratio (ACWR) and injury may be a statistical artifact of the training schedule. A hard recent week makes a hard session today more likely, and the intensity of that session is what raises injury risk. They simulated seasons from NFL and Italian soccer load data in which only same-session intensity affected injury, and the ACWR still looked strongly predictive. When session load is accounted for, ACWR loses significance in their data. They say this does not prove the earlier papers wrong. It matters because the metric underpins software and consulting products sold as injury prevention.

## Key claims

- The first speaker says a vendor once claimed it could cut injury rates by over 50%, and he calls that claim false; he is generally skeptical of injury-prevention products. [00:30]
- ACWR is the last 7 days of load divided by the last 28 days, and it has been applied to many load types, including perceived exertion, heart rate, GPS, accelerometer data and minutes played. [04:35]
- The literature proposes a U-shaped relationship with injury and a "sweet spot" of lower risk. [05:05]
- Positives named for ACWR: easy to calculate, intuitive for coaches, and it pushes coaches to consider past training and not only today. [05:36]
- The speakers say critics had already raised other problems: exponentially weighted versions, mathematical coupling of numerator and denominator, weak out-of-sample prediction, and no data in the offseason. [08:09]
- The speakers' hunch was that ACWR is an artifact of the training schedule, because training camp has both the highest load and the highest injury risk. [09:39]
- Prior studies calculate ACWR going into each session and check whether an injury occurred in that session; they find a strong relationship across sports. [10:40]
- The proposed causal structure: schedule drives both ACWR and session intensity, and session intensity drives injury. A hard prior week predicts a hard upcoming session. [11:41]
- In the speakers' own data, ACWR is highly predictive of injury in a given session. [13:12]
- In simulations where ACWR had no effect by construction, ACWR still came out extremely significant on a chi-square test. [14:13]
- The apparent injury-risk increase in the simulations was about the same order as the literature reports. [15:15]
- Sessions in the "danger zone" of ACWR were more likely to be hard sessions. [16:16]
- A suggested fix is to use the actual session load of the session in which injury occurred, for example with survival models, latent variable models, or other players' loads in the same session. [17:49]
- Prior studies have not used same-session load because the data for an injured player is usually discarded. [17:18]
- Once session load is accounted for, ACWR goes from highly significant to non-significant in all their cases. [18:19]
- The speakers say they have not disproved the field, only shown the analysis is open to confounding; a more complex model might still find real signal. [18:49] [23:35]

## Numbers and definitions

- ACWR: acute load (most recent 7 days) divided by chronic load (previous 28 days). [04:04]
- Sweet spot: captions read "08 and 1.3", almost certainly 0.8 to 1.3, but the captions do not confirm it. [05:36]
- 2014 first paper (cricket bowlers) cited over 250 times; the 2015 editorial (captions: "blanch and gabbit") cited over 150 times. [07:38]
- Uncoupled ACWR: most recent 7 days over the 28 days before those 7 days. [08:39]
- Data: one NFL team and one Italian soccer team (captions say "Syria" and "Serria", likely Serie A; not confirmed). [12:11]
- Simulation: 1,000 simulated seasons per sport, injury probability depends only on the current session's intensity. [13:43]
- Post-simulation apparent risk increase: 6.8% for the Italian team, 10.5% for the NFL team. The talk does not say whether these are relative or absolute. [15:15]
- Injury definition (Q&A): non-contact injury causing the player to miss a subsequent session or match, in line with the UEFA definition. [20:22] [20:53]
- The 6.8% and 10.5% figures are read from auto captions; they were not checked against the slides.

## Methods and frameworks named

- Directed acyclic graph of ACWR, schedule, session intensity and injury. [11:10]
- Null-model simulation: simulate season-by-season session injuries where only session load matters, then test ACWR against injury with a chi-square test. [13:43] [14:43]
- Adjustment options: survival models, latent variable models, or predicting the upcoming session load as an unmeasured confounder. [18:19] [25:11]
- Comparison of coupled and uncoupled ACWR denominators, same result. [26:42]

## Limits and open questions

- Two teams from two sports; no detail on roster size, number of injuries, or load metric used.
- Details of the real-data adjustment models are "in the paper" and were not shown. [17:49]
- The talk does not show the adjusted effect sizes, only that significance disappears.
- Whether chronic load is protective is explicitly not addressed. [26:42]
- Whether a richer multivariate model finds signal is left open. [23:35]
- The claim that better sensors would make the problem worse is the speaker's intuition, not a test. [21:54]
- The speakers' view that ACWR products are "snake oil" is their own opinion, stated early in the talk. [01:01]
- Wellness questionnaire data: one speaker says across four sports the players mostly enter the same value (captions: "seven") every day. This is his experience, not a study. [24:39]
- Minor inconsistency: the talk opens strongly against the industry but closes saying it has not disproved the literature. [18:49]

## Q&A

- How is injury defined, and are non-training injuries dropped? Non-contact injuries causing a missed session or match; cannot rule out training relation for off-field injuries. [20:22]
- Do device sensitivity limits matter? Noise in load sensors should weaken the effect, so the speaker expects better data not to remove the problem. [21:23]
- How should decision makers avoid snake oil? Much sport science is well supported (nutrition, strength and conditioning); this may not be one of them. [22:33]
- Could wellness, physiological and biomarker data in a multivariate model do better? Possibly; dose is measurable, response is not well measured, and wellness forms lack validation. [23:35] [24:39]
- Can you predict today's load from the prior 7 days? If so, treat it as an unmeasured confounder and adjust; needs statistical skill. [25:11]
- Do high vs low chronic loads differ, and is chronic load protective? Same results using acute load alone; protection not addressed; coupled and uncoupled denominators gave the same results. [26:42]

## Summary

Adam Douglas, an applied sport scientist at Catapult who also consults for Hockey Canada, shows how wearable load data from ice hockey practices and games was used with coaches. In one case, defensemen lost explosive output across a game and got less intensity in practice than forwards; skating was added to position drills and in-game explosive efforts rose. In the second, he plans practice duration and intensity ahead of a short tournament and plots measured load against the plan. His closing message is to use whatever you collect, and to feed objective load back to coaches while leaving tactical decisions to them.

## Key claims

- Hockey Canada's national teams target short tournaments of five or six games in about ten days, so preparation has to peak in a narrow window. [01:33]
- Hockey has little applied workload research, which was why Hockey Canada became a Catapult client about three years earlier. [02:36]
- The sensor combines an accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer worn between the shoulder plates, and it does not use GPS indoors. [03:40], [04:10]
- Coaches reported the defense could not sustain intensity across all three periods. Douglas says the athletes are fit, so he looked at the data instead of assuming a fitness problem. [05:45]
- With no comparable literature, he compared games and practices internally using player load per minute and a count of explosive efforts. [06:15], [06:47]
- Defense showed a different practice intensity from forwards, and explosive output trended down across the three periods. [07:18], [08:21]
- Defensemen got less exposure to explosive intensity than forwards, and forwards looked flatter in part because there are more of them and they get more rest. [08:51], [09:22]
- Coaches planning practice think technical and tactical first. Douglas pushed them to consider the physical content of drills, for example getting defensemen skating in position work instead of standing at the blue line for shots. [09:22], [10:23]
- After about three months, in-game explosive efforts were higher; Douglas cautions that Olympic arousal may have contributed. Coaches said the defense played better late in the season. [10:23], [10:53]
- For the men's under-20 program he calls the physical side "pillars" (a term he credits to a colleague) that support technical and tactical work. [11:56]
- He pre-plans each day on a pre-competition calendar as short or longer and medium or high intensity, then plots measured load against that plan for coaches. [12:26], [12:56], [13:27]
- In the plotted tournament, the highest-loaded day was an outdoor game against the Americans that went to a shootout, and the lowest load was the fourth game, against a weaker opponent. [15:01], [15:31]
- He combines wellness questionnaires, internal load, an HRV app and heart-rate data, but shows coaches only a pared-down summary of considerations. [15:31], [16:02]
- He frames recommendations as considerations because coaches face the media and decide how hard to push. [16:33]
- The team front-loads the tournament so athletes peak; that year's load trend went up into the medal round, but the team still lost, and he says luck and the human element are not accounted for. [17:04], [17:35]
- If you collect data, use it. He says there is a gap between how coaches rate a session's intensity and how athletes experience it, and an objective measure puts everyone on the same page. [19:07], [19:37]

## Numbers and definitions

- Player load per minute: described as intensity of work per minute in games and practices; player load is called a well-researched Catapult variable. [06:47]
- Explosive efforts: a frequency count of explosive movements on the ice. [06:47]
- 44%: increase in in-game explosive efforts "in three months." Comparison period and statistics not given. [10:23]
- Tournament: five or six games in about ten days. [01:33]
- Round-robin games on the 26th, 27th, 29th and 30th, double back-to-backs. [13:58]
- Short day: about 30 minutes or less. [12:56]
- Three years of tracking data on the athletes. [18:37]
- 25 athletes (can now be understood to individual level). [18:06]
- HRV measured from August through the January tournament. [24:26]
- Hypothetical "300 units" of load for one player, which Douglas says he is making up. [21:51]
- Caption problem: the group of players in the position comparison is garbled ("seven players" for defense). [07:18]

## Methods and frameworks named

- Inertial wearable sensor data on ice, summarized as player load per minute and explosive effort counts.
- Internal comparison of practices versus games by position group, plus simple game models for wins versus losses.
- Pre-competition calendar with planned day types, compared with measured load.
- Combined monitoring: subjective wellness, internal load, HRV, heart rate.
- Concept of tactical periodization (hard and easy days), mentioned in Q&A. [22:52]

## Limits and open questions

- No sample sizes for sessions or games, no statistical tests, and no control for the 44% result; the speaker flags arousal and multifactor game outcomes. [08:21], [10:23]

- Applying to other sports: any sport that measures practices and games can use it; loading choices belong to the practitioner. [22:52]
- Why so little hockey research: he does not know; likely emerging applied research in North America and closed environments. [23:23]

## Summary

Dr. Eric McNally, an orthopedic surgeon, argues that elbow ligament injuries in baseball keep rising because pitches are faster and young players specialize and pitch year-round. He presents wearable-sleeve findings that faster pitches load the elbow more and that the velocity-stress link breaks down with fatigue. Using public data, he shows that many pitchers who have the surgery do not return to prior durability or value, and that salary paid during recovery adds up to about half a billion dollars over a decade. He closes by proposing expected value decision analysis as an adjunct for choosing surgery versus non-operative treatment.

## Key claims

- The ulnar collateral ligament is the main stabilizer of the throwing elbow, and a complete tear needs reconstruction with a graft. [01:36], [02:07]
- About 900 professional pitchers have had the surgery, and he says 25% of current major league pitchers have. [03:09], [03:39]
- Two causes for continued injuries: higher velocity, and early year-round single-sport play. [04:09]
- In wearable studies, faster pitches impart more elbow stress for both youth and professional pitchers, and this is pitch speed, not arm speed. [04:39], [05:10]
- In a simulated seven-inning game, fatigue ratings rose each inning and peak fastball velocity dipped around inning five, but elbow stress went up when it should have come down in fatigued pitchers. [05:10], [05:40]
- Single-sport specialization before skeletal maturity is causing burnout of muscle and tendon, and kids feel pressure to play through pain. [07:10], [07:41]
- Among 140 established pitchers who had the surgery, 20% never pitched in the majors again, and about one in three returned but not at prior durability. [09:13], [09:43]
- Returning pitchers showed a statistically significant decline in numerous pitching metrics. [09:43], [10:13]
- Wins above replacement falls for three years before surgery and takes three years after return to reach pre-surgery level. [10:13], [10:43]
- Over 2004 to 2014, salary paid during rehab came to about half a billion dollars (2017 dollars), excluding medical, travel, minor leaguers and pre-surgery downtime. [12:14], [12:44], [13:14], [13:44]
- Non-operative treatment for low-grade tears works some of the time but failure means losing a second season. [14:14], [15:15]
- Expected value decision analysis can compare the two paths and show, via two-way sensitivity analysis, where each wins; he says it does not replace clinical decision-making. [15:46], [16:47], [17:19]

## Numbers and definitions

- 900 pitchers, 25% of current MLB pitchers. [03:09], [03:39]
- Youth survey: 200 healthy players; 75% admitted arm pain; 25% had symptoms suggestive of overuse injury; 50% felt pressured to play despite pain. [07:10], [07:41]
- Up to 10% of drafted pitchers had already had surgery (Mayo study, speaker credits Chris Camp). [07:41], [08:11]
- Youngest patient: 12. [08:11]
- 150 MLB pitchers studied, 140 "established" (10 or more games in a season). [09:13]
- 20% of established pitchers never returned to the majors; one in three came back below the 10-game threshold. [09:43]
- Recovery to the majors: 12 to 18 months; about four to four and a half years to return to prior level. [10:43]
- Medical cost per player: about $50,000, called conservative. [11:13]
- 2004 to 2014 study: about 200 surgeries; 180 days missed on average; about half starters, half relievers. [12:14], [12:44]
- Non-operative success: 40 to 80% depending on the case; at most three months of downtime. [14:14], [14:45]
- Surgery success: "80 to 100%" in different studies; a year to a year and a half of downtime. [14:45]
- Sample model: expected WAR 1.6 non-operative versus 1.3 surgery, for one representative case. [16:47]
- Caption problem: "80 to 80 to 100%" at [14:45] is garbled. "One out of three" is ambiguous about the denominator. [09:43]

## Methods and frameworks named

- Wearable elbow-stress sleeve on throwers (medial elbow torque), including a simulated game with fatigue ratings.
- Surveys of youth players; cohort analysis of public MLB data; salary and wins above replacement (WAR) measures.
- Expected value decision analysis with two-way sensitivity analysis for treatment choice.

## Limits and open questions

- The salary study was under review at the time. [11:44]
- WAR was age-matched only in the first data set; the second was not controlled, as he agrees in Q&A. [18:19]
- The salary-per-WAR figure was "back of the envelope" and not adjusted. [28:27]
- The decision example is a single sample case with chosen inputs. [16:47]
- The speaker is a clinician reporting his group's studies; the youth survey was done during his residency. [07:10]
- The speaker does not give the sample or denominator behind the relief-versus-starter injury comparison and says he does not know. [27:22]
- The fatigue study sample size is not stated.

## Q&A

- Was WAR controlled for starters versus relievers? First set age-matched, second not. [18:19]
- Recovery by age? Numbers too small, appears similar to natural age trend. [18:50]
- How to avoid the injury? Rest, pitch counts (including warm-up pitches and multiple leagues), education, wearable research on weighted balls and fatigue. [19:20], [20:21], [20:52]
- Early specialization versus remodeling of the arm in childhood? Some early throwing is needed; the problem is rest. [19:51]
- Hitters? The sleeve relies on a throwing motion so cannot measure hitters. [21:23]
- Batting after injury? He allows it if pain-free. [21:54]
- Arm slot? Next stage; slot has not shown increased elbow stress so far. [22:33]
- Coaches' attention to pitch counts? More aware now. [23:10]
- Fatigue mechanism? His hypothesis: kinetic chain fatigue makes the arm do more; core and bodyweight work suggested. [24:11]
- Does salary matter to the analysis? Depends what is measured. [25:12]
- Why research from Japan? He does not know. [25:42]
- Pitcher elbows in MRI? Pathology is common; professionals' tears are attritional. [26:13], [26:43]
- Why is the rate similar at the major league level? Elbows are what they are by then; possibly more relievers. [27:22]

## Summary

An ESPN-hosted panel shows how a data analyst and a former cornerback work together on tracking data, then thinks aloud about how to build an individual metric for pass defenders. They stress that metrics need film knowledge and that the work is a cycle of question, data, model and plain-language story. The panel walks through press coverage, coverage responsibility (with an audience poll) and anticipation, and names what tracking data cannot capture. No finished defender metric is presented, and the panel says it is brainstorming. They close with questions on the Ravens' quarterback and other industries.

## Key claims

- Before tracking data, defensive back analysis was hard because play-by-play data covers few defenders and broadcast video often omits them [01:34].
- Using tracking data amounts to teaching a computer to watch film, which needs deep game knowledge (Burke) [05:12], [05:44].
- A former player says the software does what he did when he watched film but faster and at scale [07:48], [53:42].
- A former front office researcher says the goal is a partnership in which the analyst learns enough football to work alone [11:26], [11:56].
- Kaplan describes three steps: give feedback on metric names, find stories in current league events, and meet analysts to match data with film [12:26], [13:26].
- Kaplan says coverage metrics let a TV analyst confirm that a team used man coverage on every drop back in one half of a championship game [13:57], [14:27].
- The moderator frames the process as words into numbers into words, a data science cycle with collaboration at each step [17:00], [17:30].
- For press coverage, Foxworth stresses leverage, keeping a receiver at the line, and reputation affecting where the quarterback throws [19:04], [21:36].
- Burke says the tracking provider supplies clean data with tagged snap and release events [22:39], [23:10].
- Alignment at the snap does not prove press, since bail technique can start close to the line [23:41], [24:11].
- Tracking data lacks some body detail such as hip position, and a film expert will be richer, but the data applies one rule set to every player at scale [24:42], [25:13].
- Candidate press measures: how far a receiver is moved off the expected route and how long to get three yards downfield [25:45]; distance plus velocity, since a defender can be close and losing [26:15].
- Re-routing a receiver at the line buys safeties time in zone coverage [27:47].
- Outside observers do not know the call, so some mistakes cannot be assigned [28:17], [28:49].
- For responsibility, steps are coverage identification, defender roles, route identification, then comparing where the defender should be to where he was [35:06], [35:38], [36:08].
- Foxworth says a defender is graded at fault in the shown clip, even if the play might have been completed regardless [38:10].
- For anticipation, one idea is to group plays with similar concepts and coverages and compare a defender's path to the average [41:47], [42:19]. The baseline is hard to establish [43:21].
- Anticipation may also be the precursor to reaction time, such as when a safety breaks on the ball [44:54].
- Pass denial is hard to see in statistics; a defender who prevents a throw leaves no box-score trace [46:55], [47:25].
- Ravens opponents are seen using single-high man or cover three coverages more than other teams, and the quarterback's scrambles averaged high yards against man [48:57], [49:30], [50:30].
- Football is called harder to analyze than baseball because player interactions are tangled, and tracking data helps isolate one player [52:05].
