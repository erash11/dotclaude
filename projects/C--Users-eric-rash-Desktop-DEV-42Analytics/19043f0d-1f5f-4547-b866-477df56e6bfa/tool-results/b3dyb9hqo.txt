## Summary

Glenn Fleisig of the American Sports Medicine Institute explains the rise of ulnar collateral ligament (Tommy John) surgery in baseball and what his group's data say about it. He covers the stress on the elbow, how often pitchers return after surgery, risk factors, and prevention. His headline finding: too much pitching, and especially pitching while fatigued, is the strongest risk factor he has found [33:31]. Curveballs were not shown to be more dangerous than fastballs, though he says they were not proven safe [52:31]. He closes with MLB and youth prevention efforts (PitchSmart) and wearable

## Key claims

- Professional UCL surgeries were steady for about 20 years, then climbed, so the rise is not a one-year fluke [07:42].
- About 16 percent of professional pitchers have had the surgery, with a higher share among major leaguers that he attributes to more years pitching [08:13], [09:14].
- The ligament has to resist most of the elbow stress in the cocked position, and cadaver work showed the ligament alone cannot take that load, so muscle and other tissue share it [16:28], [17:00].
- The surgery does not improve velocity or performance; he believes the two studies that found performance down toward age-expected levels [21:39], [22:10].
- Return to the majors takes about a year and a half in published studies, about 12 months in his group's data [22:40], [24:15].
- In a spring training comparison, pitchers who had returned from surgery matched never-injured pitchers on flexibility and mechanics [28:21], [28:52].
- Youth pitchers with over 100 competitive innings in a year had over three times the risk of serious elbow or shoulder injury [31:58].
- Retrospective comparisons linked surgery to pitching often when fatigued, pitching many months a year, and high pitch counts in a game [32:30], [33:00].
- Activity in children is polarized: a few do too much, many too little; moderate activity is the goal [36:38], [38:10].
- Lab and field studies did not show curveballs to be more dangerous than fastballs; he advises young pitchers to learn fastball and changeup first [39:41], [40:12], [52:31].
- Fatigue is individual; it cannot be measured in the moment, so coaches and trainers who know the athlete, plus next-day symptoms, are the guide [50:29], [51:30].
- Poor mechanics are shown to increase arm stress, but it is not proven that higher stress causes more injury [58:38].
- Drug-driven muscle gains can outpace ligaments and tendons (his statement, no data shown) [48:54].
- The ultimate goal is tracking pitchers' biomechanics in games, which is not yet available [42:13].
