## Summary

Natalie, a faculty member in data science and a sports scientist at UVA, argues that athlete-health research needs shared, open collectives. She and a colleague reviewed GPS wearable studies in team field sports and found no conclusive evidence for injury prediction, citing inconsistent injury tracking, poor statistical framing, and overclaiming from small samples. She still uses wearables daily and describes a men's basketball monitoring day: wellness survey, countermovement jumps on force plates, barbell tracking, and GPS in practice. She proposes a collective with ACC peers sharing best practices, code, and longitudinal data. The collective is currently an idea with funding requested.

## Key claims

- Wearables spread across US teams around 2015 with promises of better performance, workload tracking, and injury prediction [02:01].
- Machine learning became easy to use, and papers confirming injury prediction with wearables followed [02:31].
- A review of GPS in team field sports found no conclusive evidence for using wearables to predict injury [03:03].
- Reasons: different injury-tracking methods across papers, treating injury as a pure prediction problem, and ambitious claims from small samples [03:33, 04:03].
- Overclaiming comes from both technology companies and researchers, partly because of publishing incentives [04:33].
- Coaches' stories that technology helps are useful for generating hypotheses [05:05].
- The program aims to optimize readiness and performance, expecting fewer fatigue and overuse injuries as a side effect [06:06].
- Countermovement jumps on force plates assess neuromuscular readiness, and strategy changes reveal fatigue that jump height hides [08:39].
- Daily data feeds coach-facing dashboards that inform lifting adjustments [09:09].
- The speaker holds that there is more signal in readiness than in injury [12:09].
- Sports research mostly sits low on the evidence hierarchy because randomized trials are hard, samples are small, and methods and data are rarely shared [13:11, 13:41].
- A collective could bring best practices, FAIR principles, multi-site research, and longitudinal tracking, including across the transfer portal and after careers [16:16, 17:48].
- Needs: funding, data architecture and agreements, oversight and consent, governance, metadata [18:19, 18:49].
- Athletic departments usually worry about giving away competitive advantage; the speaker argues the advantage is gone by the time academics publish, especially for health and safety data [19:56].
- The wellness data at her program has never made it into models, but works as a conversation starter [21:59].
- The speaker says she cares more about products people use daily than about papers [26:07].

## Summary

A panel of four basketball analytics practitioners, moderated by Ben Alamar, traces the field from box-score work in the early 2000s to optical tracking, machine-learned play recognition and pose data. The panelists argue the biggest early shift was thinking per possession, that tracking and video let analysts speak a coach's language, and that data is often misused (small-sample lineups, correlations mistaken for causes). On player development they say numbers work best to design a player's environment, not as numbers handed to the player, and that practice data is still thin. They also argue that interviews and background checks are data that need their own reliability check. The session is history and opinion, with no datasets shown.

## Key claims

- The four factors came out of needing to estimate possessions per game, and the estimation formula had four inputs. [04:38]
- The biggest early change was getting people to think per possession instead of per game. [06:10]
- Adding up box score stats so a rebound, an assist and a point count the same was quickly seen as a poor way to value players. [10:16]
- Lineup data is misused most often: small samples and missing context such as matchups, actions and coverages. [12:25]
- Defensive rebounding was once called the most important stat because of its link to winning, but a panelist said it results from forcing misses. [12:55]
- A Celtics anecdote: the team was first in defensive field goal percentage by design while packing the paint, yet tenth in effective field goal percentage allowed, and a coach answered that the point is to score baskets. [13:25]
- Tracking data changed what analysts could tell coaches, since a box score cannot say what happened and the coaches' instructions in practice are mostly not in it. [16:55] [17:56]
- The first tracking data in a real NBA game was messy (players teleporting, misidentified), yet showed how far players ran and let the team change training right away. [18:58] [19:28]
- Second Spectrum taught a computer to recognize movements coaches care about, with a team member doing early quality checks on labels such as blitz and show. [20:32] [21:02]
- Tracking data is not perfect, and coaches challenge how plays are coded. Attaching video so the analyst can select and verify the plays was called a major breakthrough. [22:03]
- Players are receptive to data because they grew up in the age of analytics, though old habits are hard to change in the short term. [23:33] [24:34]
- In player development, one panelist says direct use of data with players is limited: the technology is not there yet and presenting data is not always the best coaching tool. [29:43] [30:13]
- The same panelist says data is better used to design the player's environment (for example plays and reads that send a player left), which is described as a bit of behavioral science. [31:15] [31:46]
- Practice data is thin, practices are few and drills are not structured enough for a computer to track. [32:16] [32:46] [37:25]
- Tracking now lets staff check many instances of a skill such as screen setting across a game instead of cutting tape for two or three. Players who sit on the bench get no game data. [36:24] [36:54]

## Summary

The presenters propose a team-selection model for soccer that tries to cut injuries without giving up points. Each player gets a skill rating and a next-match injury probability. A Monte Carlo tree search then simulates the coming weeks to choose the starting eleven, treating the coach's job as a sequential decision problem. Tested on open Premier League data against a greedy pick-the-best-team-each-week baseline, the model reduced simulated injuries while keeping expected points similar, and the speakers argue real managers lean toward short-term choices. - Tracking data has been available for roughly ten years and pose data is new, so teams are "behind again." Sports science also offers more information than teams know what to do with. [02:00]
- Pose data will be something teams chase for the next 5 to 10 years. [02:31]
- Players can be overloaded with information. Instincts should be built in practice, and some players tell staff they have had enough. [03:02]
- The decisions (who to play, how to improve) are the same as 20 or 40 years ago. Numbers refine them, and staff should filter before information reaches users. [03:34]
- A player on the panel accepted "the answers to the test" and said clear communication of the value to the player drives receptiveness. [04:04], [04:34]
- Staff take information to coaches, coaches pick what reaches players, and the format varies by player (a booklet for one, three clips for another). [05:04], [05:35]
- Players in their 30s grew up in the analytics generation and may be more comfortable with it than some coaches. [06:06]
- A data advantage is largely going away because the league has standardized it. Two other layers remain: how well you analyze it, and throughput into decisions and onto the floor. Getting data into the organization's mindset is called the final frontier. [08:09], [08:40], [09:11]
- The same data can support opposing arguments about one player. [09:11], [09:42]
- Analytics to coaching and medical work is more indirect than acquisition: numbers suggest a treatment, reach the trainer, then reach the player through a relationship. [09:42], [10:12]
- For prediction, the best model might move an All-Star chance from 60% to 68%, which is not dramatic for one player but adds up across many. A panelist thinks draft models have not improved much in 15 years. Player development also matters to whether a player lands in the 68% or the 32%. [11:13], [11:44]
- A scouting-side panelist sees improvement over about 10 years, mostly in how models are used alongside the eye test, and finds accuracy hard to judge. [12:16], [12:46]
- Injury prevention is where there is the most room to grow. The game is faster, players play over the summer, and interpreting a number needs a partnership with the player about how they feel. [13:47], [14:17]
- Teams have become more conservative because they want players healthy for long periods. Players arrive with more miles and the implications are not known. [15:18], [15:49]
- A panelist said there is evidence that players learn more between seasons than during them. Sleep has improved (no 9 a.m. practice after a late arrival, a nap culture). [16:20]
- The remaining barrier is communication. Data staff should add communication skills and non-quantitative staff should learn the language, so both meet in the middle. [17:51], [18:22], [18:52]
- Basketball value is contextual (a named Memphis pair fit the team's style). Teams should find similar past situations and merge the evaluation system with scouts and coaches. [19:53], [20:24]
- Comparing international leagues to the NBA has not been solved. [20:54], [21:25]
- A player on the panel did not want to know his weaknesses. A 2009 article about a "no stats All-Star" was followed by about two months of that player shooting less. [22:25], [22:56]
- Staff should decide what to tell a player, when, how and why. One panelist showed a star his free throw footage from four years earlier instead of numbers, and it helped. [23:58], [24:29], [25:29]
- Irrational confidence is important at this level. [25:29], [26:01]
- Next frontiers named: sports science and player performance, new pose data, reading body language and facial expressions. [26:31], [27:01], [27:31]
- Handling pressure in a game is the skill that separates Hall of Famers, and mental potential is called the Holy Grail. [28:01], [28:33]
- Subjective inputs (intel scores, coach ratings) can go into models if they have a consistent history, but the history is harder to collect, and psychological assessment is not standardized across NBA teams. [29:36], [30:07]
- A panelist called the NBA the most efficient draft of the major leagues. Mixed lineups allow a top-down view of whether a team wins when a player is on the floor, though it can take a lot of time to show. [31:08], [32:10]
- The new collective bargaining agreement has not slowed trades. Teams use third or fourth teams to complete deals, and cap staff teach the rest of the front office. [32:40], [33:11], [35:47]
- Audience Q&A: the next-generation executive needs breadth, humility and communication, and luck matters. A coach-story line was "if you do not have good players you will not win." [34:13], [34:44]

## Methods and frameworks named

- Three layers of edge: data, analysis, throughput. [08:09], [08:40]
- Staff-to-coach-to-player communication chain, with information tailored to the player. [05:04]
- Merging an evaluation system with scouts and coaches, and looking for similar past fits. [20:24]
- Distilling subjective scouting or coaching ratings into consistent scores that can be tested for predictiveness. [29:36]
- Using eye test and analytics separately to find and explain disagreements. [45:04], [45:34]

## Limits and open questions

- Panel conversation with no data shown, no sample sizes, and claims are mostly anecdote.
- Captions carry no speaker labels, so most claims cannot be assigned to a named panelist.
- Unnamed sources: "there is evidence" on learning between seasons, and "all the research" on sleep [16:20].
- Organizational views (team culture, front-office staffing) are the panelists' own institutional positions.
- A panelist said data advantage is fading [08:09] while another said teams are "behind again" on new data [02:00]. Both can hold, but the panel does not reconcile them.
- The LeBron and Durant story rests on two possessions, and the speaker said he did not raise sample size and variance [42:59], [43:30].

## Q&A

- Next-generation executives: luck, humility, breadth, communication. [34:13]
- Are recent coach-players more receptive to analytics? Yes, more open, with a minimal working level that is higher than before. [37:19]
- Negative effects of analytics: fans find the game too complicated, and a misleading win probability graph. [38:20], [38:51]
- Analytics in officiating: positive if it creates a meritocracy, with a guard against people changing behavior to fit the measure. [39:53], [40:25]
- Value of generational leaders: spacing, turnover saving and shot selection that filters down, plus tone. [41:56]
- Analytics versus the eye test: use both, and examine disagreements. [44:33], [45:34]
