# Case Study: Moving the Fast Session From Thursday to Friday at Kentucky

**What it is** — Morris describes one program's in-season change (QLdbKUiNadg [16:05], [16:36]).
- **Before:** Monday to Wednesday practice, Thursday the faster practice, Friday a complete walkthrough, Saturday game. With an evening Saturday game that left "48 hours potentially 72 hours" with little central-nervous-system or bioenergetic stimulus, and athletes "ended up coming out flat" (QLdbKUiNadg [16:36]).
- **Change:** a "fast Friday", keeping intensity on Friday while volume stays low.
- **Reported effect:** "our readiness will go you know through the roof both subjectively and objectively on game day"; the heart rate variability device ("mega wave", caption spelling) had shifted more parasympathetic under the old pattern, and subjective wellness rose on Saturday after the flip (QLdbKUiNadg [16:05], [16:36], [17:07]).
- **Measures:** wellness questionnaires plus a heart rate variability wearable, with the explicit caveat that heart rate variability gives "good things and bad things" (QLdbKUiNadg [16:05]).
- **Linemen detail:** linemen "don't do anything on Fridays" and the Friday drop is their lowest day, so they need the stimulation as much as the skill players (QLdbKUiNadg [13:58]). Skill players target about 75% of game high-speed distance in the week (QLdbKUiNadg [13:27]).

**Why it works** — The speaker's account is that a long gap with no stimulation leaves the nervous system and energy systems flat for the game (QLdbKUiNadg [16:36]). He gives no data or test of that mechanism. Inference: it could also be that the athletes simply feel better with a familiar-intensity day, which the talk cannot separate.

**When to apply** — As an option to examine for evening or late-kickoff games where hard work ends three days out. Eric's baseline: Eric (interview 2026-09-28, Q15) describes Baylor's game week as Sunday off, Monday heavy lift, Tuesday and Wednesday heavy practice with a lighter lift Wednesday, and "lifting and hard practice end 72 hours before a Saturday game". Inference: the rule of ending hard work 72 hours out and the Fast Friday pattern are different designs, and this talk cannot decide between them. Eric's baseline: Eric's own SportSmith writing states that subjective wellness surveys were dropped for general roster use because athletes learned to game them (`2026-02-19-sportsmith-6otb-er.md`), so the subjective half of this evidence is the half he has distrusted.

**When NOT to apply** — Confidence is untested: one team's own change, no control, no numbers shown for the readiness changes, and no result on performance or injury. The speaker reports mixed value of heart rate variability. Wellness scores are subjective. Do not cite as evidence that Friday intensity improves game performance. Do not copy it into a Baylor week without Athletic Performance and the head coach, because it conflicts with the 72-hour rule above.

**Example** — The talk's own sequence: Thursday fast day plus Friday walkthrough replaced by Friday fast day, then readiness read from wellness and heart rate variability on Saturday (QLdbKUiNadg [16:05], [16:36]).

# Usage Bias: A Model Can Undervalue a Passer on a Run-Heavy Team, and a Pass Rusher Who Forces Runs

**What it is** — Paul Sabin names the main deficiency of his model: a quarterback who is very good at passing but whose coach does not use him that way (his example is Russell Wilson) may not show up as high as he should (oZsFyPGij0U [16:28]). He raises the mirror case on defense, Aaron Donald, typically considered the best defensive player. Against the pass his win rates are "so much better than everyone else it's not even on the chart," but against the run he is "not that great," and teams can usually run more effectively with him on the field (oZsFyPGij0U [16:28], [16:58]). Sabin's argument for Donald's value is that you would rather force a team to run, since running is less efficient (oZsFyPGij0U [16:58]). His planned fix is separate run and pass models next season, so players are not hurt by how they are used (oZsFyPGij0U [16:28], [16:58]). He also mentions Derrick Henry as roughly a top-10 impact player going into the playoffs (oZsFyPGij0U [15:58]). In recent NFL seasons the model's top players were Patrick Mahomes and Lamar Jackson, as spoken (oZsFyPGij0U [15:58]).

**Why it works** — Not a mechanism claim. The speaker's account is that a single value number pools run and pass plays, so a player whose strength lies on one side is graded on the play mix his coach chooses (oZsFyPGij0U [16:28]). The Donald point (forcing less efficient plays) is the speaker's assertion with no figures given.

**When to apply** — As a standing question when reading any single-number player grade: what is the player's usage, and what would the grade be if the coach called the plays differently? Inference: PFF-style grades and snap-based values at Baylor have the same exposure to play-calling mix. Eric's baseline: no direct link found.

**When NOT to apply** — Confidence is untested: these are two named examples and a stated plan, with no numbers shown and no tested fix. The Donald claim about run-defense performance is a recollection ("teams usually can run more effectively when he's on the field"), not data in the talk. One case does not show how large the bias is across positions. The planned run and pass split was not built at the time of the talk, so no result exists.

**Example** — The speaker's own, above: Wilson (strong passer, run-heavy team) and Donald (elite pass rusher, weaker against the run) are the two illustrations of usage and play-type confounding.

# If the Alactic System Is Stimulated Only on Saturday, It May Detrain: A Five-to-Eight-Day Rule of Thumb

**What it is** — Morris says that "from a physiologist standpoint" an energy system responsible for alactic power is lost if it is not stimulated "every five to eight days", so a lineman who only does game-like work on Saturday "was potentially detraining across the season" (QLdbKUiNadg [08:47]). He classifies the lineman's work as alactic: efforts of about 8 seconds, "anything less than ten all-out", repeated, with a work-to-rest ratio of about 1 to 3.75 (QLdbKUiNadg [10:50], [11:21]). The assertion is stated as a general fact with no citation.

**Why it works** — The speaker gives no mechanism and no source for the five-to-eight-day figure. Inference: it is a detraining (loss of a trained quality without re-exposure) argument, and it sits in the same range as Issurin's residual duration for maximum speed (alactic), 5 ± 3 days, in the existing library entry below. Issurin's table is about a different population and different quality (maximal speed), so this is a similarity, not a confirmation.

**When to apply** — As a planning prompt: check when, in the week, each position group gets its most game-like, brief, all-out exposure. Eric's baseline: Eric (interview 2026-09-28, Q16): "I believe Issurin. It drives our speed programming in the off-season and what drives sprinting recommendations in season." Eric's baseline: Eric (interview 2026-09-28, Q15) describes the in-season game week as heavy lift Monday, heavy practice Tuesday and Wednesday, and lifting and hard practice ending 72 hours before a Saturday game.

**When NOT to apply** — Confidence is untested: an assertion with no data shown. The talk measures contact load in practice versus game but never measures loss of alactic capacity, so "potentially detraining" is a hypothesis about Kentucky's linemen. Do not read the range as an Issurin-style tested figure for line play. It applies to a quality (repeated alactic power efforts) that the speaker says matches an 8-second play, and it should not be extended to other energy systems.

**Example** — Inference: a line group that has a speed or short-effort exposure Tuesday and Wednesday but only walkthroughs after that goes about 72 hours with no all-out work before a Saturday game; that is the pattern the speaker worries about (QLdbKUiNadg [16:36]).

# A failed attempt to prove that curveballs are more dangerous than fastballs

**What it is** — Glenn Fleisig says ASMI had advised for years not to throw curveballs until the growth plates close ("don't throw curve balls until you're old enough to shave") and ran studies to prove it (AgouMNTeyo4 [38:40], [39:11]). The result went the other way:
- Lab: in three biomechanics studies (two in Birmingham, one in Connecticut, some by other groups), elbow torque in a curveball was the same as or a little less than in a fastball (AgouMNTeyo4 [39:11], [39:41]).
- Field: in the 10-year cohort of about 500 youth pitchers, those who started throwing curveballs younger were not the ones who got hurt (AgouMNTeyo4 [40:12]).
- Conclusion: "we have not proven that the curve ball is dangerous. We have proven pitching too much is dangerous" (AgouMNTeyo4 [40:12], [40:42]).

In Q&A he is careful: failing to prove danger is not proving safety, so young pitchers should still learn a fastball and a changeup first and add a curveball "when you're ready", because the purpose of off-speed pitches is to disturb the batter's timing (AgouMNTeyo4 [52:31], [53:02]). On pitch type, his group has studied fastballs, curveballs, changeups and sliders: fastballs, curveballs and sliders are stressful, changeups less so; split-fingered fastballs were not studied (AgouMNTeyo4 [53:02]).

**Why it works** — Fleisig's measure of stress is elbow torque calculated from motion capture: kinematics (positions and angles) from cameras and kinetics (forces) calculated by equation, torque of about 100 N·m at the cocked position for a fastball (AgouMNTeyo4 [11:46], [12:17], [15:27]). Inference: if torque is the right proxy for ligament load, a pitch with equal torque should carry equal ligament risk; the speaker does not test that proxy in this talk (he says the link from arm stress to injury is unproven, AgouMNTeyo4 [58:38]).

**When to apply** — As a worked example of a prior belief tested by two designs (lab mechanics and field cohort) and reported when it failed. Also when a coaching rule rests on tradition: ask what the stress measure and the field outcome actually show.

Eric's baseline: Applied Performance's mission is to help the team "answer questions, ask better questions, and discover new ones" rather than hand down answers unilaterally (Explicit, Playbook p.6). Inference: Fleisig reversing a position his own center held is the posture that mission describes.
**When NOT to apply** — Baseball pitching in youth to professional levels; the torque comparison does not transfer to any football skill. The speaker does not show the torque numbers for the three curveball studies or the cohort statistics, so the "same or less" result is as stated. "Not proven dangerous" is not "safe", as he says himself (AgouMNTeyo4 [52:31]). The cohort is the same one that shows the 100-innings result, and the tested pitches did not include the split-finger. A rule about when to introduce a pitch is a coaching and medical decision; do not remove an age guideline on this result alone.

**Example** — The speaker's own contrast: ASMI's advice before the studies against its results (AgouMNTeyo4 [39:11], [40:42]).

# Adding the model improves game prediction over traditional metrics, but the Vegas line stays best

**What it is** — The speaker, Ben Jenkins, tests how well the model predicts games on out-of-sample data, against a Vegas line benchmark. He reports that the Vegas line alone "is obviously the best, has the highest win prediction"; adding the deep RL Shapley values improves over the traditional metrics, and adding the synergy effects improves accuracy further, getting closer to the Vegas line (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42], [12:12]). He concludes the learned values "capture real forward-looking predictive signals" (JjeFktc4QLM [12:12]). The accuracy figures are shown only on a chart and are not stated in the captions, so no effect size can be cited here (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42]). The validation sums the players' Shapley values on the court to predict games, which a judge questioned (JjeFktc4QLM [13:13], [13:44]).

**Why it works** — The speaker gives no mechanism beyond the value being learned from outcomes and including interaction. Inference: a benchmark that already prices public information (the Vegas line) is a hard test, so falling short of it is the expected result for a player-value model rather than a failure.

**When to apply** — As a model for how to validate a player-value metric: hold out data, compare against an existing predictive benchmark, and add the new metric to it rather than replacing it. Inference: a football grade or load metric could be tested the same way against a simple baseline on held-out games.

**When NOT to apply** — Confidence is supported only for the design: out-of-sample comparison against named benchmarks is reported, but no numbers, no uncertainty, no data split and no number of games appear in the captions. The data are about five seasons of NBA play-by-play (JjeFktc4QLM [16:21]). Do not read this as beating the market: the Vegas line was best (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42]). Do not assume a similar gain for a football metric.

**Example** — The speaker's own ladder: Vegas line alone, then adding the model's values, then adding synergy, each step closer to the Vegas line (JjeFktc4QLM [11:42], [12:12]).

# Faster Pitches Put More Stress on the Elbow, Youth and Professional

**What it is** — Dr. Eric McNally's group at Henry Ford uses a wearable sleeve that detects stress and torque at the medial elbow, "right where the UCL is" (z_6M5r7031A [04:09]). He reports that, "study after study," the faster the pitch, the more elbow stress, and that this is pitch speed, not arm speed (z_6M5r7031A [04:39]). He says it holds for a 12-year-old youth pitcher and for a professional, and that it has been borne out in "numerous" independent studies from his group (z_6M5r7031A [04:39] to [05:10]). He contrasts it with older advice to avoid curveballs and off-speed pitches and "stick with straight fastballs" (z_6M5r7031A [04:39]).

Related statements from the talk:
- Arm slot: studies of slot and release point exist, and "so far" slot has not shown increased elbow stress; this is the group's next stage (z_6M5r7031A [22:33]).
- Device limit: the sleeve relies on a throwing motion, so it cannot measure elbow stress in hitters (z_6M5r7031A [21:23]).
- Trend: average fastball velocity is climbing year over year, which he offers as one of two reasons the injury persists (z_6M5r7031A [04:09]).

**Why it works** — The speaker gives no mechanism. Inference: a faster release plausibly needs more force through the arm, so more load at the elbow is plausible, but the talk does not say so.

**When to apply** — As background when a throwing sport (baseball, softball) asks whether to monitor throwing stress or velocity. Eric's baseline: a test or technology that "doesn't answer a question anyone has, or a direct need" has no place (Explicit, interview 2026-09-28, Q3). Inference: ask first what decision a sleeve or velocity number would change, then check validation (the talk does not describe any validation of the sleeve).

**When NOT to apply** — The talk gives no effect sizes, sample sizes or ranges for these studies. The studies are the speaker's group's own, summarized aloud. The sleeve's validity is not discussed in the talk. It covers pitching only; he says UCL injury is essentially one position of one sport (z_6M5r7031A [21:54]). The slot finding is a preliminary "so far." Do not use it as a velocity limit for any athlete.

**Example** — The speaker's own contrast: the old coaching rule was to avoid curveballs and change-ups because of elbow stress, while his group finds high-velocity fastballs also impart greater stress, in a 12-year-old and in a professional (z_6M5r7031A [04:39] to [05:10]).

# Inertial Movement Analysis May Describe Linemen Where GPS Distance Does Not

**What it is** — Patrick Ward (caption spelling "Patrick ward") defines inertial movement analysis (IMA) as a count of movements in different directions (forward, backward, right, left and in between), built from accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer data and reported in three threshold bands (TbYgc6_qQzo [09:12]). His argument: GPS-type measures say a lot about outside players such as defensive backs and wide receivers, who produce much locomotor activity, but little about offensive and defensive linemen, because reliability falls when units record high-intensity activity in small spaces, "say below 20 meters," which is what linemen do (TbYgc6_qQzo [09:44]). He says "we think this may be a useful metric" (TbYgc6_qQzo [09:44]).

Support offered, all described verbally without data: earlier work showing the metrics differentiate position groups in running and non-running activity, and proof-of-concept examples: a wide receiver running a straight sprint and a cut, the 5-10-5 pro agility drill, and a sled hit and drive-through (TbYgc6_qQzo [10:14] to [11:14]).

**Why it works** — The speaker's reasoning is the unit-reliability point above. The talk gives no figures for reliability by distance or for how IMA performs for linemen.

**When to apply** — Inference: when reporting offensive and defensive line sessions, where total distance may under-describe the work. Eric's baseline: IMA is among the core GPS metrics tracked, used for indoor sessions without GPS coverage (Explicit, Playbook pp.11-13). Inference: this talk proposes a different use for the same metric, small-space line work, which the baseline does not state.

**When NOT to apply** — Confidence is untested: no data are shown for the linemen claim, and the examples are single-athlete drills. The 20-meter reliability point is the speaker's statement with no citation. IMA band thresholds are not given. In the injury study, IMA models were fit but the joint model did not retain IMA, and no position-group effect was found, so the study does not show IMA helps for linemen (TbYgc6_qQzo [14:20], [15:22]).

**Example** — The speaker's own list of proof-of-concept exercises includes a collision-based sled hit and drive-through, alongside the 5-10-5 (TbYgc6_qQzo [10:44] to [11:14]). The transcript does not report what IMA showed for any of them.

# A Lottery Based Only on Final Ranking Cannot Favor the Worst Team Without Some Incentive to Tank

**What it is** — Evan Monroe (Stanford PhD student; caption spellings "Monroe" and "Munroe"; joint work with a fellow student the captions call "Martino") says the league's two objectives, backed out from its rule changes and statements, are (1) give the top pick to the lowest-ranked team with as high a probability as possible, subject to (2) removing incentives to tank (-6pe_zOKCig [04:39], [05:09]). Under a lottery that depends only on final win-loss ranking, "it's impossible" to favor the worst team at all without some incentive to tank (-6pe_zOKCig [08:44]). The options are to minimize the incentive by not favoring the worst team, or use a uniform lottery as in 1985 to 1989 (-6pe_zOKCig [08:44]). He quotes a hockey writer's line (caption spelling Greg Wyshynski) that if you do not want tanking you do not want the welfare mechanism that encourages it (-6pe_zOKCig [06:10], [06:41]). History he gives: before 1985 a coin flip between the two conference losers (worst team 50 percent), then an equal lottery for non-playoff teams that cut the worst team's chance to "between 11 and 14 percent" depending on team count (captions garbled), a 1990 figure the captions give as "70%" (looks garbled), 25 percent in 1994, and 14 percent in the most recent change (-6pe_zOKCig [03:09], [03:39], [04:09], [05:39]). He cites prior work showing statistically that teams with incentives to lose late in a season lose more (-6pe_zOKCig [06:41], [07:12]).

**Why it works** — Speaker's argument (intuition, not the proof, which he says is in the paper): once a team is eliminated from the playoffs, its chance of making them is zero, so under a fixed lottery that favors bad teams, losing any game that affects its final ranking improves its draft odds (-6pe_zOKCig [10:49], [11:20]). The league sometimes penalizes egregious cases (-6pe_zOKCig [11:20]). The formal proof is not shown in the talk and was not checked here.

**When to apply** — As a design test for any rule that rewards a poor result: if the reward depends only on the final outcome and favors the weakest, ask whether a rational participant gains by making the outcome worse. Eric's baseline: daily subjective wellness surveys were dropped after about a year because athletes learned the low-effort answers that looked clean but carried no signal (Explicit, SportSmith, 2026-02-19). Inference: that is a different mechanism (low effort, not deliberate losing) and the link to this result is by analogy only.

**When NOT to apply** — Confidence is untested: the result is a theorem inside a model where teams value only the playoffs and the first pick, at fixed values, and only the first pick is allocated (-6pe_zOKCig [02:39], [09:46]). It covers league policy, not athlete monitoring. Real teams have broader goals, as the speaker says (-6pe_zOKCig [09:46]). His claim that tanking is "most intractable in the NBA" because key players matter so much is an assertion with no data shown (-6pe_zOKCig [02:39]).

**Example** — The speaker's own: an eliminated team late in the season with a lottery that favors bad teams; every game affecting its ranking is one it gains by losing (-6pe_zOKCig [10:49], [11:20]).

# If every concussion were caught and managed strictly, about one in five players would be out at any time

**What it is** — A back-of-envelope availability model. The speaker, using the survey figure that about half of athletes have a concussion each year, takes a 50-person football team with half concussed (25 players) and 10 days out each: 250 days, "about 4% of playing days", "not completely disruptive" (npgdKKdJSww [12:12]). He then argues recovery is longer than 10 days (see the vulnerability-window entry) and reruns it at 60 days out: "21% of playing days", "one out of five athletes out at all times", about four of a football team's starters and one hockey starter at any moment, not counting repeat concussions (npgdKKdJSww [13:43], [14:14]). His conclusion is that either medical guidelines get much looser ("which won't happen") or the games change, starting with how much contact they contain (npgdKKdJSww [14:45], [22:58]).

He says he did the math "on the plane" and that "the science is iffy" on the 10-day figure (npgdKKdJSww [12:12]).

**Why it works** — It is arithmetic on three assumed inputs: incidence, days out, and playing days in a season. The speaker gives the first two and not the third. Inference: 250 days equal 4% only if the team has about 6,250 player-days (50 players over about 125 days), and the 60-day case then gives 1,500 days over 6,250, or 24%, not the 21% he states; the two figures are not exactly proportional and the denominator is not shown. Treat both as rough.

**When to apply** — As a reminder to test a proposed detection or return-to-play standard against roster availability before adopting it: ask what the standard would cost in player-days if applied to every case.

Eric's baseline: Applied Performance's role in return to performance is to supply information that supports Athletic Medicine and Athletic Performance decisions (Explicit, Playbook p.6), and it "doesn't assess readiness" in return to play, because "the template answers that" (interview 2026-09-28, Q22, Explicit). Inference: an availability estimate like this one is the kind of information Applied Performance could supply; the clearance standard itself stays with Athletic Medicine.

**When NOT to apply** — Do not use the 4% or 21% figures as estimates for Baylor or for any real team. The 50% incidence is a survey figure from the speaker's other studies; the 10-day and 60-day recoveries are assumptions, and the 60-day case is the speaker's hypothetical of what very sensitive tests might demand, not a clinical standard. The model ignores repeat concussions, position differences and substitutions. It is a 2012 talk, and the speaker discloses a consultant role and a test-owner tie (npgdKKdJSww [00:30]).

**Example** — The speaker's own: a 50-person football team, 25 concussed, 60 days out each, "four guys out on your football team every week out of your starting group" (npgdKKdJSww [14:14]).
