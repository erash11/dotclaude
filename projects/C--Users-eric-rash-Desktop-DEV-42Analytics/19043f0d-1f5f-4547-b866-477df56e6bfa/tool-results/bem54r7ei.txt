## Summary

A panel led by an economist asks whether teams in baseball, basketball and football learn from analytics, and how fast. The opening presentation takes two long-known mistakes per sport (bunts and steals, three-point shooting, fourth downs and draft pick valuation) and shows adoption was slow, in baseball taking decades. The panelists then offer reasons: a move that usually works feels right even when it is bad value, an organization needs understanding at every level, ownership must commit, and championships speed acceptance. The later discussion covers new frontiers (spatial football data, positionless basketball, two-way baseball players) and how to communicate with athletes. It matters as a set of first-hand explanations for why good analysis changes behavior slowly.

## Summary

An ESPN analytics specialist presents a plus-minus style model that estimates a single season-level value for every football player. Plain plus-minus fails in football because players share the field nearly all the time, so the model gives each position its own variance, pulls low-snap players toward a prior based on draft rank, recruiting data or last season, and uses a heavy-tailed distribution. Results rank quarterbacks highest and put centers second in importance, which the speaker finds surprising. He shows checks (quarterback versus line, transfers) and lists planned changes. The talk gives no outcome validation beyond these plausibility checks.

## Key claims

- Football has lacked a single stat that values players across all positions, jumping from passer rating to tracking data. [03:42], [04:13]
- A standard plus-minus approach applied to 2018 college data put the top quarterback first and three offensive linemen next, which the speaker calls useless. [08:17]
- Players on the same unit were on the field together about 98 to 99 percent of plays, so the model cannot separate them without extra structure. [08:49]
- The fix: give each position a different variance, where higher variance means greater impact. [09:19]
- Low-snap players are not assumed average; the model shrinks them toward a prior from draft rank, recruiting data or the previous season. [09:49], [10:21]
- Players who play multiple positions get a weighted average across positions by snap share. [11:24]
- A heavy-tailed distribution lets the best players sit further from average. [11:54]
- In college data for 2019, quarterback variance is by far the highest and the center is second. [13:26]
- The speaker explains the center result by the center's role in calling blocking schemes, which film may not show. [13:56], [14:27]
- Tight end here means a player with a hand on the ground; a standing slot player counts as slot receiver. [14:27], [14:57]
- The position ordering is stable across NFL seasons since 2007, with running backs showing a lot of uncertainty. [14:57], [15:28], [15:58]
- The model rated Patrick Mahomes and Lamar Jackson as the top players in recent NFL seasons and Derrick Henry as roughly top ten in impact. [15:58]
- The model's weakness: a strong passer on a run-heavy team may be undervalued (Russell Wilson example). [16:28]
- The previous-year prior held Joe Burrow back despite his strong season (caption: "Joe burrowed"). [17:30]
- Quarterback and line values are correlated but not tightly, so the model can separate them. [18:03], [18:34]
- Transfers in college improved on average; NFL movers declined on average. [19:05], [19:35]

## Summary

A Stanford PhD student presents a research-paper talk (joint work with a fellow student the captions name "Martino") on NBA draft design. The argument: a lottery that depends only on final win-loss ranking cannot favor the worst team without giving teams some reason to lose on purpose [08:44]. The proposed alternative ties the lottery to when wins and losses happen, re-estimating each team's chance of finishing last after every game and freezing those chances at a point chosen from the season's results [11:55, 13:26]. Simulations on past NBA seasons are shown. It matters because the league's objectives (help the worst team, discourage losing on purpose) are usually treated as opposed.

## Key claims

- Tanking means losing on purpose to improve draft position, and the speaker treats it as hardest to solve in the NBA because of how much key players matter [02:39].
- The league's goals, as the speaker infers them from past rule changes, are to favor the lowest-ranked team while removing any incentive to tank [04:39, 05:09].
- Neither goal is met well today: the worst team gets the first pick with 14% probability, and tanking chatter continues [05:39, 06:10].
- A lottery based only on final ranking cannot favor the worst team at all without leaving some incentive to tank. A uniform lottery (as in 1985 to 1989) removes the incentive but does not favor the worst team [08:44].
- A lottery based on when results happen can both favor the worst team and remove the incentive, in the speaker's model [09:15].
- Once a team is eliminated, losing games that affect its ranking improves its draft odds, so under a fixed lottery that favors bad teams a rational team should try to lose [10:49, 11:20].
- Early games are better evidence of a bad team and carry weaker incentives to lose, so the policy counts them more [16:30, 21:03].
- In the 1989 example, Miami is the worst team and gets the first pick with 44 percent probability under the proposed rule, versus equal odds under the lottery used that year [15:28].
- The speaker admits the rule does worse when the pick is very valuable: in the stress case built around a star prospect (named in captions as Patrick Ewing) the top team gets only 19% [18:31, 19:01].
- In the 2019 example, the other seven non-playoff teams get 18.5% under the current lottery, versus about 5% combined under the proposed rule [20:02, 20:32].
- The speaker concedes the rule might raise the incentive to rebuild over several seasons, which the model does not cover [13:58, 23:33].

## Numbers and definitions

- Before 1985 the two conference losers had a coin flip, so the worst team had a 50% chance [03:09].
- From 1985 an equal lottery for non-playoff teams cut the worst team's chance to "between 11 and 14 percent", depending on team count (phrasing garbled in captions) [03:39].
- The captions give a 1990 figure as "70%", which looks garbled; 1994 is 25%; the most recent change (called "last year") is 14% [04:09].
- No-tanking condition: the benefit of winning a game should be at least the benefit of losing it [10:17].
- Model setup: teams value two things, making the playoffs and getting the first pick, each at a fixed value [09:46]. Results are very sensitive when the two values are near equal and flat once the playoff value is three to five times the pick's [25:06].
- Freeze point: probabilities are fixed after roughly 330 games in the 1989 simulation; the speaker says the rule adjusts "over the first 300 games" and the freeze comes "a third of the way through the season" [17:00, 17:30]. These are slightly inconsistent in the captions.
