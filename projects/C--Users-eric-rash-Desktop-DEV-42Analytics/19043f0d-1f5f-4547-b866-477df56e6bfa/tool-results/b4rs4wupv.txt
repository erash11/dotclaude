## Summary

Ben Jenkins, a PhD candidate at Florida Atlantic University, presents a causal model for crediting defenders in American football. Traditional metrics credit only the tackler, and predictive models favor high-volume tacklers. His framework models all 22 players jointly with a transformer, estimates expected points saved for each defender by comparing expected points added with and without that defender's tackle, and uses propensity weighting and doubly robust estimation. On the first 9 weeks of a 2022 season it credits set-up defenders, ranks known top players better than supervised models, and aggregates to team results. He calls it a proof of concept.

## Key claims

- Football has a defensive attribution problem: on a 6-yard loss, traditional metrics credit only the tackler though several defenders contributed. [00:00] [00:31]
- Supervised models predict outcomes, mix correlation with causation, favor high-volume tacklers and struggle to separate skill from opportunity. [01:01] [01:31]
- Causal inference asks what would have happened without the defender; it isolates individual contributions and separates player from scheme better, but needs more assumptions and quality control and cannot be applied in every scenario. [01:31] [02:01]
- Defenders do not act independently, so the model covers coordination, interference (for example, a defensive end setting the edge so a linebacker can tackle) and substitution effects, which violate independence. [02:31] [03:01] [03:31]
- The framework, called expected points saved, treats a defender making the tackle as the treatment and expected points added as the outcome. [04:02] [04:33]
- It uses a transformer over all 22 players, a propensity score model, an outcome model and inverse propensity weighting. [04:33] [05:04] [05:35]
- On the example play, the tackler scores highest and four other defenders get positive credit, which the speaker says shows the model captures coordinated effort. [06:09]
- Aaron Donald's expected points saved is around 0.27. [06:40]
- The method ranks players who earned All-Pro or Pro Bowl honors better than supervised models. [07:09] [07:39]
- Mean individual values aggregate to team level and explain 41% of variance in tackle play outcomes. [07:39]
- On synthetic data the method shows higher R-squared and lower RMSE than independent models, and ranks non-rookie players better against salaries and Pro Bowl status. [08:40]
- 68% of plays show significant interference effects, and modeling them reduces bias. [08:40] [09:10]
- The speaker calls it the first multi-agent causal inference framework in professional football for defensive evaluation; it is a proof of concept using the first 9 weeks of the 2022 regular season. [09:10]
- Intended next steps are quarterback decision-making and defensive back evaluation; stated uses are player evaluation, contract decisions and defensive scheme optimization. [09:41] [10:11]

## Numbers and definitions

- Expected points saved: expected points added on the play when the defender does not make the tackle, minus expected points added when he does (as described). [04:02]
- Expected points added: the traditional NFL metric of how much the offense benefits on a play. [04:02]
- Treatment: a binary indicator that the defender made the tackle. [04:02]
- 6-yard loss: the example running play. [00:00]

## Methods and frameworks named

- Interference-aware causal framework with a multi-agent transformer over 22 players. [02:31] [04:33]
- A directed acyclic graph with features: each defender's position, speed and direction, ball carrier features, relational features, 10 teammates, opponents and game context. [04:33] [05:04]
- Propensity score model and outcome model, with inverse propensity weighting to make a pseudo-population; doubly robust estimation for protection against model misspecification. [05:04] [05:35] [09:41]

## Limits and open questions

## Summary

Ben Jenkins presents a deep reinforcement learning model that learns NBA player value from game outcomes, treating basketball as a sequence of states, actions and rewards, and splits team value among players with a neural approximation of Shapley values. He argues that identical box score actions have very different win impact depending on game state, that defense and off-ball value are missed by box score metrics, and that player synergy is measurable and predictive. He reports faster stabilization than regularized adjusted plus-minus, a Rudy Gobert example, 89 positive and 61 negative synergies, and better game prediction than traditional metrics, though the Vegas line remains best.

## Key claims

- Winning depends on context: the same made three-pointer matters more late in a close game, and value depends on teammates, opponents and interactions among the five on the floor. [00:00] [00:31]
- PER, win shares and box plus-minus aggregate box score statistics with fixed weights, overweight scoring, struggle with defense and lack context. [01:01] [01:31]
- Adjusted and regularized adjusted plus-minus regress possession outcomes on players on the floor but assume context-invariant contributions and miss synergy. [01:31]
- Sequential models such as expected possession value capture context and flow but struggle to credit individual players; the talk proposes deep reinforcement learning to combine both. [02:02] [02:32]
- In the example, a made three in the first quarter with a 15-point lead changed win probability by 2%, while one in the fourth quarter of a tied game with 1:30 left changed it by 23%; the speaker calls this a 10 times difference. [03:04]
- The model treats basketball as a Markov decision process and uses an advantage function for context-aware value. [04:05] [04:35]
- It values on-ball actions by action type, role and context, and values defense and off-ball influence by presence and constraints, not individual actions. [04:35] [05:07]
- Shapley values allocate team value by replacing each player with a learned baseline embedding; a neural approximation is used for computation. [05:38] [06:08]
- Compared with regularized adjusted plus-minus, the method needs about 67% fewer possessions for comparable accuracy and stabilizes in about 3 weeks versus 2 months. [06:38] [07:08]
- Action values peak in close clutch games, where a three-pointer can be worth more than double the traditional value, and diminish in blowouts; offensive rebounds and steals are valued significantly. [07:39]
- Jokic ranks first in the top 15; the ranking agrees substantially with regularized adjusted plus-minus and worse with box plus-minus, which the speaker ties to defensive valuation. [08:09] [08:40]
- Rudy Gobert ranks 54th on traditional metrics and 12th on this method; the box score missed 45% of his defensive value. [09:10]
- Defensive specialists are systematically undervalued by traditional metrics, a potential competitive advantage, and complementary skills matter in roster construction. [09:40]
- The study found 89 positive and 61 negative synergies, and traditional metrics wrongly assume player values add. [10:10] [10:41]
- Two equally talented rosters can differ by five or more wins because of fit, and adding synergy explains some unexplained variance. [11:11] [11:42]
- Adding the model to a Vegas line benchmark improves prediction, and adding synergy improves it further, though the Vegas line alone is best. [11:42] [12:12]
- In a capped league, winning comes from optimizing interactions, not collecting stars. [12:43]

## Numbers and definitions

- 67% fewer possessions than regularized adjusted plus-minus for comparable accuracy. [06:38]

- About five seasons of play-by-play data, "thousands of games." [16:21]

## Methods and frameworks named

- Markov decision process with states, actions, stochastic transitions and rewards in expected points and win probability. [04:05] [04:35]
- Temporal-difference learning and a distributional win-probability model appear in the video description but are not described in the captions.
