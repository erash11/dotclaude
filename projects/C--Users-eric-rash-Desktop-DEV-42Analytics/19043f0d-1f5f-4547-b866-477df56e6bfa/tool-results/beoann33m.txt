## Summary

A research-paper-competition talk that relates inertial sensor training load to non-contact time-loss injuries in football. It starts with the limits of earlier load and injury papers (binary injury coding, no censoring, small samples, thresholds that vary by team, retrospective rather than predictive) and cites work finding single-club models predict poorly on unseen data. The study followed 101 players for one season with 29 non-contact injuries. The best model combined total player load, low player load and high impacts: higher volume and higher physical intensity went with higher risk, and the speaker frames this as risk context for practitioners, not prediction.

## Key claims

- Non-contact injuries matter because they are thought to be partly due to training errors: too much too soon, too much for too long, or too little [03:03].
- Earlier papers often merge contact and non-contact injuries, mix time loss and non-time loss, skip censoring, give thresholds that differ across papers, and are retrospective [03:33], [04:04], [05:06], [06:08].
- A cited Australian football study found single-club injury models predicted poorly on unseen data [06:39], [07:10].
- The aim was to find associations between training load variables and non-contact time-loss injury, not to predict before a session [07:41].
- Metrics used were total player load, four player load effort bands, impacts (three thresholds) and IMA (three thresholds) [08:12], [08:42], [09:12].
- The speaker argues IMA helps with linemen because GPS-type measures are less reliable for high-intensity work in spaces under 20 meters [09:44].
- The joint model (total player load, player load low, impacts high) fit best; total player load and impacts high carried the highest added risk, and player load low was inversely related [14:20], [14:51], [15:22].
- Position group did not add explanatory value, possibly because of too few injuries per group [15:22], [15:53].
- Volume and intensity are risk factors to be combined with coaches' own judgment [15:53], [16:23].
- Injury is multifactorial; intrinsic and extrinsic factors and their change over a season would be needed for prediction [17:24], [17:54].

## Numbers and definitions

- Hundreds of millions: about 200 million dollars per year paid to players on injured reserve, in "some articles" [01:02].
- Knee injuries 3.1 per 1000 athlete exposures; hip and hamstring 2.2 per 1000 (ten years of NFL training camp data, cited work by "feely and colleagues", caption spelling) [02:03].
- Athlete exposure: one athlete in one game or training session [02:33].
- Season length: 16 NFL games, 9 to 13 in college [01:32].
- Sample: 101 football athletes, one full season preseason through regular season [11:14].
- Definition: non-contact injury that caused missed later sessions or games, coded by a medical professional [11:14], [11:45].
- Training load normalized per minute and standardized by position group [11:45], [12:15].
- Models: logistic regression, compared with Bayesian information criterion and out-of-sample likelihood, with leave-one-out-style cross validation [12:47], [13:18]. Caption reads "four logistic regression models" as "for"; model count is ambiguous (one per variable group plus a joint model).
- 29 non-contact injuries [13:18].
- Caption problems: the figure "200 million dollars" at [01:02] appears as captioned; "Bayesian information criterion" is clear; effect sizes (odds ratios) are not given.

## Methods and frameworks named

- Catapult-type inertial sensors with player load, effort bands, impacts and IMA (accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer) [08:12], [09:12].
- Censoring of athletes removed from a session by injury [12:15].
- Logistic regression and leave-one-out style validation on one season [12:47], [13:18].

## Limits and open questions

- One team, one season; 29 injuries is small and no position-group effect was found [13:18], [15:22].
- No game load, so no games injuries [16:53].
- Association only; the speaker says prediction would need intrinsic and extrinsic factors [17:24].
- Effect sizes and thresholds were not shown, and several injury types were pooled [16:23].
- Most injuries occurred in training camp, per Q&A [22:05].
- Underreporting of pain is a recognized limit [24:10].

## Q&A

- Is the problem data or random assignment, and could effort drive both load and injury? Random assignment would help if a coach agreed; effort perception data exist but were not in this paper [18:24], [19:27].
- A panelist said a full picture needs internal load, HRV, hydration, hip mobility asymmetry and pain, and RPE; the speaker said no research shows joint range or posture predicts injury, and measures would change during a season [19:59], [20:29].
- Did the 101 players include roster cuts and did the final 53 differ? Most injuries were in camp and effort looks similar across players, with starters managed more [21:35], [22:05].
- Medical-attention and contact injuries? They looked at time loss and non-time loss; underreporting is a big limit; contact injury and over-exertion link is plausible but hard to model [23:09], [24:10], [24:41].

# Applications: Volume and Intensity in Injury Incidence in Football Athletes

## Where this applies

- Inference: Catapult practice reporting. The talk's three useful metrics (total player load as volume, impacts high as intensity, player load low as protective) [14:51], [15:22] suggest which columns to review beside the standard load view after each practice, rather than relying on one load number.
- Inference: Line play monitoring. The speaker argues IMA is more informative for linemen than GPS-style distance because of small-space, high-intensity work [09:44]. This applies to how Eric reports OL and DL sessions, where total distance may under-describe load.
- Inference: Injury logging. The talk's definition (non-contact, time loss, medically coded, athlete removed from the injury day's data after the event [11:14], [12:15]) can be used as a template for how Athletic Medicine and Applied Performance code injuries so load analyses are comparable.
- Inference: Preseason camp planning. Q&A says most injuries happened in training camp [22:05], so camp-week load comparisons by position group are where to look first.

## Eric's baseline

- Eric's baseline: The core GPS metrics tracked include player load, field time and IMA for indoor sessions, in the Catapult workflow. The talk uses the same families of metrics, so it agrees with the tracked set but extends it with injury analysis.
- Eric's baseline: No sport- or position-specific GPS thresholds exist in the data dictionary, and no formal injury-risk model is attributed to Eric. The talk extends this: it shows how a team might build position-standardized load measures (z-type scaling within position [11:45]) but gives no thresholds, and the speaker warns published thresholds vary by team [05:06].
- Eric's baseline: Injury risk is reduced, not eliminated, and abrupt progression is treated as a driver (Inferred, course team position, speaker unconfirmed). The speaker's stance that the aim is associated risk, not prediction [07:41], agrees.
- Eric's baseline: "Culture of fear" criticism of load monitoring (Inferred, course team position, speaker unconfirmed). The talk's cautious framing, a risk factor that a coach combines with experience [16:23], is compatible.

## What would have to be true

- Enough non-contact time-loss injuries to model. The talk had 29 injuries across 101 athletes in one season [13:18], and still could not model position groups [15:22].
- Clean, consistent injury coding by Athletic Medicine and a rule for censoring athletes who leave a session injured [12:47].
- Same device settings and band thresholds across the season; the speaker says the effort bands and IMA each use fixed thresholds [08:42], [09:12] but does not give their values.
- Game load, which the talk lacked [16:53]; Baylor game data would need to be added to say anything about game injuries.
- Out-of-sample checking, since the speaker cites work showing single-club models predict poorly on unseen data [06:39].

## Do not apply when

- Using these results as thresholds for Baylor athletes. One team, one season and 29 injuries cannot support a cut point, and the speaker himself says thresholds bounce around [05:06].
- Using it to change practice plans because a session scored high on impacts. The result is an association, not a predictive tool [07:41].
- Applying to non-football athletes or to games.
- Reading it as evidence for a mobility screen: the speaker says no research links specific joint range or posture to injury [20:29].

## First step

Export one season of Baylor Catapult data to a spreadsheet with total player load per minute and IMA counts by position group, and match it to the Athletic Medicine non-contact time-loss injury list. Just describe it; build no model.
