## Summary

Two industrial and organizational psychologists describe a one-hour psychological assessment given to every player at the NFL combine since 2013. It measures cognitive ability and personality traits tied to football, and is meant as an addition to physical and film evidence. A three-year validation study compared combine scores with coach ratings of players after at least a year, plus penalties and injuries. Correlations are modest (about 0.22 to 0.35) but the speakers show that top and bottom scorers are rated very differently. The second half describes player-type clusters, a bust-rate comparison using a public value metric, and planned longitudinal modeling of second contracts.

## Key claims

- The goal was to add a standardized psychological measure alongside combine physical tests, not to replace them [04:13], [06:17].
- A league study with team executives produced 16 factors, with intelligence and personality as the two key areas [05:46].
- Intelligence items cover learning, thinking quickly and avoiding mental errors; personality items cover leadership, teamwork, self-control, dependability, preparedness and team fit [06:50].
- The test has run at the combine since 2013 with full participation, and the data were also turned into a coaching report [07:20], [07:51].
- The validation compared pre-draft scores with coach ratings after the players had been on a team for at least a year [09:54].
- The speaker calls correlations of this size good for behavioral science and says they are uncorrected [10:24].
- High, middle and low test scores matched coach rating bands; coaches were more straightforward than expected [12:27], [12:59].
- Low scorers on intelligence were about three times as likely to be rated poor as star; high scorers about three times as likely rated star [13:30].
- Personality: low scorers were about four times as likely to be rated low, high scorers about four and a half times as likely rated high [14:32].
- Intelligence and personality together predicted some ratings better, such as decisions under stress [15:33], [16:03].
- Decisions under stress also correlated with penalties per play and yards per penalty, and the result held in a larger sample [16:35], [17:07].
- Personality variables relate to injuries only weakly, with significance, in an early study; injury type has not been examined [17:37], [18:07].
- Cluster analysis found nine player types combining coach-rated physical talent and the psychological scores [22:16].
- A "whole package" cluster had a lower bust rate than a low-on-everything cluster, and two clusters with opposite strengths had the same failure rate [24:19], [25:20].
- The speakers argue this approach can find undervalued players and de-risk draft and contract decisions [25:51].
- Planned work: longitudinal models for second-contract decisions [26:53], [27:24].

## Numbers and definitions

- Test length: one hour [07:20]. Almost 100 players tested the morning of the talk [28:26].
- Validation: three years, 1,500 player scores, about 330 combine participants per year (plus a Super Regional), about 70 percent team participation across all 32 teams [08:52], [09:22].
- Coach ratings on about 24 dimensions after at least a year; about 500 players with performance data [09:54].
- Coaches' scale of about seven points [12:59].
- Correlations (captions read "0 28," "about3," "a0 29," "about 35," "0 22"): about 0.28 [10:24], about 0.3 for the cognitive composite [10:56], about 0.29 for personality [11:26], about 0.35 combined [15:33], 0.22 for decisions under stress [16:03]. The captions garble the decimal points; these are the likeliest readings.
- Top and bottom groups each about 25 percent of the sample [12:27].
- Rated low by coaches 13 percent of the time, good to great 87 percent (high intelligence scorers) [14:01]; 91 percent good to great (strong personality scorers) [15:02].
- Larger penalty sample about 400 players [17:07].
- Clusters: nine profiles from 2014 to 2016 data, shown as z-scores [22:16].
- "Approximate value" of four or below is read as a bust, about half of drafted players; six or above counts as successful [23:48], [24:19]. Bust rate 41 percent in the "whole package" cluster and 83 percent in the low cluster [24:19]. A "0%" figure for the six-or-above line in the low cluster is stated at [24:50]; the caption is unclear.
- Pick 199 in the sixth round (Tom Brady, 2000 draft) as an example of misses [03:11].

## Methods and frameworks named

- Competency model from a team-executive study; subscale items built from intelligence and personality dimensions [05:46], [06:50].
- Criterion validation against coach ratings, penalties, yards per penalty and injuries [09:54], [17:07], [17:37].
- Cluster analysis and mixture models for player types [21:46]; Pro Football Reference approximate value as the outcome [23:48].
- Planned longitudinal modeling [26:53].

## Limits and open questions

- The main outcome is coach opinion; the talk does not say whether coaches saw the test results [09:22].
- No confidence intervals, sample sizes by cluster or position breakdowns are shown for the clusters [22:16], [25:20].
- Injury relationship is small and unreported in effect size [17:37].
- Rookie-year and one-to-two-year outcomes only; long-term is future work [26:23].
- The speakers sell or run the assessment; Harold Goldstein, a co-author, was absent [00:03].
- Several names and terms are mis-captioned ("Wonderlick," "Dbacks," "Yak").
