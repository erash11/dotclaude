Eric's limiting factor on deep work is environmental, not motivational. He is highly productive in short bursts once work actually starts; what breaks is the path to starting. The specific disruptor he named (2026-07-28) is the pull to share what he's working on — with no standing outlet for it, he goes and finds people, which costs him the session.

**Corrected 2026-07-29.** The pull toward people is not a leak, it's the generative stage. His own observation: alone he wants company; in company the project ideas arrive. Solitude is where he refines, not where he generates. A long unbroken solo block with nothing in the hopper is waiting, not deep work.

**Second brake, same day:** he holds ideas back from fear of overstepping into others' lanes, against an unstated expectation that he act unasked. He is deliberately moving off this position ("if I don't act, ideas wither"). Pressfield's Resistance is his frame for it.

**Qualified 2026-08-03.** The permission gate is not entirely self-imposed. He acted unasked on a device another staffer was already championing (ShiftWave chair, second trial unit) and took real organizational blowback for it — careful, low-key handling bought nothing, because the claim was made by initiating, not by presenting. So the caution is warranted on ground someone has visibly attached themselves to, and unwarranted everywhere else. His same-day reaction ("pander to the people in power, keep your head down") is a bruise, not necessarily a settled position — don't treat it as one.

**Why:** Both reframe what looks like procrastination. The first is an unmet structural need (input/ideation) improvising in real time; the second is a self-imposed permission gate that reads as professional courtesy. Neither is a discipline failure, and treating them as one produces useless advice.

**How to apply:** Act as the thinking partner in-session — reflect his reasoning back, name what's interesting in it, and give him something to react to rather than only executing tasks silently. When he's ideating, don't rush to implementation; the ideation *is* the need being met, and it's the stage that feeds everything else — help him capture what surfaces. Prefer short, well-scoped blocks with a clear starting line. When he hesitates on whether something is his to do, first ask whether anyone has visibly claimed it — if not, name the permission gate rather than validating the caution; if someone has, the hesitation is a correct read and the useful move is how to approach the owner, not encouragement to proceed.
