 Course introduction
 Rehab running, or on-field rehabilitation, is the crucial bridge between gym-based
 rehab and a full return to sport. Its purpose goes beyond healing the injured
 structure. It prepares the athlete to meet the technical, tactical, and physical
 demands of competition. This stage typically begins with the athlete's first steps
 back into running and progresses through increasingly complex and demanding
 scenarios. A structured framework ensures that the athlete regains locomotive and
 physical qualities while minimizing the risk of flare-ups or re-injury.
 On-field rehab has two primary objectives:
 Fully rehabilitating the injured area
 Preparing the athlete to handle the realities of their sport
 It is not enough to restore function in isolation. The athlete must also be
 conditioned to execute technical and tactical tasks under game-like intensity.
Sportsmith
 Managing risk is central to this process. Training loads must be progressed
 carefully to avoid negative responses such as soreness, tightness, or recurrence of
 the original injury. Success depends on clarity. You must know what physical and
 locomotive activities the athlete's sport demands and how to progress their ability
 to perform them safely and specifically.
 Finding common ground across team sports
 One of the biggest challenges in designing an on-field rehab framework is the sheer
 variety of actions performed in sport. Footballers dribble and press, rugby players
 tackle and pass, and basketball players cut and pivot. Yet, despite these surface
 differences, invasion-based sports all share the same tactical patterns: teams attack
 by creating and invading space, and they defend by protecting or closing space.
 Technical actions also overlap, such as carrying or passing a ball, pressing an
 opponent, or recovering into position.
 At the foundation of these tactical and technical actions lie a handful of locomotive
 tasks that never change. Sprinting, accelerating, decelerating, and changing
 direction are the common denominators of performance across team sports. These
 four actions form the locomotive pillars of on-field rehab. Regardless of the sport,
 you can build progression frameworks around these pillars and then layer technical
 or tactical context on top. For example, an acceleration drill may be progressed into
 a pressing scenario, while deceleration can be integrated into a defensive recovery
 pattern.
 Three pillars of on-field rehab
 All sound on-field rehab frameworks can be built around these three major themes:
 Submaximal and maximal velocity running
 Athletes must be gradually exposed to increasing speeds, culminating in repeated
 exposures at sprint velocity before they are ready to compete
 Multi-directional speed and agility
 Here, the athlete rehearses accelerations, decelerations, and changes of direction
 under increasing load, eventually re-creating the movement patterns required in
 offensive and defensive scenarios
 Sport-specific skills
 Technical and tactical tasks, passing, pressing, contesting, carrying must be layered
 onto locomotive drills so the athlete can demonstrate physical and skill execution
 together in realistic game contexts.
Sportsmith
 Putting the framework into practice
 The first step in applying these principles is to audit the sport in question. Break it
 down into technical, tactical, and physical domains, then identify the overlaps and
 commonalities. From there, anchor your framework on the four locomotive pillars;
 sprinting, acceleration, deceleration, and change of direction. Progression is about
 gradually increasing both the intensity and the complexity of tasks. Start with
 submaximal efforts and controlled environments, then move toward maximal
 outputs in chaotic, game-like contexts. Incorporate offensive and defensive
 scenarios so that physical tasks are performed for tactical purposes, such as creating
 space in attack or closing space in defense. Throughout the process, closely
 monitor the athlete's response. Signs of overload which are tightness, fatigue, or
 symptom recurrence should trigger an adjustment to the plan. Finally, use the
 athlete's competitive benchmarks as your guide. Re-exposure to sprint distance,
 high-speed running volumes, and repeated accelerations should all be achieved
 before the athlete is cleared for full return.
 Rehab running is far more than simply getting an athlete back to jogging. It is a
 structured, progressive, and context-driven process that reconnects the athlete with
 the locomotive, technical, and tactical demands of their sport. By grounding your
 framework in universal pillars, progressing tasks from simple to complex, and
 ensuring benchmarks are met, you can safely and effectively guide athletes from the
 gym back onto the field with confidence.

 On-field rehab
 frameworks
 A framework is the backbone of
 on-field rehab. It provides structure,
 consistency, and efficiency, but it is not a rigid protocol. Instead, it acts as a guide that
 balances evidence with practitioner experience, allowing you to adapt to the needs of
 the individual athlete and the context of their injury. A good framework helps you
 manage risk, align rehab with the actual demands of sport, and make decisions quickly
 and confidently.
Sportsmith
 Why frameworks matter
 There are four key benefits to using a framework in on-field rehab. First, it allows
 you to take calculated risks. Rehab is never risk-free, but a framework acts like
 guardrails, ensuring you avoid progressing too quickly and causing a recurrence.
 Second, when a framework mirrors the performance requirements of sport, it
 enhances preparedness for return to play. Third, frameworks build in flexibility.
 They are not step-by-step manuals but adaptable guides that can be individualized
 for each athlete. And finally, they promote efficiency and consistency. With a
 structured approach, you can build programs faster, prescribe exercises with
 confidence, and make better decisions on the fly, all of which increase athlete trust
 in the process.
 Levels of on-field rehab
 On-field rehab can be split into levels that move progressively from controlled,
 low-intensity work to chaotic, high-intensity, game-like situations. These levels
 typically sit within the mid-to-late stages of rehab:
 Early stage: Gym-based work before progressing to the field.
 Mid-stage: Often broken into on-field rehab levels 1, 2, and 3.
 Late stage: Levels 4 and 5, leading into team training and return to play.
 For example, in the hamstring case study, the athlete completed one session at
 each level because of the injury's mild nature. With more severe injuries, multiple
 sessions at each level may be required. The athlete then transitioned into team
 training sessions before being cleared for competition.
 This level-based structure scaffolds the athlete's gradual exposure to the
 locomotive actions of their sport, ensuring intensity and complexity build
 systematically.
 Progressing intensity and complexity
 Two objectives drive the progression process: intensity and complexity.
 Intensity begins with competency. Athletes are reintroduced to locomotive
 actions, sprinting, accelerating, decelerating, changing direction, and
 submaximal running, at low to moderate speeds. Over time, the focus shifts to
 capacity, where the athlete must tolerate higher intensities and volumes until
 they can handle maximum velocity and repeated exposures.
 Complexity is best understood through the control-to-chaos continuum
 popularized by Matt Tabiner. Early sessions are highly controlled and low-risk.
 As athletes progress, the environment becomes less predictable, moving toward
 "chaos", highly reactive, game-like situations. Complexity is increased by
 manipulating variables such as space, opposition, and task constraints, creating
 more realistic environments for decision-making and movement execution.
Sportsmith
 A common misconception is that each new level automatically means more chaos
 across all drills. In reality, progression is gradual. Some actions may still require
 controlled environments at higher intensities, while others are ready to be tested
 under chaotic, game-like conditions. Intensity and complexity must be treated as
 separate but equally important goals.
 Control to chaos in practice
 The continuum is not simply about intensity or randomness, it is about preparing
 athletes to act effectively under the same uncertainty they will face in competition.
 At the controlled end, you structure the environment tightly, focusing on safe
 reintroduction of movement. At the chaotic end, drills replicate unpredictable
 scenarios where athletes must choose and execute the right action under pressure.
 This progression demands careful design. Using constraint-based learning
 principles, you can manipulate the task, the environment, or the individual's role to
 adjust the level of complexity. Over time, the ratio shifts: more work is performed
 in chaotic settings while less remains in tightly controlled conditions.
 End-stage rehab: competitive demands
 At the end of the process, athletes must be able to combine high intensity with high
 complexity. This is not just about physical readiness but also about confidence and
 decision-making. An athlete should be willing to sprint into space, accelerate to
 chase down an opponent, or react decisively in a chaotic small-sided game. If they
 hesitate in these competitive situations, they are not ready to return.
 In other words, the final stage of rehab is not optional chaos, it is essential.
 Exposing athletes to competitive, high-risk, and high-intensity scenarios is the only
 way to ensure they are prepared for the realities of their sport.
 Conclusion
 Frameworks bring order to the messy reality of on-field rehab. By breaking the
 process into progressive levels and systematically increasing both intensity and
 complexity, you create a safe and effective pathway back to competition. The
 ultimate goal is simple: an athlete who is physically prepared, mentally confident,
 and capable of executing high-intensity actions in chaotic, competitive
 environments. Anything less leaves them underprepared for the demands of sport.

 Linear submaximal &
 maximal running
 progressions
 The first major pillar of on-field rehab
 is the progression from submaximal to
 maximal running velocity. This pillar begins with preparing the athlete to return to
 running and continues through jogging, running, striding, and ultimately sprinting.
 Each step involves increasing intensity and mechanical demand, so a structured and
 careful progression is essential.
Sportsmith
 Understanding running velocities
 Running speed categories can be broken down into clear ranges:
 Walking: <6 km/h, less than 25% of max velocity.
 Sprinting: >27 km/h, above 85% of max velocity.
 As speed increases, vertical ground reaction forces grow larger, contact times get
 shorter, and muscular power demands rise--especially at the hip, knee, and ankle.
 This has implications for injury-specific rehab: ankle-dominant strategies are more
 relevant at lower speeds, while hip-dominant mechanics dominate as athletes
 approach striding and sprinting.
 Preparation for running
 Before progressing into steady-state running, athletes should demonstrate
 competence in drills that reintroduce upright running mechanics and repetitive
 impact cycles. These include:
 Dribble walks and runs (over ankle, shin, and knee height) to reinforce front-
 side mechanics.
 Skipping drills (e.g., A-skips) to add stiffness and reactivity.
 Low-intensity jumping such as pogos and unilateral hops for repetitive loading.
 Scissor shuffles and bounds to target aggressive hip flexion and extension.
 These preparatory drills serve both as warm-ups and readiness assessments,
 ensuring athletes can tolerate dynamic impact and postural demands before
 progressing.
 Progressing running speed
 The transition from jogging to sprinting is managed across the on-field rehab
 levels:
 Level 5: >95% up to 100%.
 Early sessions
 Structured progressions often use fixed distances (e.g., 60 m) with set completion
 times. For example:
 Reps gradually increase in speed across the session, building confidence and
 tolerance.
Sportsmith
 This method allows you to prescribe exact speeds, control intensity, and give
 athletes immediate feedback.
 Mid-stage sessions
 structures, such as broken strides:
 20 m acceleration 20 m speed maintenance 20 m deceleration.
 Completion times are set for the middle phase to ensure target velocities are
 reached.
 This balances controlled exposure with athlete feedback, using recovery walks and
 ongoing communication to manage comfort and confidence.
 Criteria for progression
 than simple tolerance. Objective criteria help guide decision-making:
 Biological healing time appropriate for the injury.
 Completion of previous running sessions with minimal pain or awareness.
 Force production benchmarks, such as:
 Nordics).
 Reactive strength assessments, such as hopping tasks, to ensure readiness for
 fast stretch-shortening cycle demands.
 Meeting these benchmarks provides confidence before exposing the athlete to high-
 velocity, high-risk tasks.
 Late-stage progression
 In the later stages, running speed is advanced using a combination of:
 Undulating approaches: Once above 85%, alternating sessions between higher
 and lower intensities to manage load and risk.
 of max before tapering slightly, while more complex injuries may require holding
 speeds or cycling intensity over several sessions.
 Crucially, athletes should achieve at least one, but ideally two, exposures above
 90% of max velocity before being cleared for training or competition.
Sportsmith
 Conclusion
 The progression from submaximal to maximal velocity running is a primary
 concern in on-field rehab. Each increase in speed brings greater intensity and
 mechanical demand, so athletes must be systematically prepared through
 preparatory drills, structured progressions, and objective readiness criteria. By
 combining linear and undulating approaches, and ensuring exposures above 90%
 are achieved, you can return athletes to competition with both confidence and
 resilience.

 Multidirectional
 speed & agility
 progressions
 Multi-directional speed and agility is a
 broad umbrella term that describes an
 athlete's ability to accelerate, decelerate, change direction, and maintain speed in
 multiple movement patterns. Unlike agility in its narrow sense, which is often defined
 by cognitive and perceptual elements, multi-directional speed incorporates both the
 physical and decision-making aspects needed in sport. It is central to on-field rehab
 because these high-intensity locomotive actions occur constantly in invasion-based
 games.
Sportsmith
 Breaking Down Multi-Directional Speed
 Multi-directional speed can be separated into three key components:
 Acceleration (short, explosive starts).
 Deceleration (rapid braking under load).
 Change of direction (angles ranging from shallow cuts to 180-degree turns).
 All three involve high rates of velocity change, which demand large ground reaction
 forces, rapid eccentric braking, and robust technical execution. This makes them
 both essential to performance and high risk if progressed poorly.
 What influences intensity?
 The intensity of multi-directional speed actions can be manipulated through
 several key variables:
 higher demands.
 direction loads.
 than shallow ones.
 time raises joint stress.
 intensity.
 competitive scenarios.
 These factors rarely operate independently. For example, higher approach speeds
 also raise the eccentric braking demand of a cut, while reducing space naturally
 lowers approach velocity. Thinking about them in combination helps you design
 drills that match both the athlete's current capacity and the desired rehab outcome.
 Linking intensity and complexity
 Just as with straight-line running, two main objectives guide progression in multi-
 directional work:
 Drills can be plotted on a matrix of intensity (vertical axis) and complexity
 (horizontal axis). Early rehab drills sit low on both, while later-stage drills push
 into high intensity and high complexity. This interplay aligns with the broader on-
 field rehab process: athletes must redevelop locomotive actions and then
 demonstrate them in chaotic environments.
Sportsmith
 Practical drill progressions
 Using the hamstring case study as a reference, here's how drills might be
 introduced across on-field rehab levels:
 Curvilinear accelerations at submaximal speeds.
 Go-and-stop accelerations into controlled decelerations.
 Introductory lateral movement tasks (e.g., diamond drill, lateral shuffles).
 Level 3: Increasing velocity, introducing closed reactivity
 Defensive mirror drills in small squares, where the defender reacts to the
 attacker's movement.
 Level 4: High intensity, more open reactivity
 Partner chase-and-tag drills.
 Large-space compass drills combining cutting with reactive ball pickups.
 Stop-and-chase tasks that integrate turnovers and rapid deceleration.
 Level 5: Highly chaotic, near-maximal intensities
 Competitive, sport-specific scenarios using multiple athletes.
 Drills designed with constraints to replicate game unpredictability.
 Integration into small-sided games or positional training before full return.
 Building an exercise menu
 library of exercises that match each stage of on-field rehab. For example,
 curvilinear sprints may begin as low-intensity, controlled drills, but when
 combined with a ball or reactive stimulus, they shift to higher complexity. This
 approach allows you to deliberately design progressions rather than relying on
 intuition alone.
 Conclusion
 Multi-directional speed and agility is about much more than isolated cutting drills.
 It is the structured redevelopment of acceleration, deceleration, and change-of-
 direction ability across both intensity and complexity continuums. By manipulating
 variables such as entry speed, angle, space, and reactivity, you can create drill
 progressions that take athletes from controlled, low-risk movements to chaotic,
 sport-specific tasks at maximum intensity. Only when athletes can execute these
 actions confidently in unpredictable environments should they be considered ready
 for full return.

 Part 5
 Sport specific skill
 progression
 The final pillar of on-field rehab is
 sport-specific skill integration. It is not
 enough for an athlete to accelerate, decelerate, or change direction in isolation. They
 must also demonstrate the ability to perform these locomotive actions while executing
 the technical and tactical skills of their sport. This progression ensures that when they
 return to competition, they are ready for the dual challenge of physical performance
 and decision-making under realistic game conditions.
Sportsmith
 Offensive and defensive skills
 When designing sport-specific drills, it helps to revisit the fundamental tactical and
 technical demands common across invasion sports:
 Offense: about creating space.
 Tactical actions: pressing forward, overlapping, running into space.
 Technical actions: moving with the ball, passing, kicking or throwing,
 catching or receiving.
 Defense: about closing space.
 Tactical actions: covering, retreating, closing down.
 Technical actions: tackling, intercepting, contesting possession.
 Sport-specific rehab drills must gradually reintroduce these actions, first in
 controlled settings, then under increasingly variable and competitive conditions.
 Objectives of sport-specific drills
 The overarching aims are twofold:
 1.Develop competency and capacity in executing technical skills and movement
 patterns.
 2.Expose athletes to progressively variable, unpredictable environments that
 mirror training and competition.
 In practice, this means designing drills that increase both intensity and complexity,
 leaning on the same continuums used in multi-directional speed work.
 Scaling intensity and complexity
 Several new continuums guide how sport-specific drills progress through rehab
 levels:
 Drill type: from stationary technical skills moving technical skills
 positional drills fatigued positional drills open competitive play.
 Practice structure: from blocked (repetitive, predictable) serial (sequenced,
 known order) random(unpredictable, game-like).
 Environmental demands: adding players, coaches, or constraints to create
 pressure, reactivity, and unpredictability.
 This structured progression ensures athletes move from tightly controlled
 environments in early rehab to chaotic, competitive environments in the later
 stages.
Sportsmith
 Practical progressions by rehab level
 Using the hamstring case study as an example:
 Level 1:
 Stationary technical skills only.
 No use of the injured limb for kicking or striking.
 Low intensity, block practice structure.
 Level 2:
 Introduce injured limb for skills such as kicking.
 Begin moving technical skills, but still pre-planned and controlled.
 Low intensity, block practice.
 Level 3:
 Positional drills introduced, with predictable sequences.
 Moderate intensity but still limited opportunity for sprinting or chaotic
 scenarios.
 Mix of block and serial practice.
 Level 4:
 Random and unpredictable drills introduced.
 Pressure added from additional players or staff.
 Fatigue incorporated, testing the athlete's ability to maintain skill under
 stress.
 Greater crossover with multi-directional speed drills.
 Level 5:
 Fully evasive and competitive situations, ideally within modified training
 games.
 High complexity and high intensity, requiring athletes to combine
 technical, tactical, and locomotive actions at near-maximal levels.
 Examples: 2v1 or 2v2 competitive games, chase-and-evade scenarios with
 constraints.
 Examples of drills
 velocity skill work.
 Mid rehab (Level 3): Positional skill sequences, pre-planned cutting or passing
 drills.
 2v1 attacking squares (progressing from small medium large spaces).
 Small-sided games with defensive pressure.
 End-to-end evasion drills where attackers attempt to score while defenders
 contest.
 These drills can be manipulated by space, defensive intensity, and involvement of
 additional players to ensure progressive exposure.
Sportsmith
 Transition to training and competition
 The end goal is not just technical execution but confidence in executing those skills
 under realistic, chaotic conditions. By Level 5, athletes should be performing
 sport-specific actions at high intensity in competitive environments. The final
 transition to training involves further exposure to these demands in team settings
 before clearance for competition. For a low-grade hamstring injury, two additional
 sessions within team training may be sufficient. For more severe injuries, extended
 exposure is often required.
 Conclusion
 Sport-specific skills are the final bridge in on-field rehab. They ensure athletes can
 execute technical and tactical demands while performing locomotive actions under
 pressure. By systematically progressing drill type, practice structure, and
 environmental complexity, you create a pathway that takes athletes from
 controlled, stationary skill work to chaotic, competitive play. Only then can you be
 confident they are ready to return to their sport.

 Pulling it all
 together
 This course has explored the principles
 of rehab running and on-field
 rehabilitation for team sports. Across seven modules, we examined the key locomotive
 demands of invasion-based sports, the stages of on-field rehab, and the three major
 pillars:
 Submaximal and maximal velocity running.
 Multi-directional speed and agility.
 Sport-specific skills.
Sportsmith
 Each pillar was unpacked with clear strategies for progressing both intensity and
 complexity, supported by real-world case studies. The ultimate goal has been to
 help you build systematic, adaptable, and context-specific frameworks for guiding
 athletes safely back to competition.
 The core message
 No action in sport happens in isolation. Every sprint, deceleration, or cut occurs
 while the athlete is also making technical and tactical decisions. This means on-
 field rehab must integrate locomotive actions with game-relevant contexts. To do
 this effectively, practitioners need confidence that athletes have been exposed to
 both the demands of the actions themselves and the unpredictability of the
 environments in which they occur.
 Building your own framework
 If you want to design your own on-field rehab framework, four practical steps can
 guide you:
 1.Split your on-field rehab into levels.
 The number of levels (three, five, seven) is less important than having a
 clear structure that takes athletes from controlled reintroduction to chaotic,
 game-like environments.
 2.Identify your pillars.
 In this course, we used three: velocity running, multi-directional speed and
 agility, and sport-specific skills.
 You can adapt or expand these depending on your context and
 environment.
 3.Decide on your intensity factors.
 Choose the variables that will allow you to scale difficulty.
 Examples include running speed, change-of-direction angle, entry velocity,
 available space, reactivity demands, and practice structure (blocked
 serial random).
 4.Map your exercises and drills.
 Fit them into your chosen levels, pillars, and intensity factors.
 For example, early levels may use block practice and stationary drills, while
 later levels emphasize chaotic environments, positional skills, and
 competitive play.
 By following these four steps, you create a framework that is systematic yet flexible
 enough to adapt to different injuries, contexts, and athlete needs.
 A final perspective
 There is no single "right" or "wrong" way to deliver on-field rehab. The principles
 in this course are based on scientific evidence and years of applied practice, but
Sportsmith
 they represent just one perspective. Your role as a practitioner is to adapt these
 principles to your environment, your athletes, and the demands of your sport.
 If you remember one thing, let it be this: the two equally important objectives of
 on-field rehab are to progress the intensity of locomotive actions and to increase
 the complexity of the environments in which they are performed. A structured
 approach to these objectives will put you more than halfway toward a successful
 return-to-play outcome.
 Conclusion
 On-field rehab is about more than returning athletes to running. It is about
 preparing them to compete confidently and effectively in the unpredictable, high-
 intensity environments of sport. By using levels, pillars, intensity factors, and
 carefully chosen drills, you can create a framework that balances risk, readiness,
 and realism, ensuring athletes not only return but thrive on their return to play.
