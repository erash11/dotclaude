######## framework--encoding-problem-shared-case-framework.md
# The Encoding Problem: Same Athlete, Different Lens

**What it is** — Weber's observation that different professional groups reviewing the same athlete and the same data routinely see different problems, not because they disagree about the facts, but because those facts are "encoded" differently by discipline: a physiotherapist sees tissue tolerance, healing, and rehabilitation progress; a medical practitioner sees health status and risk; a strength and conditioning coach sees readiness, output, and return-to-performance demands; a sports scientist sees load, exposure, and the numerical pathway to competition; a coach sees availability (can the athlete train, can the athlete play) (Module_6B_Part_A PDF p.1). His proposed fix is a written, shared case framework: document how a given scenario (his example: a hamstring injury) will be assessed, the checkpoints, expected timing, and who acts at each handoff, built collaboratively with medical, physiotherapy, strength, and sport science staff so each group also learns what the others do (Video Lab 6B summary, 02:44–06:37; PDF p.1).

**Why it works** — Weber's mechanism: when staff understand what other disciplines are doing, "departments become less defensive, less territorial, and more collaborative... staff are more willing to share information, raise concerns early, and adjust without fear or confusion" (PDF p.1). He is explicit this is not about eliminating disciplinary difference — healthy technical disagreement should be "respected and examined" — but about ensuring differences operate inside a shared structure rather than each discipline silently running its own model (PDF p.2).

**When to apply** — Any interdisciplinary case (injury, load management, return-to-play) where different departments have historically produced different read-outs of the same athlete without a documented shared plan. **Eric's baseline:** Applied Performance's stated mission is to enable the high-performance team to answer questions, ask better questions, and discover new ones, operating inside Baylor Athletics Health & Wellness alongside, not above or below, Athletics Performance, Athletic Medicine, Performance Nutrition, and Mental Health Services [method-baseline.md, Department model]. **Inference:** this framework is a concrete method (a written pathway, checkpoints, and criteria per case type) for making that enabling role operational rather than aspirational.

**When NOT to apply** — Weber distinguishes healthy technical challenge from a staff member working outside the agreed process; the framework is meant to protect the former and surface the latter, not to suppress disagreement (PDF p.2). His own anecdote — presenting injury-risk evidence that was new and unfamiliar to a coach and doctor who lacked a shared framework for understanding it, followed by the predicted injuries occurring — is a personal account illustrating a communication failure, not evidence that the specific injury-risk claim itself was correct (PDF p.2).

**Example** — Weber's own summary of the stakes: "correct data is not enough. If the leader cannot get the information understood across disciplines, the work has failed at the level that matters most" (PDF p.2).

######## framework--evidence-stacking-before-acting.md
# Evidence Stacking: Don't Decide on One Dramatic Signal

**What it is** — Weber's central question for any consequential decision, per the lesson summary: does staff have enough independent, relevant information to act (Podcast 2 summary, 00:00–02:20). A dramatic single result or first impression should prompt further investigation, not settle the decision. An evidence stack combines the event/problem history, clinical findings where relevant, the athlete's own prior results, movement and capacity checks, and performance under the conditions the decision actually concerns (00:00–02:20). His practical takeaway: define the decision first, identify the important unanswered questions, then choose measures that each add distinct evidence rather than repeating what is already known (17:35–18:15). This is the same caution Weber repeats in narrower form elsewhere in the course: in Lab 2A, one test returning to baseline is treated as one data point rather than a stand-alone clearance (Lab 2A summary, 03:20–05:02); in Webinar 1 Part III, one favorable test result is not a complete return-to-running answer (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 07:44–08:54); and in Podcast 1, he warns specifically against treating a single red dashboard indicator as automatic grounds to remove an athlete (Podcast 1 summary, 03:38–04:24).

**Why it works** — Weber's own stated purpose, per the lesson summary, is to separate meaningful change from noise and to communicate decisions with appropriate confidence (Podcast 2 summary, 00:00–02:20). **Inference:** the likely reason combining sources helps is that different evidence types fail in different, largely independent ways: a single clinical impression, a single hop-test result, and a single speed-exposure metric can each individually mislead, but they are unlikely to all mislead in the same direction at once. Updating the working assessment as each new piece arrives, illustrated with Weber's soccer-knee and hamstring anecdotes (02:20–17:35), is more robust than treating any one piece as decisive.

**When to apply** — Any return-to-play, load, or selection decision where one metric alone is being used to justify the call, especially under time pressure or when a result is unusually dramatic (very good or very bad). **Eric's baseline:** the course transcripts in Eric's own corpus argue "you don't need a fancy test or anything to do that. But you do have to have a good coach's eye," while Eric's own SportSmith writing moved the opposite direction on daily monitoring by replacing subjective wellness surveys with objective mRSI [method-baseline.md Tensions #3]. **Inference:** evidence stacking offers a synthesis rather than forcing a choice between those two positions: the coach's eye is one more independent stream to stack alongside the objective metric, not a competitor to it.

**When NOT to apply** — Weber is explicit that his anecdotes illustrate a reasoning approach; they do not establish that a triple-hop baseline can diagnose a knee injury or that any specific stack of tests can clear an athlete to play, and medical diagnosis and clearance remain clinical decisions outside this framework's scope (Podcast 2 summary, 18:15 caveat). It also does not specify how many independent streams are enough for a given decision; that judgment is left to the practitioner.

**Example** — Weber's hamstring case: clinical progress and gym measures were supplemented with hopping, running-speed exposure, and a comparison of running mechanics at matched speeds, specifically to close the gap between controlled tests and actual field demands before returning the athlete to competition (Podcast 2 summary, 10:30–17:35). The reported outcome is one case, not a validated return-to-play rule.

######## framework--grey-zone-overlap-ownership.md
# The Grey Zone: Naming Ownership Where Medical and Performance Overlap

**What it is** — Weber's term for the overlap between medical/physiotherapy staff and strength/performance staff, where differences in training, priorities, timelines, and risk tolerance become a problem specifically when the overlap has no agreed operating rules (Video Lab 6E summary, 00:00–02:44). His proposed governance: agree the phases, decision points, measures, and criteria the department will use; assign a named operational owner for each phase, define what that person can change independently, and state which decisions require escalation and who must be informed, and how quickly, when a plan changes (02:44–10:43). One example division he describes: medical leadership early in rehabilitation, strength staff taking operational responsibility for return-to-running, with both continuing to contribute — but he is explicit this division should shift when the actual skills and collaboration willingness of the specific practitioners warrant it, since a job title alone, per the lesson summary, does not establish who is best equipped for a specific task (03:59–07:11).

**Why it works** — Weber's own stated aim is to make responsibilities and decisions explicit while preserving each discipline's perspective (Video Lab 6E summary, 00:00–02:44). **Inference:** without a named owner per phase, both disciplines can reasonably believe the other is responsible, or both can independently act on the same decision point with different criteria, the same underlying logic as the strategy/tactics model (see [[framework--strategy-as-highway-tactics-as-lanes]]). His team exercise makes the mismatch tangible: walk through one rehabilitation case twice, once from the physiotherapist's priorities and constraints and once from the strength coach's, then compare the two accounts at each handoff and agree ownership, progression criteria, information to transfer, change authority, and notification rules (10:43–13:02).

**When to apply** — Any return-to-play pathway where Athletic Medicine and Applied Performance/Athletics Performance both have a legitimate stake in the same phase. **Eric's baseline:** Applied Performance's role in return-to-performance is described as advisory, supplying information that supports Athletics Performance's and Athletic Medicine's decisions, and no specific RTP criteria, battery, or phased-return protocol appears anywhere in the reviewed corpus [method-baseline.md, Rehab and return to play]. **Inference:** this framework's phase-by-phase ownership map is a direct, concrete answer to that named gap.

**When NOT to apply** — Weber's phase-division example (medical early, strength for return-to-running) is his own illustration, not a validated or universal division of labor; he states explicitly it should change based on who the actual practitioners are and how they work together, not be applied as a fixed template. This framework also does not resolve clinical criteria for a specific injury type — it governs who owns which decision, not what the decision's medical threshold should be.

**Example** — Weber's escalation rule: name an operational owner for each phase, state what that person can change independently, and specify which decisions must be escalated, plus who must be informed and how quickly when the athlete's plan changes (07:11–10:43).

######## framework--integration-problem-five-break-points.md
# The Integration Problem: Five Break Points Between Data and Decision

**What it is** — Weber's framework for a specific failure mode: a practitioner collects genuinely useful information, but it never influences a decision because the organization has no reliable way to interpret, discuss, or act on it. "Good information does not guarantee influence... this is usually not a knowledge problem" (The_Leader_s_Integration_Cheat_Sheet.pdf p.1). His five break points, tracing a signal from collection to action: **(1) language gap** — teams use different terms or underlying models for athlete status, readiness, risk, or rehabilitation; **(2) decision pathway gap** — no agreed route identifies who collects, reviews, discusses, and uses technical information, or what triggers a conversation; **(3) authority gap** — a senior decision-maker can override a technical recommendation without discussion or explanation (Weber favors hearing competing cases and explaining the decision, rather than a silent override); **(4) relationship deficit** — the person who must act does not yet understand or trust the evidence; per the lesson summary, Weber frames this as a shared responsibility, with practitioners sharing responsibility for preparing colleagues to interpret new information; **(5) process absence** — missing meeting gates, decision checkpoints, or review steps let a team skip important intermediate judgments (Video Lab 6A summary, 06:58–13:14; cheat sheet p.1). His diagnostic test: "if your recommendation changes as it moves up the chain, there is likely an integration leak" (cheat sheet p.1).

**Why it works** — Weber's core claim, stated directly, is that departments fail through structure, not intent: recommendations get ignored, diluted, or misunderstood because the environment lacks the pathway to handle them, not because the underlying analysis was wrong (cheat sheet p.1). **Inference:** naming five distinct break points turns a vague sense that good work isn't landing into a specific, auditable question, which of the five links actually failed.

**When to apply** — Reviewing a past decision where technically sound information did not change the outcome, or auditing a department's current pathway for a specific recurring signal (e.g., a fatigue flag, an asymmetry finding). Weber's own audit question directly names the exercise: "you need to ask whether your data has ever directly changed a major decision, and whether you can trace the path from collection to action" (cheat sheet p.1). **Eric's baseline:** KPI-threshold identification for diagnostic screening and performance testing is named as a departmental operating principle, but no specific RTP criteria, battery, or phased-return protocol appears anywhere in the reviewed corpus [method-baseline.md, Rehab and return to play]. **Inference:** Weber's audit question is a useful test to run against that specific gap.

**When NOT to apply** — Weber's own account of a past bone-stress-injury case, where he says he had not communicated his evidence in a form colleagues could use, is a personal anecdote illustrating the communication point; it does not establish a general injury-prediction rule from training surface, and the transcript cannot verify the causal claim (Video Lab 6A summary, 13:14–15:38). This framework also assumes the underlying information was sound in the first place; it diagnoses pathway failure, not measurement or inference failure (see [[framework--measurement-versus-inference]] for that separate question).

**Example** — Weber's authority-gap distinction: overriding a technical recommendation is "sometimes necessary, but it should happen transparently and with explanation" (cheat sheet p.1) — the failure mode is the silent override, not the override itself.

######## framework--kaigi-decision-meeting-structure.md
# The Kaigi Decision Meeting

**What it is** — Weber's structured meeting format for use when the question and realistic options are already clear and the team needs to decide, not explore. Elements: prepare before the meeting by defining the question and narrowing it to two or three realistic options, with relevant information available in advance so participants arrive informed; keep evidence precise, stating what was measured, what is known, and what remains unknown; time-box contributions so short speaking limits push people to the critical information, with supporting detail left in the pre-read; and leave a decision log recording the action, owner, due date, and review method (Kaigi-transcript summary, 00:00–05:56; see [[framework--decision-log-owner-action-date-review]]). Brainstorming, complex technical work, and staff development are explicitly carved out as needing a different, slower pace (05:56–07:27).

**Why it works** — Weber's own stated priority for this format is speed, clarity, and accountability specifically when the decision space is already narrow (Kaigi-transcript summary, 00:00–02:18). **Inference:** forcing preparation and concise contributions likely works because the hard thinking (narrowing the options, gathering the evidence) has already happened before the room convenes, so meeting time is spent deciding, not discovering.

**When to apply** — Recurring operational decisions with a known, bounded option set: Weber's own worked example is an AFL pre-training Go/No-Go review, where a leader names each player, responsible practitioners give concise observations, other staff add relevant information without repeated prompting, and the group combines evidence rather than changing training on one measure alone — aiming for under 30 seconds per player across roughly 50 players, with flagged players receiving assigned follow-up reported back within 20 minutes (Kaigi-transcript summary, 07:28–12:13). Weber also shows the format can be dropped into a stalled meeting mid-stream: switch explicitly into a 10-minute decision phase, state the decision, establish ownership, document the outcome, and move on (12:14–13:23).

**When NOT to apply** — Weber is explicit that a decision meeting has different requirements from sharing information, creating solutions, or educating staff (00:00–02:18); **Inference:** using kaigi structure to force a decision before the options are actually narrowed to two or three, or before enough evidence exists, would likely produce a fast but poorly grounded call. His own Go/No-Go numbers (roughly 15–17 staff, under 30 seconds per player, 20-minute follow-up) are his example's parameters, not validated operating thresholds or a recommended clinical protocol. See [[framework--meeting-typology-decide-diagnose-inform-build]] for the broader set of meeting modes; kaigi is specifically the **decide** mode and should not be reached for when the actual need is **diagnose** or **build**.

**Example** — Weber's stalled-meeting rescue: when a broader problem-solving discussion stalls, explicitly announce the switch to decision mode, give it a fixed short window (his example: 10 minutes), and require a stated decision, an owner, and a documented outcome before the group moves on (12:14–13:23).

######## framework--knowledge-behaviour-matrix.md
# The Knowledge x Behaviour Matrix

**What it is** — A 2x2 grid Weber uses to discuss staff capability separately from staff engagement. The **knowledge axis** (horizontal): **handbrake** (insufficient or poorly aligned knowledge for the setting), **pawn** (fundamentals without complex problem-solving), **practitioner** (strong across domains, able to contribute), **operator** (broad knowledge, independent complex problem-solving) (Webinar 1 Part II summary, 07:35–10:00). The **behaviour axis** (vertical): **misaligned**, **coasting** (contributes in a stable role without pushing change), **catalyst** (proactively influences the team even without leading change alone), and **beacon** at the top of the range (Webinar 1 Part II summary, 10:00–11:57; Lab 1B Part 2 summary, 00:00–01:07). Weber is explicit these are role-capability labels, meant to describe knowledge and behaviour rather than to judge a person's overall worth (Lab 1B Part 1 summary, 03:59–06:10). A companion student worksheet (published under Weber's SpeedSig brand) operationalizes the grid as a self-audit: define both axes in your own words, self-place on each axis now and for a 3–5 year target, then map 3–5 colleagues onto the same categories using initials or role titles only (Knowledge_Behaviour_Profile_Student_Workbook.pdf, Sections A–C). Note: the workbook's own behaviour-category list (Section A3) shows only three labels — Misaligned, Coaster, Catalyst — omitting "Beacon"; whether Beacon is a dropped top tier or a later course revision is not resolved in this corpus.

**Why it works** — Weber's stated purpose is developmental, not evaluative: leaders use the grid to spot skill gaps and plan continuing professional development, and staff use it to compare self-assessment against supervisor feedback (Lab 1B Part 2 summary, 02:10–04:04). **Inference:** splitting knowledge from behaviour also separates two different interventions, a low-knowledge/high-catalyst person likely needs training, while a high-knowledge/coasting person needs a different kind of push, consistent with Weber's own worked contrast (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 00:00–03:27).

**When to apply** — Structuring a 1:1 development conversation, a hiring discussion, or a mentoring assignment where the real question is whether a person needs more knowledge, more engagement, or both. Weber's advice to pick mentors with comparable sport-setting experience (Lab 1B Part 1 summary, 06:10–07:18) is a direct extension of the knowledge axis; see [[heuristic--choose-mentors-with-sport-specific-experience]].

**When NOT to apply** — Do not use this grid, or any output from the SpeedSig worksheet, to place a named Baylor colleague into a "handbrake" or "misaligned" category in any written record; Weber's own framing is explicit that the grid is meant to evaluate knowledge and behavior, not a person's overall worth (Lab 1B Part 1 summary, 03:59–06:10), and the course's own worksheet instructs anonymizing colleagues by role title for exactly this reason. It is also entirely self- or supervisor-report, with no validation evidence that placement on the grid predicts performance or that movement between quadrants is trainable on any particular timeline. Any real personnel decision built on it would still need direct evidence, fair process, and Baylor HR policy (Lab 1B Part 1 summary, 07:18–08:02).

**Example** — Weber's own worked contrast: someone with limited knowledge but strong initiative may be worth developing, while deep expertise paired with little engagement can hold a team's progress back (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 00:00–03:27).

**Related** — [[heuristic--make-development-visible-with-cpd-checkins]] and [[heuristic--choose-mentors-with-sport-specific-experience]] — the two concrete practices Weber pairs with this matrix.
######## framework--measurement-versus-inference.md
# Measurement vs. Inference

**What it is** — Weber's distinction between what a tool actually captures (a **measurement**: force, distance, speed, displacement) and the explanation applied to that output (an **inference**: fatigue, tissue status, readiness) (Lab 2A summary, 00:00–01:45). His examples, per the lesson summary: calling a lower countermovement-jump result neurological fatigue, or a GPS change tissue stress — neither label follows directly from the measurement alone, and athletes can produce a similar output through a different movement strategy, so a stable score can hide a real change in mechanics (01:45–03:20). His stated communication rule: report what was measured, identify what is being inferred, state your confidence in that inference, and recommend the next exposure or check (05:02–06:02).

**Why it works** — **Inference:** collapsing the distinction likely lets a causal story ride along silently with a number, so the story gets treated with the same confidence as the measurement itself. Naming the inference as an inference forces an explicit confidence statement instead of an implicit one, and makes the underlying reasoning available for someone else to challenge.

**When to apply** — Any report or conversation where a device output is being used to say something about the athlete's internal state (fatigue, readiness, tissue tolerance) rather than just the output itself. **Eric's baseline:** modified reactive strength index (mRSI) from daily CMJ testing was deliberately chosen as the daily athlete-readiness marker in place of subjective wellness questionnaires, because it is "objective, consistent, and actionable in a way the wellness surveys never quite were" [method-baseline.md, Force plate and neuromuscular monitoring]. **Inference:** this framework's discipline would ask the same question of mRSI that Weber asks of his CMJ example, what is actually measured (jump-derived force/time output), and what is being inferred from it (neuromuscular readiness), stated with what confidence.

**When NOT to apply** — This is a communication and reasoning discipline, not a claim that inference is unreliable or should be avoided; Weber's own examples (hamstring context: injury history, strength tests, jumping/hopping, speed exposure, running mechanics) show that combining several measurements to support one inference is the intended use, not a replacement for inference altogether (Lab 2A summary, 03:20–05:02). It also does not by itself tell you whether an observed change exceeds measurement noise — see [[framework--reliability-vs-sensitivity-mdc-and-swc]] for that separate question.

**Example** — Weber treats one test returning to baseline as one data point, not a stand-alone clearance, in his hamstring illustration (Lab 2A summary, 03:20–05:02) — the same discipline generalized into a full framework in [[framework--evidence-stacking-before-acting]].

**Related** — [[framework--evidence-stacking-before-acting]] — combining multiple inferences before acting on any one of them. [[principle--means-and-modality-before-technology]] — the upstream discipline of naming the question before choosing what to measure.
######## framework--meeting-typology-decide-diagnose-inform-build.md
# Name the Meeting Mode Before You Set the Agenda: Decide, Diagnose, Inform, Build

**What it is** — Weber's four meeting modes, each with a different structure and expected output. **Decide**: a clear question with limited options, using pre-reading, time-boxed input, an explicit decision, owner, due date, and review — the same structure as the kaigi format (Lab 3B summary, 01:03–02:14; see [[framework--kaigi-decision-meeting-structure]]). **Diagnose**: a known problem whose cause or solution is uncertain, gathering perspectives and hypotheses on a defined problem with time-boxed contributions (Weber's example: roughly 30 minutes), assigning a smaller group to gather more information if unresolved, then scheduling a later decision (02:14–04:03). **Inform**: sharing updates and context, with an explicit warning that inform meetings can drift into unplanned problem-solving if not held to that purpose (04:37–05:02). **Build**: co-creating or redesigning a system, structure, or training week, giving people room to contribute, considering psychological safety, and allowing time to digest ideas across possibly several meetings rather than forcing one immediate decision (05:34–06:18). Weber also names two composite formats: an **operations meeting** (brief status updates, blocker removal, ownership assignment, combining informing with quick decisions, 05:02–05:34) and a **retrospective/after-action review** (a factual, blameless timeline of what happened, using measures and named gaps, translated into procedure changes, 06:18–07:08). His practical rule: for each agenda item, write one verb — decide, diagnose, inform, or build — plus the question, expected output, time limit, and pre-reading (07:08–08:09).

**Why it works** — Weber's stated mechanism: naming the mode in advance tells participants what kind of contribution is needed and protects creative or diagnostic work from being cut short by premature decision pressure, while letting genuinely decidable questions move fast once the options are known. A single staff meeting can move through several modes in sequence, provided each segment states its own purpose and output so participants know which mode they are in at any given moment (04:03–04:37).

**When to apply** — Building or reviewing any recurring departmental meeting's agenda, or diagnosing why a specific meeting series feels unproductive (often because it is running **decide** structure on a **diagnose** problem, or vice versa). **Eric's baseline:** Eric's own course material describes an annual plan being adapted as circumstances change while still reaching the same destination [method-baseline.md, Periodization and programming]. **Inference:** the retrospective mode in this typology gives a named structure to that kind of after-the-fact review, which the baseline shows happening informally rather than through a documented format.

**When NOT to apply** — Weber gives no evidence that naming a mode improves meeting outcomes beyond his own stated experience; this is a proposed practice, not a tested intervention. It also does not resolve who decides which mode an agenda item needs; that judgment is left to whoever sets the agenda. A leader who uses a meeting to gather perspectives that test their own initial view before deciding (07:08–08:09) is deliberately using diagnose-mode dynamics inside what will eventually become a decide-mode outcome, a legitimate but easily-missed hybrid use.

**Example** — Weber's retrospective example, per the lesson summary: reviewing a run of injuries with a factual timeline and named missing information, kept blameless, then translated into a procedure change rather than a verdict on any individual (06:18–07:08).
