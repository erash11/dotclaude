# Case Study: Bondarchuk's Developmental/Competitive/Restorative Blocks and the 1988 Hammer Throw Podium

**What it is** — Issurin credits Anatoly Bondarchuk, hammer-throw coach, with one of the
precursor systems to Block Periodization (PDF p. 32, printed p. 21). Based on his own
athletic career (1972 Olympic hammer-throw champion) and doctoral research on throwers'
training programs (1988), Bondarchuk built a periodization chart using three types of
specialized mesocycle-blocks: **developmental** (workload gradually increases to maximum),
**competitive** (load stabilizes, athletes focus on competitive performance), and
**restorative** (active recovery, preparing for the next developmental block). Stated
durations: developmental and competitive blocks are "usually four weeks," restorative "can
be shortened to two weeks." Sequencing depends on the competition schedule and individual
athlete response. The claimed outcome: Bondarchuk coached the gold, silver, and bronze
medalists in hammer throw at the 1988 Olympics — "the entire Olympic podium occupied by
athletes of the same coach" (Bondarchuk, 1986 and 1988, as cited by Issurin).

**Why it works — the author gives no mechanism beyond the general BPC rationale** —
Issurin does not describe Bondarchuk's own stated reasoning for why these particular block
lengths (4/4/2 weeks) or this particular three-way split (by competitive readiness rather
than by ability type) were chosen; the mechanism offered is the same general BPC logic
(concentrated, sequenced blocks beat simultaneous multi-ability training) applied
retroactively to Bondarchuk's design.

**When to apply — untested, use as a historical precedent, not as evidence** — Useful for
explaining the lineage of block-periodization thinking (Bondarchuk predates and directly
inspired Issurin's own accumulation/transmutation/realization taxonomy, per PDF p. 32-33)
and as an alternative three-block naming scheme organized around competitive readiness
(developmental/competitive/restorative) rather than ability type
(accumulation/transmutation/realization) — worth distinguishing the two schemes explicitly
when citing "Bondarchuk's blocks" versus "Issurin's blocks."

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — This is a single coach's single-event outcome (n = 1
coaching program, one Olympic final) with no comparison group, no report of what other
finalists' training looked like, and total reliance on the coach's and Issurin's own
secondary account rather than an independent case audit. A three-medal sweep at one Olympics
is a striking result but cannot isolate the training design's contribution from athlete
talent, competitor variance on the day, or other coaching factors. Do not cite this as
evidence that a 4/4/2-week block cadence "works" for hammer throw or any other event; it
illustrates a design, not a validated dose-response finding. This is distinct from and
should not be merged with Issurin's own accumulation/transmutation/realization taxonomy — see
[[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]] for that separate, though
historically related, three-block system.

**Example** — A coach citing "block periodization has a track record" should specify
which system (Bondarchuk's competitive-readiness blocks or Issurin's ability-type blocks) and
should not present the 1988 podium sweep as generalizable evidence beyond "one documented
historical instance this concept coincided with a strong outcome."

**Related** — [[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]],
[[case-study--issurin-kaverin-canoe-kayak-block-periodization]] — a comparable single-coach
case from the same era; consider also cross-referencing
`fundamentals-of-sprinting--moyer/framework--sprint-exercise-classes-bondarchuk-four-categories.md`
(vault) — the *same* coach (Bondarchuk), but a completely different, unrelated framework
of his (an exercise-classification taxonomy by movement similarity, not a mesocycle-timing
system). The two should never be conflated when either is cited.

# Two Studies Issurin Cites Against Simultaneous Development: Swimmer Interference and Kayaker Decay

**What it is** — Issurin presents two data-backed examples as motivation for abandoning
simultaneous multi-ability training (traditional periodization) in favor of consecutive,
concentrated blocks.

**(1) Swimmer concurrent-training interference (PDF pp. 21-22, printed pp. 10-11, Figure
1.3).** High-level male swimmers were studied over an 8-week early-season program combining
extensive swimming with resistive/power dry-land work aimed at swim-specific strength
(9-11 workouts/week; sample size not stated in this text). Measured: maximal force of
tethered swimming (Ftsw), dry-land explosive strength (Fexp), and dry-land strength
endurance (SE). Result: strength endurance improved markedly; swim-specific strength did not
improve; explosive strength *decreased* — despite about 30% of dry-land program time devoted
to maximal/explosive strength work. Issurin's mechanistic explanation: extensive endurance
work captures the metabolic (anabolic) energy needed for the hypertrophy and neural-drive
adaptations that explosive/maximal strength require, and the resulting chronic fatigue keeps
central and peripheral neural factors from the state needed for strength gains
(citing Klausen, 1990, for the neural-mechanism point).

**(2) Kayaker decay under traditional periodization (PDF p. 25-26, printed pp. 14-15,
Figure 1.4 — the in-text citation reads "Fig.1.3," which appears to be a labeling error in
the source; the described variables match Figure 1.4's caption, not Figure 1.3's).** A group
of highly qualified kayakers were tracked across a full annual cycle built on the classical
model (Issurin et al., 1986). Measured: mean distance velocity (Vd), velocity at anaerobic
threshold (V-AT), peak paddle force (PF), stroke rate (SR), muscle mass (MM). Result: the
long general-preparatory period raised V-AT, muscle mass and peak force substantially; but
during the extended, highly specialized competitive period, extensive aerobic work and
maximum-strength work were reduced or dropped as "harmful for racing technique," and V-AT,
peak force, and muscle mass all *declined* by the competitive period. Distance velocity
still reached its season maximum, but only via increased stroke rate compensating for
reduced force — i.e., technique/rate covered for a real loss in the underlying strength and
aerobic qualities. Issurin's summary: "the timing pattern for developing different abilities
was far from optimal."

**Why it works** — For the swimmer case, Issurin's own stated mechanism is that extensive
endurance work "captures the metabolic energy that is necessary for anabolism during
post-exercise recovery," suppressing the muscle hypertrophy and neural-drive adaptations
strength training requires, and that chronic fatigue from high-volume training keeps
"central and peripheral neural factors... far from optimum" (PDF pp. 21-22, printed pp.
10-11, citing Klausen, 1990, for the neural-mechanism point). For the kayaker case, Issurin
gives no physiological mechanism for the decline beyond narration of what happened (reduced
aerobic and strength work during the competitive period, PDF p. 25, printed p. 14) — treat
that case as a described pattern, not an explained one. **Inference:** labeling the two
cases as "concurrent interference" versus "sequential decay" is this entry's own
categorization for cross-referencing purposes, not language Issurin himself uses; both are
offered by Issurin as evidence for the same underlying argument against simultaneous,
non-block training (see [[principle--concentration-of-training-workloads]]), which in turn
motivates the consecutive block sequencing in
[[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]] and
[[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]].

**When to apply** — The swimmer study is the stronger evidence of the two: it is a
controlled, measured 8-week study with named outcome variables, giving concrete numbers
useful for illustrating concurrent-training interference to Baylor strength staff
programming for athletes who need both an aerobic base and explosive/max strength (e.g.,
many football positions in a fall-camp conditioning window). The kayaker case is useful
mainly as a cautionary illustration of "building a quality early in the year and then
letting it decay unaddressed."

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — The swimmer study's exact sample size is not given in
this text (only workout frequency, 9-11/week), so its statistical strength cannot be judged
from this source alone — treat the direction of the finding (interference occurred) as
`supported` but the specific numeric changes in Figure 1.3 as unverifiable without the
original 1986/whichever-year source Issurin drew Figure 1.3 from (not cited by name on this
page). **Untested:** the kayaker case is Issurin's own team's data (Issurin et al., 1986),
observational across one season with no control group — it demonstrates a plausible pattern,
not a controlled comparison against an alternative periodization model run on the same
athletes. Neither study is football-specific; football's in-season structure (weekly
competition, not one late-season peak) differs from both the swimmers' fixed 8-week block and
the kayakers' single annual peak design.

**Example** — A Baylor position group with both an in-season conditioning demand and a
strength-maintenance demand should treat "just add more of both" as the swimmer study's
failure mode: without deliberate sequencing or volume management, the concurrent aerobic
load can blunt the intended strength/power adaptation even when strength work is
programmed.

**Related** — [[principle--concentration-of-training-workloads]] (Table 1.4's rationale,
which these two studies are offered as concrete illustrations of),
[[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]].

# Case Study: The USSR Canoe-Kayak Team and the Original Accumulation/Transmutation/Realization Design

**What it is** — Issurin's own account of where his accumulation-transmutation-realization
taxonomy came from (PDF p. 33, printed p. 22): in the early 1980s, elite USSR canoe-kayak
paddlers were training an "enormous exercise volume" that the author and colleagues judged
excessive. The block-and-mesocycle-sequencing idea was "conceptualized, implemented and
proved in practice" with the national team, then published (Issurin & Kaverin, 1985). The
modified design allowed "about a 10-15% reduction in the annual training volume." Follow-up
testing during preparation reportedly showed "considerable improvement in the main fitness
components in all subgroups." The claimed competitive outcomes: three gold and three silver
medals at the 1988 Seoul Olympics, and eight gold medals (1989) and nine gold medals (1990)
at the World Championships.

**Why it works — the author's own interpretation, not independently verified here** —
Issurin attributes the improvement to reduced simultaneous multi-ability training and better
volume management under the new block structure, consistent with the general BPC rationale
in [[principle--concentration-of-training-workloads]]. No comparison data (e.g., what the
same team's results looked like in prior Olympic/World Championship cycles under the old
model, or what other nations' medal counts did over the same period) is presented in this
chapter to isolate the training-design change from other factors (athlete development,
equipment, competitor turnover).

**When to apply — untested, treat as the concept's origin story, not as proof** — Useful
as the direct provenance of Issurin's own taxonomy (see
[[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]]) and as a data point that a
national federation with centralized, well-resourced athlete monitoring implemented this
design at scale (not just with one athlete).

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — This is the concept's inventor reporting on his own
uncontrolled intervention with his own national team, published by the same author (Issurin
& Kaverin, 1985) — the account here (2008 book) is a secondary retelling with no cited
independent replication or outcome audit. As with the Bondarchuk case, do not treat the
Olympic and World Championship medal counts as a controlled test of the training design;
1980s Soviet sport had structural advantages (centralized athlete development, resourcing)
that are not present in most other training contexts, including a US college football
program. The "10-15% volume reduction" figure has no comparison baseline stated (reduction
from what specific prior volume, over what specific prior period) beyond "the annual
training volume."

**Example** — Citing "block periodization produced Olympic medals" should specify this is
one 1980s Soviet national-team case reported by the method's own originator, not a
controlled trial, and should be paired with the caveat already logged in
[[case-study--bondarchuk-hammer-throw-block-mesocycles]] about single-coach, single-program
evidence.

**Related** — [[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]],
[[case-study--bondarchuk-hammer-throw-block-mesocycles]].

# Squat-First vs. Squat-After-Hang-Pull Changes Whether You Get More Reps or More Power

**What it is** — Spreuwenberg et al. (2006), cited by Issurin (PDF p. 73, printed p. 62):
**nine** resistance-trained male athletes performed the back squat (4 sets at 85% of 1RM) on
two separate days under two protocols. Protocol A: squat performed during the initial part
of the workout. Protocol B: squat performed after a whole-body resistance exercise (hang
pull). Results: Protocol A produced significantly more repetitions per set (8.0 ± 1.9 vs.
5.4 ± 2.7 in Protocol B), but average power per set was *higher* in Protocol B. Issurin
attributes the power difference to postactivation potentiation induced by the preceding
whole-body power exercise.

**Why it works** — Issurin reports only the study authors' own attribution for the power
finding: they "attributed this phenomenon to postactivation potentiation induced by the
preceding whole-body power exercise" (PDF p. 73, printed p. 62). **Inference:** the
companion explanation for the rep-count finding, that fresh (unfatigued) muscle sustains
more total repetitions at a given relative load than muscle that has just performed a
preceding whole-body lift, is this entry's own reasoning to account for the Protocol A
result; Issurin does not state that explanation for the rep-count difference in this
passage.

**When to apply** — Choosing exercise order based on the specific training goal for that
session: Issurin's stated takeaway (PDF p. 73, printed p. 62) is that a coach wanting maximum
hypertrophy stimulus should place the squat early in the session (more total volume/reps),
while a coach targeting maximum power output should place it after appropriate whole-body
resistance work, to exploit postactivation potentiation. Directly usable for Baylor
strength-staff session design when max-effort lower-body power output (not volume) is the
stated goal of a given lift.

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — This is a small (n = 9), single published study;
Issurin does not report effect sizes, statistical significance values, or the specific
whole-body exercise's loading in this citation beyond "hang pull" — treat the direction of
the finding as `supported` but do not treat the exact magnitude (8.0 vs. 5.4 reps) as
something to replicate precisely without reading the original study. It is unclear from this
text whether the postactivation-potentiation effect generalizes to different athlete
populations (this sample was resistance-trained males), different preceding exercises, or
different rest intervals between the potentiating exercise and the squat.

**Example** — A power-focused lower-body day for football linemen might sequence a
moderate-load hang pull or clean pull before back squats at 85% 1RM, accepting fewer total
squat reps in exchange for higher power output per rep on the squats that follow — a
hypertrophy-focused day for the same lift would instead put the squat first.

[[principle--training-modality-compatibility-in-a-workout]].

# A Structured Warm-Up/Cool-Down Program Cut Soccer Injuries Roughly Four-Fold Over Six Months

**What it is** — Ekstrand et al. (1983), cited by Issurin (PDF p. 58, printed p. 47,
Figure 2.1): **twelve** national-division soccer teams (**180 players**) were split into two
groups. The intervention group (six teams) used a modified program with carefully structured
warm-ups (ball exercises plus a stretching program) and cool-downs (jogging plus hold-relax
stretching), supervised by doctors and physiotherapists. The control group (six teams)
trained traditionally. Over a six-month follow-up, the intervention group showed "highly
significant superiority": a roughly **four-fold reduction in the number of injuries** and a
"drastic decrease" in workouts and games missed to musculoskeletal disorders. Issurin
separately cites supporting single-factor findings: stretching in warm-up increases lower
extremity range of motion in soccer players (Moller et al., 1985); active warm-up *without*
stretching does not affect flexibility (Zakas et al., 2006); and intense prior exercise
meaningfully stimulates aerobic metabolism in working muscles during subsequent strenuous
performance (Bangsbo et al., 2001). A related study (Moller et al., 1985, PDF p. 68, printed
p. 57) tracked 48 adult soccer players across three warm-up/cool-down protocols and found
that inserting a stretching series into the *cool-down* (not just the warm-up) produced range-
of-motion benefits both immediately after the session and 24 hours later, while stretching
only in the warm-up helped immediately but not at 24 hours.

**Why it works** — Issurin frames warm-up and cool-down as serving three functions each:
metabolic adjustment, technical/coordination adjustment, and mental readiness (warm-up,
Table 2.6, PDF p. 60, printed p. 49); and reduction of body temperature/HR/blood pressure,
removal of metabolic byproducts, endocrine-system recovery, and reduced emotional tension
(cool-down, Table 2.11, PDF p. 69, printed p. 58). **Contested — flag directly:** Issurin's
cool-down rationale explicitly frames blood lactate and "other acid metabolites" as "waste
products" to be "removed" from the muscles (PDF p. 68-69, printed pp. 57-58), citing a
specific clearance kinetic (blood lactate removal after a 4-minute exhausting time trial
takes about 20 minutes, Juel et al., 1990). This is in tension with a modern-physiology
claim already captured in this library's Verkhoshansky staging
([[supertraining--verkhoshansky/claim--lactate-and-oxygen-debt-myths]], `proven`): lactate is
not a toxin causing soreness or requiring active "removal," and it clears from the blood on
its own within about an hour of strenuous exercise regardless of cool-down activity, with
current research attributing post-exercise soreness to muscle/connective-tissue microtrauma
rather than residual lactate. The specific clearance-time citation (20 minutes for a 4-minute
time trial) may still be a real kinetic fact — that is not itself disputed here — but
Issurin's *framing* of this as evidence that cool-down actively "removes" a harmful waste
product reflects the outdated "lactic acid as toxin" model the Verkhoshansky entry corrects.
Cite this book's clearance-time number without repeating its waste-product framing.

**When to apply** — The Ekstrand et al. (1983) result is directly relevant to Applied
Performance's collaboration with Athletic Medicine on injury reduction: a structured,
medically-supervised warm-up/cool-down protocol is associated with a large, measured
reduction in soft-tissue injury incidence in a team sport, over a real six-month season, not
an acute lab measure. The cool-down-stretching timing finding (Moller et al., 1985) suggests
cool-down, not just warm-up, is where a stretching series may do more for next-day range of
motion.

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — This is an observational, non-randomized team-level
comparison (teams chose or were assigned to conditions; individual randomization is not
described), and the intervention bundled multiple changes at once (warm-up content, cool-down
content, and medical/physiotherapy supervision), so the four-fold injury reduction cannot be
attributed to warm-up or cool-down alone — Issurin himself flags this study as "atypical
because it refers to the complex effects of warming-up, cooling-down and medical supervision"
(PDF p. 58, printed p. 47). **Contested:** stretching's effect on preventing delayed-onset
muscle soreness specifically is described by Issurin himself as disputed within this same
chapter — "supported by several studies (Hartfield, 1985) and contradicted by others (High
et al., 1989)" (PDF p. 70, printed p. 59) — do not cite stretching-prevents-DOMS as settled.

**Example** — A Baylor position group's practice-ending routine that pairs light locomotion
with a stretching series (rather than stretching only in the pre-practice warm-up) has some
support here for next-day range-of-motion benefit, though the causal strength of the
underlying Moller et al. (1985) study for injury outcomes specifically (as opposed to ROM)
is not established by this citation.

the direct source of the lactate-framing contest above — recommend the Verkhoshansky entry
add a reciprocal link back to this one once both are reviewed).

# Which Training Modalities Tolerate Fatigue, and Which Need a Fresh Athlete

**What it is** — Figure 2.3 (PDF p. 72, printed p. 60) ranks eleven training modalities by
how much athlete fatigue they can tolerate before the acute training effect is compromised,
from "well rested" through "slightly fatigued," "moderately fatigued," to "fatigued":
**require well-rested or only slightly fatigued athletes** — maximal speed (least
fatigue-tolerant of all), acquisition of new technical skills, explosive strength, maximal
strength (neural mechanism, e.g. 1-3RM); **tolerate up to moderate fatigue** — anaerobic
glycolytic power, technique perfection, aerobic power, maximal strength (hypertrophy, e.g.
8-10RM); **tolerate fatigue through to the end of the session (most fatigue-tolerant)** —
anaerobic glycolytic capacity/endurance, aerobic endurance (long duration), strength
endurance, flexibility. Issurin's stated reasoning (PDF pp. 71-73, printed pp. 60-62):
exercises needing precise excitatory neural output (max speed, explosive strength, technical
skill acquisition, neural-mechanism strength) are blocked by inhibitory CNS output in
fatigued athletes, so they belong early; anaerobic glycolytic power needs available energy
reserves that are depleted in fatigued athletes; hypertrophy-strength work depends on total
mechanical work and post-exercise recovery conditions (not the athlete's pre-exercise
freshness), so it can be placed later or even after an endurance session; anaerobic
glycolytic capacity and strength/aerobic endurance work are defined by sustained effort
*despite* accumulating fatigue, so some fatigue is expected and even required for the
intended stimulus.

**Why it works** — CNS-dependent, high-precision, high-velocity qualities are limited by
inhibitory neural output and available energy stores that both degrade with fatigue,
while volume/duration-dependent qualities (hypertrophy, strength/aerobic endurance) depend
on total accumulated work or post-session recovery rather than the athlete's moment-to-moment
freshness — so sequencing exists to protect the fatigue-sensitive qualities' window, not
because "harder exercises always go first."

**When to apply** — Ordering exercises within a single practice or lift: place max-speed
work, new skill acquisition, explosive strength, and low-rep neural-strength work at the
start of a session (or after only light warm-up fatigue); place hypertrophy work, technical
perfection under fatigue, and long-duration aerobic/strength-endurance work later, when some
accumulated fatigue is acceptable or even intended. Issurin gives a worked example (PDF
p. 72, printed p. 61): an endurance athlete needing to preserve muscle mass can have a
hypertrophy-focused strength session (8-10RM) placed *after* a medium-load endurance
session, because hypertrophy's driver is total mechanical work and post-workout recovery
conditions, not pre-workout freshness — this would be the wrong sequencing choice for a
neural-strength (1-3RM) session, which does need "prime time."

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — **The author gives no primary citation for Figure 2.3
itself** — it reads as the author's own synthesized ranking rather than a reproduced,
independently-sourced scale; treat the ordinal ranking (which modality is more or less
fatigue-tolerant than its neighbor) as a reasonable but unvalidated synthesis, not a measured
result. The figure gives no guidance on *how much* fatigue is "slight" vs. "moderate" in
objective terms (RPE, HR, or specific fatigue markers), so applying it requires a coach's own
judgment call about where an athlete currently sits on that continuum. It also does not
address multi-session days directly — see [[heuristic--two-a-day-workout-sequencing-by-dominant-ability]]
for cross-session sequencing.

**Example** — A football practice combining new install (technical skill acquisition) with
conditioning: install work belongs at the start of practice when players are fresh and can
attend to technical detail; conditioning work belongs at the end, where sustained effort
under fatigue is the intended stimulus rather than a limitation.

[[principle--training-modality-compatibility-in-a-workout]].

# Structuring Two-a-Day (and Denser) Training Days by Dominant Ability and Rest Interval

**What it is** — Issurin describes how high-performance programs structure multiple same-day
sessions, most commonly two-a-days but up to four (routine in training-camp practice) or
occasionally six (an "exception," per anecdotal coach reports, PDF p. 76, printed p. 65). For
a four-session aerobic-development day (Figure 2.5, PDF p. 77, printed p. 66): session 1
(early morning) uses small load — warm-up, alactic bouts combined with medium-intensity
aerobic work, addressing morning stiffness/soreness; session 2 (mid-morning, after a
multi-hour break) uses medium/substantial load — aerobic interval series combined with
technical tasks, where sprint bouts interact positively with the aerobic work; session 3
(early afternoon, after a one-hour break) uses medium/substantial load — concentrated aerobic
work; session 4 (late afternoon, after a three-hour break) uses substantial load — continuous
aerobic exercise and aerobic-resistance work, with an emphasized, longer cool-down given
accumulated fatigue. For anaerobic-development days (Figure 2.6, PDF p. 78, printed p. 67):
session 2 uses substantial load for anaerobic glycolytic power (fast lactate/oxygen-debt
accumulation); the one-hour break before session 3 allows roughly 70-80% of accumulated
lactate to be oxidized (Volkov, 1986), so session 3's anaerobic glycolytic capacity work
still starts with an already-activated glycolytic/enzymatic system; session 4 (after a
three-hour break, athletes arriving fatigued) targets anaerobic strength endurance,
deliberately sustaining progressive fatigue. For the more common two-a-day structure, Table
2.13 (PDF p. 79, printed p. 68) gives compatible first/second-workout pairings by dominant
ability: aerobic power/capacity — fartlek (10-15s sprint, 3-6min at anaerobic threshold)
first, aerobic interval series second; anaerobic glycolytic power/capacity — anaerobic power
interval series first, anaerobic capacity interval series second; maximal speed — anaerobic
alactic interval series first, anaerobic alactic plus explosive strength exercises second;
techno-tactical abilities — techno-tactical simulation/maximum speed first, match/scrimmage
second.

**Why it works** — The break length between sessions determines how much of the prior
session's fatigue and metabolic byproduct load has cleared before the next session begins,
and the daily plan deliberately exploits this: shorter breaks (one hour) leave residual
glycolytic activation that *helps* a subsequent glycolytic-capacity session, while longer
breaks (three hours) still leave athletes fatigued enough that only fatigue-tolerant
modalities (strength endurance, continuous aerobic work) are appropriate for the day's final
session.

**When to apply** — Directly applicable to football fall-camp scheduling, where two-a-day
sessions are standard. **Eric's baseline** names a specific priority stack for fall camp —
"Goal 1, alactic and aerobic capacity/power for fall-camp demands" (`method-baseline.md`,
"Conditioning and energy systems," citing `class-4-2026.md` pp.34, 39) — and Table 2.13's
maximal-speed pairing (anaerobic alactic interval series both sessions, explosive strength
added to the second) is a directly relevant template for structuring a two-a-day built
around that same alactic/aerobic priority.

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — Issurin gives no controlled-study citation for the
specific figures in this section (session load levels, the four-session structure, the
70-80% lactate-oxidation-in-one-hour figure attributed to Volkov, 1986, is the one sourced
number; the rest of the daily-series structure reads as the author's own applied synthesis).
The four-session model is explicitly noted as less common in practice than the two-a-day.
**Inference:** none of this section's examples are football-specific (they are framed around
individual/endurance-and-power sports generally); the specific break lengths (one hour,
three hours) and load progressions should be treated as a template to adapt, not a
prescription to copy directly into a football fall-camp schedule without accounting for
football-specific factors (equipment, heat, practice structure, position-group differences)
not addressed in this chapter.

**Example** — A fall-camp two-a-day built around the department's stated alactic/aerobic
priority could pair a morning session of alactic interval sprint work with an afternoon
session combining more alactic work and explosive strength (per Table 2.13's maximal-speed
row), rather than defaulting to a generic "conditioning AM, lift PM" split that does not
target a specific dominant ability across the two sessions.
