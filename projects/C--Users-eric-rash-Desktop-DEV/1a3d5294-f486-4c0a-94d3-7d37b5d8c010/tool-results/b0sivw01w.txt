# Concentration of Training Workloads Is the Cornerstone Principle of Block Periodization

**What it is** — Issurin states the Block Periodization Concept (BPC) rests on one
foundational principle, concentration of training workloads, from which two others follow:
(1) a minimal number of targeted abilities per block, because highly concentrated loads
"only provide sufficient training stimuli to improve the targeted ability," and (2)
consecutive (not simultaneous) development of abilities, because "the number of decisive
abilities exceeds the number of abilities that can be developed simultaneously" (PDF p. 37,
printed p. 26, Figure 1.7). The rationale is laid out as a table of contradictions in the
traditional, simultaneous-development model for high-level athletes (Table 1.4, PDF p. 20,
printed p. 9): concurrent workloads compete for limited energy supply, differing
physiological-system restoration times leave athletes under-recovered for all targets at
once, workloads interact negatively (energy deficits, technical complexity, neuromuscular
fatigue), mental concentration cannot be split across many targets, and the training
stimulus needed for a high-level athlete's progress cannot be reached when it is divided
among many abilities.

**Why it works** — Issurin's own explanation is that above a threshold of athletic level,
a quality only responds to a training stimulus concentrated enough to exceed that
threshold; splitting the same total training time across many qualities keeps each one's
stimulus below that threshold. He does not give a quantitative mechanism for where that
threshold sits or how "concentrated enough" is defined for a given ability or athlete — the
principle is argued from coaches' and scientists' collective experience (Table 1.4) rather
than derived from a stated dose-response model.

**When to apply** — Designing training blocks for advanced, high-level athletes juggling
more qualities than can be trained well at once (the explicit target population, per
Issurin). **Eric's baseline:** this converges with the department's own compatibility rule,
"you can't ride two horses with one saddle" — incompatible qualities are not both trained at
full intensity in the same block (`method-baseline.md`, "Periodization and programming,"
citing `01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md` pp.28-29). It also converges with
[[periodization--bompa/principle--concentrated-loading-overreaching]], which independently
describes concentrated, saturated loading on one quality with a delayed 4-12 week rebound.

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — Issurin's own text (PDF p. 44, printed p. 33) states the
traditional, complex (simultaneous multi-ability) model "has visible benefits for the
preparation of low- and mid-level athletes": it is "more diversified, attractive and
lively," and medium-level concentration is "sufficient stimulation" at that level — the
concentration principle is explicitly scoped to high-performance athletes, not a universal
programming rule. **Contested:** Issurin's blanket claim elsewhere in the same chapter that
the traditional model "does not work well for high-performance athletes" (PDF p. 19,
printed p. 8) is disputed by this library's own Bompa & Haff 5th-edition material
(`periodization--bompa`), which is a current (not 1984-vintage) textbook still teaching and
defending a form of the traditional/complex periodization model for high-level athletes.
Issurin's critique in this chapter names Bompa's *1984* edition specifically (PDF p. 13,
printed p. 2) as one of the texts that propagated the traditional model he is arguing
against — it is not a critique of the Bompa & Haff 5th edition already in this library,
which incorporates concentrated-loading and conjugated-sequence ideas closer to Issurin's
own. **Inference:** Issurin's own later peer-reviewed review (2016, *Sports Medicine*,
"Benefits and Limitations of Block Periodized Training Approaches," Zotero item
`PT9QTQ2I`, not yet full-text-cached in this ingest) is titled around limitations as well as
benefits and likely narrows this 2008 book's confident framing; treat the frontmatter
`confidence: contested` as applying specifically to the universal "traditional doesn't work
for high performers" claim, not to the narrower, self-limited concentration principle.

**Example** — A collegiate football strength staff trying to develop max strength,
aerobic capacity, alactic power, and technical skill acquisition all at full intensity in
the same 4-week block, per Table 1.4's logic, risks under-stimulating all four rather than
meaningfully improving any one; concentrating the block on 1-2 of them (with the rest held
at a retention dose) is the alternative Issurin argues for.

**Related** — [[framework--block-periodization-mesocycle-taxonomy]] — the three-block
structure this principle produces. [[periodization--bompa/principle--concentrated-loading-overreaching]]
and [[periodization--bompa/framework--step-loading-paradigm]] — converging concentrated-load
ideas already in this library. [[supertraining--verkhoshansky/principle--specificity-of-training-ten-dimensions]] (staging) —

# No More Than Three Training Modalities Per Workout, With 65-70% of Time on One or Two

**What it is** — Issurin extends the concentration principle to single-session design
(Figure 2.4, PDF p. 75, printed p. 63; Table 2.12, PDF p. 76, printed p. 65) with five
stated rules (PDF p. 75, printed p. 64): (1) a workout should contain **no more than three
training modalities** — typically one dominant, one compatible with the main purpose, and
one for technique/tactics improvement or restoration; (2) **65-70% of total training time**
in a development workout should go to one or two selected modalities, to preserve workload
concentration; (3) after a key-workout, the following session should show a significant
load reduction — running two consecutive key-workouts is possible but risks excessive load
concentration; (4) hypertrophy-focused sessions require workload to be "substantially
reduced" for **at least 20 hours** afterward to protect the anabolic phase of muscle
restoration; (5) limiting modality count matters most in high-performance training —
junior/developmental athletes' programs can be more diverse and multilateral. Figure 2.4
maps which modalities are compatible as secondary/tertiary additions to five dominant ones:
aerobic endurance pairs with alactic sprint, strength-endurance-aerobic, and
hypertrophy-strength (placed after); anaerobic glycolytic endurance pairs with
strength-endurance-anaerobic, aerobic restoration, and mixed aerobic-anaerobic endurance;
alactic sprint pairs with aerobic endurance, explosive strength, hypertrophy-strength
(after), and aerobic restoration; hypertrophy-strength pairs with neural-strength, flexibility,
and aerobic restoration; new-technical-skill learning pairs with any modality, but only
*after* the dominant task. Table 2.12 gives the physiological/psychological rationale for
several of the strongest pairings (e.g., aerobic endurance + strength endurance: increased
oxidation supports the resistance work; alactic sprint + aerobic endurance: brief sprints
break monotony and keep a wide muscle-fiber pool activated for the subsequent aerobic work).

**Why it works — the specific numeric thresholds are asserted, not derived** — The
underlying logic is the same concentration principle from Chapter 1
([[principle--concentration-of-training-workloads]]): splitting a session across too many
modalities dilutes the stimulus for all of them. **Inference:** Issurin gives no stated
derivation for why the cutoff is exactly three modalities rather than two or four, why the
time-share threshold is 65-70% rather than some other range, or why 20 hours is the specific
reduced-load window after hypertrophy work (versus, e.g., 24 or 48 hours) — these read as
the author's own practical rules of thumb built from coaching experience rather than
citations to a dose-response study.

**When to apply** — Auditing whether a practice plan or lift is trying to do too much:
count distinct training modalities in a session and check whether one or two of them
actually receive the majority of training time, per the 65-70% rule. Use Figure 2.4/Table
2.12 to check whether a planned secondary modality is a known-compatible pairing or an
unaddressed combination. **Eric's baseline** independently states a directionally identical
rule for compatibility, though at the block level rather than the single-session level:
"incompatible qualities... are not both targeted at full intensity in the same block"
(`method-baseline.md`, "Periodization and programming," citing
`01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md` pp.28-29, "you can't ride two horses with one
saddle") — this Issurin entry gives that same instinct a specific numeric structure (≤3
modalities, 65-70% time-share) that the baseline material does not currently specify at
either the block or session level.

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — Treat the ≤3-modality, 65-70%, and 20-hour figures as
Issurin's own heuristics rather than established constants — no citation or underlying data
is given for any of the three in this chapter. Issurin explicitly narrows the number
constraints to high-performance training; junior/developmental programming is stated to
reasonably need more variety, not fewer modalities. Figure 2.4's compatibility map is not
exhaustive (it covers five dominant modalities, not every possible combination) — an
untested pairing should not be assumed incompatible or compatible by default; Issurin gives
no guidance for modalities not shown in the figure.

**Example** — A practice plan combining strength-endurance, aerobic-anaerobic conditioning,
maximal-speed sprint work, and new-install technical work in one session would have four
distinct modalities, exceeding the stated ≤3 rule; per Figure 2.4, glycolytic endurance
pairs well with strength-endurance-anaerobic and aerobic restoration, but maximal speed and
new technical skill acquisition would need to be reduced to secondary/tertiary roles or
moved to a separate session to keep the session within the concentration guideline.

# Accumulation, Transmutation (Transformation), and Realization: the Three Mesocycle-Blocks

**What it is** — Issurin's core structural unit is the mesocycle-block, one of three types
(Table 1.7, PDF p. 37, printed p. 26): **Accumulation** targets basic abilities (aerobic
endurance, muscular strength, basic coordination) with high volume/reduced intensity and
"reasonable restoration"; **Transmutation** targets sport-specific abilities (special
endurance, strength endurance, proper technique) with reduced volume/increased intensity,
and explicitly does *not* provide full restoration — fatigue accumulates; **Realization**
targets integrative, competition-ready preparedness (model performances, maximum speed,
event-specific tactics) at low-medium volume/high intensity, with full restoration required
going in. Each type has its own monitoring focus: basic-ability tests for accumulation,
sport-specific-ability tests for transmutation, and maximum speed/strategy checks for
realization. **Locator note:** the 1985 origin paper (Issurin & Kaverin) and this book's
1.2.1 narrative (PDF p. 33, printed p. 22) use the term **transformation**; from section
1.2.2 onward (PDF p. 37+, printed p. 26+) and in the book's own tables, the term used is
**transmutation**. Both names refer to the same middle block; record both because a search
for either term should resolve to this entry.

**Why it works** — The sequencing is designed to exploit the fact that different motor
abilities have different **residual training effect** durations (see
[[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]]): basic abilities (aerobic
endurance, max strength) decay slowest, so they can be built early and coast on their own
residual while transmutation and realization narrow the focus toward faster-decaying,
more specific and speed-dependent qualities close to competition. Issurin frames this as
superimposition of residual effects (Figure 1.8, PDF p. 39, printed p. 28) rather than
concurrent development of everything at once.

**When to apply** — Structuring a training stage (the accumulation-transmutation-realization
sequence, ending in competition) for advanced athletes who need multiple qualities peaked
together — see [[framework--training-stage-and-multi-peak-annual-cycle]] for how these three
blocks compose into a full annual plan. **Eric's baseline** shows a structurally similar but
independently-arrived-at sequence: the department's own preparatory-period design goes
hypertrophy/work-capacity, then basic strength, then strength-to-power
(`method-baseline.md`, "Periodization and programming," citing week1-lecture.md pp.43-48) —
a general-to-specific progression that parallels accumulation-to-realization, though the
baseline's sequence is not explicitly framed with Issurin's residual-effect superimposition
logic or his restoration-status distinctions between blocks.

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — This is a three-way classification built for advanced
athletes who can tolerate the fatigue-accumulating transmutation block (see
[[principle--concentration-of-training-workloads]] for the low-/mid-level-athlete
carve-out). It presumes a training stage that ends in a real competition or test date to
absorb the realization block's tapering logic; without a fixed target date, there is no
clear trigger for when accumulation should end and transmutation begin. **Contested (partly):**
the vault's Bompa & Haff material (`periodization--bompa/principle--concentrated-loading-overreaching`)
independently describes a comparable saturated-load-then-restitution structure with a
4-12 week delayed rebound — a longer and less rigidly three-staged model than Issurin's
roughly two-month training stage (below); the two should be read as convergent but not
identical prescriptions, and a program note should say which model's timing a given plan
follows.

**Example** — A training stage built for a mid-season peak: 3-4 weeks of accumulation
(aerobic base, general strength), 3-4 weeks of transmutation (sport-specific conditioning,
strength-endurance, technique under fatigue), then 1-2 weeks of realization (tapered
volume, high-intensity model-speed work, full restoration) ending at the target competition
or test date.

# The Training Stage (A-T-R + Competition) Is the Annual Plan's Building Block, Enabling Multiple Peaks

**What it is** — Issurin's annual cycle is built from a sequence of **training stages**,
each one containing all three mesocycle-blocks (accumulation, transmutation, realization) in
order, ending in a competition (PDF p. 38, printed p. 27). Each training stage "gives the
appearance of an annual cycle in miniature" — a block resembling a preparatory period
(accumulation), one resembling a competition period (transmutation), and a tapering block
ending in competitive performance (realization) (PDF p. 39, printed p. 28). The duration of
a training stage is set by how long the constituent residual training effects can be
superimposed (Figure 1.8; see
[[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]]) and "should be about two
months," though Issurin notes stages can run shorter near season's end or longer earlier in
the season, in which case "special measures should be used to prolong residual training
effects" (unspecified in this chapter — likely developed in Chapter 3, not yet ingested).
The number of training stages in a season "usually varies from four to seven," depending on
the sport's competition calendar (PDF p. 40, printed p. 29, Figure 1.9). Issurin lists five
claimed benefits of this design over the traditional model (PDF p. 40, printed p. 29, and
Table 1.8, PDF p. 42, printed p. 31): reduced total training volume without reducing workout
count; more purposeful monitoring (fewer targeted abilities per stage); improved
psychological focus and motivation; more targeted nutrition per block (e.g., higher protein
during strength-focused blocks); and — most relevant to the peaking question — "a
multi-stage annual plan creates more favorable conditions for peaking in time for the main
competition of the season," with intermediate peaks plannable mid-season or even early
season, not only at a single season-end date.

**Why it works** — Because each training stage runs its own compressed
accumulation-transmutation-realization cycle and ends in a real competitive test, the athlete
is brought to a peak (or near-peak) at the end of every stage rather than once per year;
stacking four to seven such stages across a season is what produces the multi-peak pattern.
Issurin's stated physiological contrast (Table 1.8) is that the traditional model relies on
adaptation to concurrent stimuli across a long period, while this model relies on
superimposing sequential residual effects from short, concentrated blocks — the same
mechanism underlying [[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]].

**When to apply** — This is the entry most directly responsive to the strength-and-conditioning
miss-log gap ("peaking phase structure for collegiate football," logged 2026-09-03): for a
football program needing camp-readiness, then in-season week-to-week competitive readiness,
this framework suggests treating each significant test point (fall camp scrimmage, each
game week, or a defined in-season block) as a candidate training-stage boundary rather than
relying on a single annual peak, with the caveat below about untested claims. **Eric's
baseline** does not currently describe an explicit training-stage or multi-peak model for
football; the closest baseline material is the general macro/meso/microcycle planning
language and the in-season rule "maintain intensity, reduce volume" (`method-baseline.md`,
"Periodization and programming" and "Strength and power," citing
`01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md` pp.17-18, 20-21) — this Issurin framework offers a
more explicit structural answer to "how do you actually sequence a season with more than one
peak" than anything currently captured in the baseline.

**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — The "four to seven stages" and "about two months"
figures are stated without a derivation the reader can check — Issurin gives no worked
example of how stage count or duration is calculated for a specific sport's calendar in
this chapter. **Inference:** football's in-season structure (weekly games, not discrete
multi-week competition-free blocks) does not map cleanly onto a ~2-month training stage —
Issurin's model is built from and mainly evidenced by individual/dual sports with

accumulation, ~8 weeks, winter conditioning); Stage 2 (strength/power transmutation into

Stage 4 (fall-camp transmutation, ending in the first scrimmage or game week as
realization); Stage 5+ (in-season stages keyed to bye weeks or rivalry-week peaks, pending
