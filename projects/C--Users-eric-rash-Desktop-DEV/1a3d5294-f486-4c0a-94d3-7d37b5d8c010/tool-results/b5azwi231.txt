# "Adaptive Reconstruction" vs. "Supercompensation": Verkhoshansky/Siff's Sharper Critique

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky (via Siff) argues "adaptive reconstruction" is a more
accurate term than "supercompensation" for the body's response to training load, for two
specific reasons stated in the text: (1) enhanced performance is not simply the result of
increased stores of bioenergetic substances (the energy-overshoot picture supercompensation
implies), but also of structural changes in muscle and connective tissue and enhanced
efficiency of bioenergetic and neuromuscular processing; (2) no supercompensation has been
observed for any bioenergetic substance except glycogen — other biochemicals deplete under
strenuous exercise but do not overshoot their baseline afterward, and each returns to
baseline on its own timeline, making the timing of a "supercompensation window" for
non-glycogen substances indeterminate. Russian sources use "Current Adaptation Reserves"
(CAR) for the readily-available (superficial) and emergency (deep) energy sources drawn on
during Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome resistance phase. (PDF pp. 94-96, printed pp.
83-85.)

**Why it works** — If supercompensation literally meant every adaptation-relevant substance
or structure overshoots baseline on a predictable clock, timing the next training stimulus
to "catch the peak" would be a precise science; the empirical finding that this overshoot
pattern is essentially unique to glycogen undercuts that precision and supports treating
"the next stimulus should land on a rebound" as a general heuristic, not a substance-by-
substance timing rule.

**When to apply** — This library already carries the supercompensation model in
[[periodization--bompa/principle--supercompensation-cycle]], which itself notes
"contemporary sport science largely prefers the fitness-fatigue (two-factor) model over
literal supercompensation" and recommends treating supercompensation "as a useful mental
model, not a measured prescription." **Inference:** this entry adds the specific mechanism
for that caution (structural/efficiency changes beyond energy stores, and the
glycogen-only overshoot finding) and the CAR terminology, which the Bompa entry does not
include; use both together rather than duplicating either.

**When NOT to apply** — This entry is rated `contested` because it is itself a critique of
a still widely-taught model (supercompensation), argued from a mix of established fact
(structural adaptation beyond energy stores) and an absence-of-evidence claim (no
overshoot observed for other biochemicals) that could be revised by newer measurement
techniques. **Inference:** don't treat "adaptive reconstruction" as a rejection of
periodization practice built on supercompensation timing; the text itself notes
supercompensation theory, despite its shortcomings, had a profound and still-useful impact
on periodization design.

**Example** — **Inference:** glycogen loading before an endurance event is a legitimate
application of true supercompensation; assuming a similarly precise "peak window" exists
for, say, connective tissue remodeling or neuromuscular efficiency gains is not supported
by the same evidence.

**Related** — [[periodization--bompa/principle--supercompensation-cycle]] (reciprocal link
recommended there: note that Supertraining Ch1.16 sharpens the "useful mental model, not a
measured prescription" caution with the specific glycogen-only-overshoot finding and the
CAR/adaptive-reconstruction terminology).

# The Bilateral Deficit: Two Limbs Together Produce Less Force Than the Sum of Each Alone

**What it is** — Force produced during a bilateral (two-limb, e.g. barbell) contraction is
usually less than the sum of the forces each limb produces individually (e.g. dumbbells),
a phenomenon called the bilateral deficit, accompanied by reduced integrated EMG
suggesting the prime movers are less activated bilaterally (Coyle et al. 1981;
Vandervoort et al. 1984). The deficit is reported as insignificant in athletes who always
use their limbs simultaneously in their sport (e.g. weightlifters), and the text
recommends athletes whose sport involves bilateral action of the same muscle groups train
bilaterally to minimize the deficit (Secher, 1975). (PDF p. 39, printed p. 28.)

**Why it works** — The deficit is attributed to a neural, not mechanical, limitation:
reduced activation of the prime movers during simultaneous bilateral effort, which
training the specific bilateral pattern appears to reduce over time — another instance of
narrow, mode-specific neuromuscular adaptation.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** when deciding whether to prioritize bilateral (back
squat, bench, clean) or unilateral (split squat, single-leg press, lunge) exercise
variants, match the training mode to how the sport actually loads the limbs. For sports
with genuinely unilateral force production (sprinting, most cutting and
change-of-direction actions), this text's finding argues for meaningful unilateral training
exposure, not exclusively bilateral lifts, since bilateral-only training may under-develop
the single-limb activation football and track actions require. This football-specific
application is this entry's own extension, not stated in the text.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** do not use this to argue against bilateral lifts
altogether; compound bilateral lifts remain valuable for maximal strength and are
appropriate where the sport itself uses bilateral effort (e.g. some blocking or lifting
actions). Verkhoshansky gives no specific deficit magnitude for team-sport athletes in
this section (PDF p. 39, printed p. 28); **Inference:** the deficit's size and
trainability likely vary by athlete and lift, which is this entry's own caution.

**Example** — **Inference:** A sprinter's single-leg force production during ground contact is not fully
predicted by their bilateral back squat strength alone; programming that includes
single-leg strength work targets the bilateral deficit gap directly relevant to sprint and
change-of-direction performance.

# Collagenous Tissue's Four-Region Loading Curve, and Why Loading Rate Changes What Fails First

**What it is** — Collagenous tissue (e.g. the ACL, studied by Frankel & Nordin 1980)
loaded to failure shows four regions: Region I, the "toe region," where the crimped
tissue straightens with little force and mild stretching here produces no long-term
flexibility change; Region II, a near-linear region where fibres are fully uncrimped and
parallel; Region III, where sequential microfailure of overstretched fibres begins; Region
IV, complete failure. Loading rate changes the outcome: increasing loading rate roughly
fourfold raised knee ligament failure load by almost 50% (Kennedy et al., 1976), and which
structure fails first flips with rate — at slow loading rates the ligament's bony
insertion is the weakest link, while at fast loading rates the ligament itself is the
weakest link (bone's strength rises faster with rate than the ligament's). Healthy tendon's
tensile strength can be more than twice that of its associated muscle, which is why
ruptures are more common in muscle than tendon. (PDF pp. 54-56, printed pp. 43-45.)

**Why it works** — Collagen is viscoelastic: it behaves more elastically under rapid
loading and more plastically (creeping, deforming) under slow, sustained loading, so both
the loading speed and the tissue's own rate-dependent stiffness change determine where in
the body an excessive load actually causes failure.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** relevant background for understanding why static
stretching held gently (Region I) does not meaningfully change tissue length or
flexibility over time, and why ballistic or rapid loading, once past Region II, carries
real microfailure risk; also relevant context for why the pattern of injury (bone
avulsion vs. mid-ligament tear vs. muscle strain) can differ by the speed of the loading
event, which matters for how Athletic Medicine and Applied Performance jointly interpret
an injury mechanism.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** this is basic tissue mechanics from cited
1970s-1980s cadaveric/animal ligament research (ACL specifically), not a live-tissue,
in-vivo human athlete dataset; do not use the specific numbers (50% failure-load increase,
the fourfold rate figure) as applicable outside the original ACL loading-rate study cited.
This is background science, not a clinical decision rule, and should not substitute for
Athletic Medicine's own injury assessment framework.

**Example** — **Inference:** a sudden high-rate valgus load to the knee is more likely to
produce mid-ligament ACL failure, while a slower, sustained load is more likely to fail at
the bony insertion first; this pattern helps explain, but does not replace, imaging and
clinical exam in determining actual injury location.

# Continuous Circuit Training Does Not Simultaneously Build Strength and Endurance; Interval Circuit Training Is a Better Fit

**What it is** — Research does not support the claim (made by some instructors and machine
manufacturers) that continuous circuit training (CCT) simultaneously develops
cardiovascular endurance and strength. Interval circuit training (ICT: high-intensity
loading with regular rest intervals) is described as more suitable for developing strength
and strength-endurance together. Supporting evidence cited: prolonged low-intensity
endurance training performed alongside strength training in the same phase seriously
compromises strength/power development, partly because fast-twitch fibres can start
behaving like slow-twitch fibres under prolonged low-intensity load; muscle biopsy studies
of distance runners' gastrocnemius after a marathon show necrosis and inflammation
detectable at least 7 days later, an abnormality not seen in comparable biopsies from
weightlifters, sprinters, or rowers after strenuous sessions. (PDF p. 70, printed p. 59.)

**Why it works** — Continuous, moderate-intensity circuit work trains the aerobic/oxidative
system and biases fibre behavior toward slow-twitch characteristics, which is a different
and partly conflicting neuromuscular target from the high-force, high-velocity, largely
fast-twitch demands of strength and power work; ICT's rest intervals let each work bout
stay in a higher-intensity, more strength/power-specific zone instead of drifting toward
an endurance stimulus.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** relevant when designing off-season/GPP programming
that needs to develop both conditioning and strength: the text's recommendation is to
prescribe cardiovascular training separately, in limited amounts, early in the off-season,
with higher-intensity resistance training emphasized later, and to use interval-style
(work/rest) circuits rather than continuous circuits when the goal includes
strength-endurance. **Eric's baseline:** this converges with the department's own
conditioning priority stack for football (alactic and aerobic capacity/power first, with
glycolytic conditioning absorbed incidentally rather than programmed as a primary target;
`method-baseline.md`, "Conditioning and energy systems," citing `class-4-2026.md pp.34,
39`), though the baseline material does not itself cite this fibre-type mechanism.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** this is evidence against combining continuous,
prolonged low-intensity work with concurrent strength training in the same block, not an
argument against all conditioning; short, high-intensity interval conditioning is treated
as more compatible with strength/power goals. The gastrocnemius necrosis finding is
specific to marathon-distance running, not necessarily to all forms of aerobic
conditioning used in team-sport contexts.

**Example** — **Inference:** programming continuous, moderate-pace circuit stations as a
"two birds, one stone" strength-and-conditioning solution is not well supported by this
evidence; separating a short conditioning block from resistance training, or using
interval-format circuits with real rest between high-intensity efforts, is the better-
supported approach.

# Muscle Adapts in Days; Tendon, Ligament, and Bone Take Weeks to Months

**What it is** — Muscle tissue adapts to increased loading within several days, whereas
connective tissues (tendon, ligament, joint capsule) and tissue systems with a high
connective-tissue content (bone, cartilage) only show significant adaptation and
hypertrophy after several weeks to months of progressive loading (McDonagh & Davies,
1984). Verkhoshansky states training prescription must account for the different
adaptation rates of all involved systems and avoid overtraining whichever system adapts
slowest; he recommends gradual load increases and avoiding impulsive/explosive methods
particularly during General Physical Preparation (GPP) and for relative novices to
strength training. (PDF p. 46, printed p. 35.)

**Why it works** — Muscle's adaptive machinery (protein synthesis, neural recruitment)
responds faster than the slower turnover and remodeling of collagenous tissue; a program
that progresses load at the muscle's adaptation rate can outpace what tendon, ligament,
and bone can structurally tolerate, which is a well-established mechanism in tendon and
connective-tissue injury research independent of this text.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** directly supports conservative, gradual load
progression in GPP/early training blocks and for novice athletes specifically, and argues
against introducing high-impulse plyometric or explosive work before a base period of
progressive loading has given connective tissue time to adapt. This is a mechanistic
rationale for why "the athlete feels ready for more" (muscle-level readiness) is not
sufficient justification for a rapid load jump (tendon/bone-level readiness lags behind).

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** this passage gives no specific timeline (only
"several days" vs. "several weeks or months") and no athlete-population-specific data; do
not treat it as a precise dosing table. **Inference:** this is a general injury-risk
mechanism, not a substitute for clinical input from Athletic Medicine on a specific
athlete's tissue status or injury history.

**Example** — **Inference:** a novice athlete whose squat strength (muscle-level) is
progressing quickly should still have their loading ramp paced by a longer timeline
(weeks to months) appropriate to tendon and bone adaptation, not by how fast their 1RM is
climbing.

# Classic Energy-System Percentage Tables by Sport (Fox & Mathews, 1974) — Football Listed at ~90% Phosphagen

**What it is** — Verkhoshansky reproduces Fox & Mathews' (1974) classic tables estimating
the percentage contribution of the short-term (phosphagen/ATP-CP), intermediate
(non-oxidative glycolytic), and long-term (oxidative/aerobic) energy systems to different
training modes and sports. American football is listed at 90% short-term, 10%
intermediate, 0% long-term — among the most phosphagen-dominant sports in the table,
comparable to golf (95/5/0) and volleyball (90/10/0), and far more phosphagen-dominant
than field hockey or soccer half-backs (60/20/20). Verkhoshansky himself cautions that
this "highly determinate" scheme, compiled decades earlier, has been questioned, that
figures for some events (he names the 1500m specifically) have since been revised, and
that it should not be used casually because it disguises moment-to-moment metabolic
demand shifts within a single sport. (PDF pp. 91-92, printed pp. 80-81.)

**Why it works** — The table estimates an average, whole-game contribution, whereas actual
play consists of short high-intensity bursts (plays) separated by long rest (huddles,
substitutions), so an average figure can obscure the real repeated-sprint, phosphagen-
dominant-per-play structure while also underweighting the role of aerobic recovery between
bursts.

**When to apply** — **Eric's baseline:** the department's stated conditioning priority for
football is alactic and aerobic capacity/power for fall-camp and game demands, with
glycolytic conditioning treated as a "worst case scenario" absorbed incidentally rather
than programmed as a primary target (`method-baseline.md`, "Conditioning and energy
systems," citing `class-4-2026.md pp.34, 39`); the baseline also states the aerobic
system's main value for speed-power athletes is inter-effort recovery (regenerating ATP
between bouts), not distance capacity (citing `2026-02-05-hp5355-class3-transcript.md
p.9`). **Inference:** this table's 90/10/0 split for football (near-zero credited to the
long-term/aerobic system) looks, at first read, to be in tension with the baseline's
explicit inclusion of aerobic capacity as a Goal-1 priority for football — but the tension
likely resolves once "aerobic system" is understood functionally: the baseline treats
aerobic fitness as enabling faster ATP/phosphagen regeneration between the very
phosphagen-dominant plays this table describes, not as a source of in-play energy. Read
together, not as contradictory: this table describes what fuels a single play; the
baseline describes what determines how well that fuel regenerates for the next one.

**When NOT to apply** — Verkhoshansky's own caution applies directly here: **Inference**
do not use the 90/10/0 football figure as a precise or current number; it is a decades-old
average estimate for the sport as a whole, not a modern, position-specific or play-by-play
breakdown, and the source text itself flags the whole table's accuracy as needing
re-examination. This entry is rated `contested` because it is presented as a classic,
already-questioned scheme rather than an established modern reference.

**Example** — **Inference:** a single football play (a few seconds of maximal effort)
draws almost entirely on the phosphagen system, consistent with the table; but the
athlete's ability to repeat that near-maximal output play after play, series after series,
depends heavily on aerobic capacity for recovery between plays — the quality the baseline
prioritizes.

**Related** — None in this ingest yet; candidate future link to a conditioning synthesis
note once Phase 4 of the Flint project reaches conditioning and energy systems.

# The Classic "Slow-Fibres-First-by-Force-Magnitude" Recruitment Model Fails for Ballistic Movement

**What it is** — The standard recruitment scheme (slow-twitch fibres recruited first up to
roughly 20% of maximum force, then progressively more fast-twitch fibres as force rises)
holds for graded, progressive loading, but Bosco (1982, cited PDF pp. 74-75, printed pp.
63-64) shows it breaks down for ballistic motion. Walking at moderate speed produces over
700 N of vertical ground reaction force yet, by contact time and movement pattern, relies
almost entirely on slow-twitch action; a maximal, high-velocity knee extension against a
leg-press plate produces less force than walking despite being nearly pure fast-twitch
action. The force-velocity curve itself differs by jump type: squat jumps and isokinetic
testing reflect mainly the muscle's contractile component and follow the traditional
hyperbolic F-V curve, while countermovement (dip) jumps and depth jumps are strongly
shaped by elastic energy released from the Series Elastic Component during the preceding
rapid stretch, producing an entirely different curve shape that does not fit the
traditional model. Sprinting, high jump, and long jump coordinates also fall far from the
traditional F-V curve. Bosco specifically cautions that isokinetic training is not
advisable for athletes in predominantly ballistic sports.

**Why it works** — Force magnitude alone does not determine which fibres are recruited or
how force is produced; movement pattern, contact duration, and whether elastic energy
storage/release is involved (SSC-mediated ballistic actions vs. purely contractile,
non-ballistic actions) change both the recruitment pattern and the resulting force-time and
force-velocity relationships.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** reinforces
[[framework--strength-deficit-jump-comparison-estimate]] and
[[claim--fiber-recruitment-not-simply-graded-by-force|this entry's]] own caution: SJ, CMJ,
and depth-jump testing are not interchangeable measures of the same underlying quality,
because they load the contractile component and the SEC to very different degrees. Also
reinforces caution about isokinetic testing/training for ballistic-sport athletes already
raised in [[claim--velocity-and-contraction-specificity-asymmetric-transfer]].

**When NOT to apply** — This entry is rated `contested` rather than `supported` because it
directly overturns a simpler, still widely-taught recruitment model (force-graded,
slow-then-fast) using a small number of illustrative examples (walking, car-pushing,
seated knee extension) rather than a systematic dataset; **Inference:** treat the classic
graded model as a reasonable first approximation for slow, controlled movement, and this
critique as the more accurate account specifically for ballistic/SSC-involved movement.

**Example** — **Inference:** a coach should not assume a squat-jump-based force
measurement predicts depth-jump or sprint ground-reaction performance; the elastic
contribution present in the latter two is largely absent from the former.

# Biochemical Adaptations Occur in a Fixed Order, and Reverse in the Opposite Order When Training Stops

**What it is** — Citing Platonov (1988), Verkhoshansky states biochemical adaptation to
training follows a definite sequence, not a simultaneous shift: first, oxidative ATP
resynthesis potential and glycogen level increase; next, structural muscle protein (myosin)
and the intensity of non-oxidative ATP resynthesis (glycolysis) increase; only after that
does creatine phosphate (CP) level rise. During overtraining or detraining, these
adaptations are lost in the reverse order they were gained. The longer the training period,
the more thorough the adaptation and the longer those biochemical changes persist after
training stops (especially glycogen and CP levels). This gain/loss asymmetry across
different systems is termed heterochronic (different tissues and processes change on
different timelines). Overtraining specifically shows a distinct pattern: reduced
glycogenolytic activity, decreased ascorbic acid/glutathione/glycogen, blood plasma
dysproteinaemia, and reduced sympathico-adrenal function tied to disrupted acid-base
balance. (PDF pp. 96-97, printed pp. 85-86.)

**Why it works** — Different adaptations (enzymatic/oxidative capacity, structural protein
synthesis, phosphagen system capacity) depend on different cellular mechanisms with
different synthesis and degradation rates, so a uniform "the body adapts, then de-adapts"
model misses that some qualities are gained early and lost late, while others are gained
late and lost early.

**When to apply** — **Eric's baseline:** the department's own conditioning material states
that traits easiest to build (aerobic) are lost fastest under detraining, while traits
hardest to build (alactic/speed) are lost slowest, and uses this ordering to sequence how
often each quality needs re-exposure (`method-baseline.md`, "Conditioning and energy
systems," citing `class-4-2026.md p.47`). **Inference:** this converges directionally with
Platonov's heterochronic principle (different systems have different adaptation/de-
adaptation timelines) but the two are not a stated one-to-one match — the baseline's
build-easy/lose-fast claim is about energy-system categories (aerobic vs. alactic), while
Platonov's sequence is about biochemical sub-processes within training generally (oxidative
capacity, structural protein, glycolysis, CP). Treat them as parallel instances of the same
underlying heterochronicity concept, not as the same specific claim restated.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** the specific ordering (oxidative/glycogen, then
structural protein/glycolysis, then CP) is cited to one secondary source (Platonov, 1988)
without the underlying primary data described in this text; do not treat this exact
four-stage sequence as a settled, universally-timed protocol for monitoring detraining. Use
it as a general principle (adaptation and loss are not uniform across systems) rather than
a specific testing checklist.

**Example** — **Inference:** an athlete returning from a multi-week layoff might show
CP-system/power qualities holding up relatively better than the earlier-gained oxidative
and glycogen-storage qualities, or vice versa, depending on the specific loss sequence;
either way, "detraining" is not a single uniform decline and re-testing across qualities
(not just one) gives a more accurate readiness picture.

# Lactate Does Not "Poison" Muscle or Cause Soreness; "Oxygen Debt" Is an Outdated Concept

**What it is** — Two corrections to common misconceptions. (1) Muscle fatigue is not due
to "lactic acid poisoning"; lactate formation is accompanied by hydrogen ions that increase
acidity and can interfere with contraction and enzyme efficiency, but lactate itself is not
the toxin popularly described, and blood lactate returns to normal within about an hour of
strenuous interval training — well before delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) typically
peaks. The most pronounced soreness actually occurs after prolonged endurance efforts kept
below the lactate threshold, so current theory attributes soreness to transient muscle
cell or connective tissue damage, not lactic acid. (2) The classic "oxygen debt" concept
(the idea that post-exercise heavy breathing "repays" an oxygen deficit incurred during
anaerobic work) is now considered outdated; the post-exercise period instead reflects
recovery oxygen consumption used to regenerate ATP and CP and restore respiratory, ionic,
circulatory, hormonal, and thermal systems to baseline (McArdle, Katch & Katch, 1991). The
older term "anaerobic threshold" has similarly been replaced by "blood lactate threshold"
or OBLA (Onset of Blood Lactate Accumulation) since lactate does not require an absence of
oxygen to form. (PDF pp. 87, 89, printed pp. 76, 78.)

**Why it works** — Both corrections reflect the same underlying update in exercise
physiology: lactate is a normal metabolic byproduct and fuel source, not an anaerobic waste
product to be "cleared" or "repaid," and glycolysis runs alongside oxidative metabolism
rather than only replacing it when oxygen is absent (also noted in this book: glycolysis
dominates during sprinting even though muscles have adequate oxygen).

**When to apply** — **Inference:** useful for correcting persistent coaching-room and
classroom misconceptions (e.g. in HHPR 5355 teaching) — "lactic acid causes the burn and
next-day soreness" and "you're paying back oxygen debt after a sprint" are both outdated
framings that this entry can directly correct with citable sources.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** correcting the mechanism does not mean lactate is
irrelevant to training; the blood lactate threshold (OBLA) remains a legitimate, useful
marker for endurance training intensity and athlete conditioning status — only the
"lactic acid as toxin/soreness cause" framing and the "oxygen debt" framing are being
corrected here, not the practical value of lactate-threshold-based training zones.

**Example** — **Inference:** an athlete sore two days after a hard endurance session is
more likely dealing with muscle/connective tissue microtrauma than residual lactate, which
would have cleared within an hour; recovery guidance should be based on that, not on
"flushing out lactic acid."

# Soviet Sport-School Guideline Ages by Sport (Start, Specialize, Peak)

begin specialization, and expect high performance, drawn from the Soviet sports-school
system. Examples: basketball 7-8 / 10-12 / 20-25; gymnastics (women) 4-5 / 10-11 / 14-18;
gymnastics (men) 5-6 / 12-14 / 18-24; swimming 3-7 / 10-12 / 16-18; track-and-field 6-7 /
13-14 / 18-23; weightlifting 10-11 / 17-18 / 21-28; wrestling 10-11 / 15-16 / 24-28 (17
sports listed in total). Early, intense specialization is explicitly discouraged in
strength and endurance sports for children; the average age of Soviet Olympians in the

**Why it works** — **Inference:** the likely underlying rationale matches this library's
existing long-term athletic development material — early specialization in
strength/endurance sports produces faster short-term progress but a lower ultimate ceiling
and more attrition, because the young athlete has not built the broad physical base
specialization later draws on. Verkhoshansky's text does not itself restate this mechanism
in this passage; it presents the table and the age-of-Olympians statistic as its supporting
evidence (PDF p. 36, printed p. 25).

**When to apply** — **Inference:** usable as a more granular, sport-by-sport reference
table alongside this library's existing LTAD guidance. It converges with, and adds numeric
detail beyond, the Bompa library's
[[periodization--bompa/framework--ltad-sequential-model|Long-Term Sequential Development
Model]], which states a general "not before ~14-15" specialization guideline but does not
break it out by individual sport; this convergence is this entry's own comparison, not
stated in either source.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** this is a 20th-century Soviet sports-school table,
not a modern, population-representative dataset; individual biological maturation varies
(see [[periodization--bompa/heuristic--biological-vs-training-age|Biological vs Training
Age]]), and American collegiate sport pathways (e.g. football, not listed in Table 1.2 at
all) do not map cleanly onto these Soviet age bands. Treat the specific ages as
illustrative, not prescriptive, for a Baylor Athletics context; this caution is this
entry's own judgment.

**Example** — **Inference:** The table's general caution against early strength/endurance specialization
supports keeping a young multi-sport athlete on broad physical preparation rather than
early single-sport strength specialization, consistent with existing LTAD guidance already
in this library.

# A Minimum Load Threshold Drives Strength Gains, and It Rises as the Athlete Adapts

**What it is** — Hettinger & Muller (1953) are cited for a threshold training stimulus of
roughly one-third of maximal strength needed to produce a strength increase in an average
person; as strength rises, the required stimulus intensity rises too, eventually reaching
80-95% of the athlete's maximum, sometimes matching or exceeding actual competition
intensity (Verkhoshansky, 1977). Each stimulus level has its own "strengthening
threshold," a ceiling beyond which no further gain occurs at that intensity, and this
ceiling is described as further from the untrained state the less trained the athlete is.
Separately, Korobkov, Gerasimov & Vasiliev (via Verkhoshansky, 1977) are cited for the
finding that in the initial stages of training, strength increases roughly uniformly
regardless of load, with approximately equivalent gains reported at 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%
of 1RM; raising training intensity early (heavier load, faster tempo, less rest) does not
reliably improve on this until strength has already progressed some way. (PDF pp. 33-34,
printed pp. 22-23.)

**Why it works** — Early neuromuscular adaptation (see
[[principle--neuromuscular-stimulation-drives-early-strength-gains]]) responds to almost
any sufficiently repeated stimulus above a low floor; once that easy adaptive window
closes, further progress requires a stimulus that approaches the athlete's current
ceiling, because there is less slack left in recruitment and coordination to exploit.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** supports keeping early-phase (novice) load prescription
simple and consistency-focused rather than chasing high intensities, and supports
progressively raising relative intensity toward 80-95% of max as an athlete's training age
increases, consistent with this book's own training-age-scaling heuristic
([[heuristic--program-variable-complexity-scales-with-training-age]]).

**When NOT to apply** — These are single-source, decades-old figures cited without a
stated sample or modern replication in this text (Hettinger 1961; Korobkov, Gerasimov &
Vasiliev via Verkhoshansky 1977; PDF pp. 33-34, printed pp. 22-23). **Inference:** do not
treat "one-third of max" or the specific 20/40/60/80% equivalence as precise, universal
cutoffs for a current athlete population; use them as a directional argument for why novice
programming can be simple, not as exact percentages to program from.

**Example** — **Inference:** A true beginner's program built around consistent submaximal loading (e.g.
50-60% 1RM) can produce comparable early strength gains to a heavier protocol, so there is
little value in front-loading high intensity before the athlete has trained long enough to
need it.

# High-Velocity Training Transfers to Low Velocity Better Than the Reverse; Contraction Modes Barely Cross Over

**What it is** — Two specific, cited findings under the specificity umbrella: (1)
low-velocity isokinetic training improves low-velocity strength but has minimal effect on
high-velocity strength; high-velocity isokinetic training improves high-velocity strength
and transfers somewhat better to low-velocity strength than the reverse direction
(Moffroid & Whipple 1970; Perrine & Edgerton 1981; Kanehisa & Miyashita 1983b). (2)
Contraction-mode adaptations are narrow: concentric-eccentric (weightlifting-style)
training raises competitive strength but produces only small isometric or concentric
isokinetic gains; isometric training raises isometric strength but not concentric
isokinetic strength; elbow-extension training with weights increases triceps strength and
girth without a matching isokinetic-strength change. Static and dynamic training even
produce different microscopic structural changes in the muscle. (PDF pp. 38-40, printed
pp. 27-29.)

**Why it works** — Force-velocity curve position (see
[[principle--force-velocity-relationship-and-training-specificity]]) and contraction-mode
recruitment patterns are trained narrowly because different neural drive strategies and
structural adaptations underlie each; high-velocity training likely also recruits fast-
twitch units that contribute somewhat at slower speeds, which is the proposed reason the
transfer is asymmetric rather than symmetric.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** when selecting exercise velocity and contraction mode,
match both to the sport action being targeted, and consider biasing toward higher-velocity
work when in doubt, since the text's cited data shows it transfers in both directions
somewhat, whereas slow, heavy work transfers poorly to fast actions. **Eric's baseline:**
the isometric seated calf raise and other isometric testing are run on the ForceDecks
"Mid-Thigh Pull" setting as part of the department's standard protocol
(`method-baseline.md`, "Force plate and neuromuscular monitoring," citing
`protocol-iso-seated-calf-raise.md`, `revised-force-plate-testing-v3.md`). **Inference:**
this claim's contraction-mode specificity finding supports treating isometric testing as
its own distinct quality rather than a proxy for dynamic 1RM strength; neither source states
this connection directly.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference:** these are 1970s-1980s isokinetic-dynamometer
studies (PDF pp. 38-40, printed pp. 27-29); isokinetic machine training is a narrow,
lab-style modality rarely used in current collegiate S&C practice, and this specific
transfer asymmetry has not been independently re-verified here for free-weight or
plyometric training modes. **Inference:** do not assume the same asymmetric-transfer ratio
applies to, say, barbell velocity-based training without separate evidence; this extension
is not addressed in the text.

**Example** — **Inference:** An athlete tested for isometric mid-thigh pull strength should not be assumed
to have an equivalent dynamic 1RM or sprint force capability; each needs its own
assessment and, if targeted, its own specific training exposure.

# The Voluntary-Involuntary Strength Reserve Has a Measured Size

**What it is** — Two cited figures quantify how much force voluntary effort leaves
unused relative to the athlete's true ceiling. (1) In experienced weightlifters, the mean
gap between training maximum (TFmax) and competitive maximum (CFmax) is about 12.5 ± 2.5%,
larger in heavier weight classes (Zatsiorsky, 1995; PDF p. 13, printed p. 2). (2) Under
involuntary or hypnotic conditions, strength output can rise by up to 35% in untrained
people, but by less than 10% in trained athletes (PDF p. 13, printed p. 2, no primary
citation given in text for this figure).

**Why it works** — Both numbers describe the same phenomenon from different angles: a
protective neural inhibition normally holds voluntary output below the muscle's true
contractile ceiling, and anything that reduces that inhibition (competitive arousal,
hypnosis, involuntary reflex recruitment) exposes more of it. Training narrows the
untapped reserve because part of the strength-training adaptation is learning to
recruit and inhibit less, not just growing tissue.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** this is best used as a coaching narrative for why
max-effort numbers move around by context (weigh-in day nerves, meet atmosphere, testing
environment), and as a rough expectation that a smaller gap between training and
competitive/testing efforts is itself a marker of higher training status. Neither the
book nor Eric's baseline states this as an applied rule; it is this entry's own synthesis
of the two cited numbers.

**When NOT to apply** — Verkhoshansky gives no primary citation for the hypnosis figure
(PDF p. 13, printed p. 2); treat it as a single, unverified secondary claim, not a number
to plan around. The 12.5 ± 2.5% figure is stated specifically for experienced Olympic
weightlifters under meet conditions (Zatsiorsky, 1995; PDF p. 13, printed p. 2).
**Inference:** it should not be assumed to transfer directly to Baylor's team-sport
athletes tested on a force plate (ForceDecks CMJ/IMTP) rather than in a weightlifting
competition, and neither number should be used as a correction factor applied to that data
without independent verification; this caution is this entry's own judgment, not stated in
`method-baseline.md`.

**Example** — **Inference:** An athlete who tests near-identically on a submaximal VBT-estimated 1RM and
a rare true max attempt is showing a small reserve gap; a large, inconsistent gap between
estimated and tested max may point to arousal/readiness on test day rather than a change
in underlying capacity.
