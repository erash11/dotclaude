# Thoracic Outlet Syndrome in Overhead Throwing Athletes

**An evidence brief for softball performance staff.**

Prepared 2026-09-18. Every citation in this document was verified against PubMed
(NCBI E-utilities), Europe PMC, or the publisher record before inclusion.
Sources from the Applied Performance Zotero library are marked
`[Zotero: KEY, p. N]`. All others are marked `[External]` and are peer-reviewed
primary sources or systematic reviews unless stated otherwise.

> **How to read the confidence labels.** This brief separates what is
> **Established** (measured, replicated), **Plausible but unproven** (a coherent
> mechanism with no direct test), and **Speculation** (asserted in practice,
> unsupported in literature). The distinction matters. A claim presented as
> stronger than it is will not survive a second opinion, and it will cost the
> person making it credibility on the claims that *are* solid.

---

## Bottom line up front

1. **The peer comparison cannot be made.** TOS is not separately coded in the
   NCAA Injury Surveillance Program. There is no national softball TOS rate to
   compare against. (§1.4)
2. **TOS is rare enough that no single program can estimate its own rate.**
   Population incidence is roughly **2–3 per 100,000 per year** for neurogenic
   TOS. On a 35-athlete roster, expected cases per season is about **0.0014**.
   Two cases is a plausible chance cluster, not a signal. (§1.5)
3. **Diagnosis drives counts at least as much as biology does.** There is no
   gold-standard test for neurogenic TOS. The provocative test most used in an
   athletic training room is **positive in 47–62% of people who do not have
   TOS**. A program that starts looking will find cases; one that does not,
   will not. (§1.3)
4. **The throwing motion is the exposure, and the histology does not implicate
   the weight room.** The cocked position roughly doubles scalene stiffness. The
   scalene muscle changes found in TOS patients are type II *atrophy* plus
   fibrosis — the opposite of a resistance-training signature. (§2.4, §2.5)
5. **"Don't lift before throwing" has no research support**, and the adjacent
   evidence leans against it. The defensible version is much narrower. (§4)
6. **The endurance hypothesis is coherent and worth acting on, but it is not
   established.** The best supporting study found a 45% rotator endurance
   deficit in TOS patients — in 40-year-old non-athletes, with the direction of
   causation unresolved. (§3)
7. **The strongest modifiable exposure is workload, and it is not the strength
   coach's lever.** Collegiate softball pitchers throw **2.9× the innings of
   baseball pitchers on a staff one-third the size**, under no pitch count limit
   and no mandated rest — and about **half of true pitch volume is bullpen and
   warm-up that nobody counts**. (§5)

## Section 1 — What TOS is, how common it is, and why the peer comparison fails

### 1.1 Three conditions, not one

TOS is compression of the brachial plexus, subclavian artery, or subclavian vein
in one of three anatomical spaces. The subtypes behave very differently and
should never be discussed as a single entity.

| Subtype | General population | Athletes |
|---|---|---|
| **Neurogenic (nTOS)** | 82–95% | 66–74% |
| **Venous (vTOS / Paget-Schroetter, effort thrombosis)** | 3–16% | **26–34%** |
| **Arterial (aTOS)** | ~1–2% | ~2% (1 of 52 in the largest pro series) |

`[External]` Illig KA, et al. *Ann Vasc Surg* 2021;70:263-72. PMID 32771464 — 526 consecutive referrals: 82% nTOS, 16% vTOS, 2% aTOS.
`[External]` Chandra V, Little C, Lee JT. *J Vasc Surg* 2014;60(4):1012-7. PMID 24835692 — 41 competitive athletes: 66% nTOS, 34% Paget-Schroetter.
`[External]` Chauhan A, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2024;52(7):1692-9. PMID 38702964 — 52 pro baseball players: 69% neurogenic, 29% venous.
`[External]` Gutman MJ, et al. *Phys Sportsmed* 2022;50(2):141-6. PMID 33472488 — 27 MLB pitchers: 74% nTOS, 26% vTOS.

**Established.** Venous TOS is roughly **5- to 10-fold over-represented in
athletes** relative to the general population. The mechanism is chronic
repetitive compression of the subclavian vein in the costoclavicular space.
`[External]` Vemuri C, et al. *J Vasc Surg Venous Lymphat Disord* 2016;4(4):485-500. PMID 27639006.

**Caveat:** every athlete proportion above is a *referral-center or treated-case*
proportion, not an incidence. They describe the mix among athletes who reached a
TOS surgeon — not the mix among athletes who have TOS.

### 1.2 The base rate — and a statistic to stop repeating

**Do not use "3–80 per 1,000."** It is the most commonly cited TOS incidence
figure and it does not survive tracing:

1. StatPearls (where most clinicians land) says "3 to 80 cases per 1,000," citing —
2. Jones MR, et al. *Pain Ther* 2019;8(1):5-18 (PMID 31037504), which says
   "Several articles report an incidence of 3–80/1000 [4]," citing —
3. Citisli V. *J Pain Relief* 2015;4(2):1-7 — **which is not indexed in
   PubMed/MEDLINE at all.** A PubMed author search returns zero results.

A 27-fold-wide range is itself the tell that nobody measured anything.

**What the actual data say.** `[External]` Illig KA, Rodriguez-Zoppi E.
*Thorac Surg Clin* 2021;31(1):11-17. PMID 33220767, opening line: *"The incidence
of neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome is completely unknown, and has been wildly
overestimated in the past."* Their prospective referral database back-calculates:

- **nTOS ≈ 2–3 per 100,000 per year**
- **vTOS ≈ 0.5–1 per 100,000 per year**
- **aTOS sporadic**

The discrepancy between the folklore figure and the measured one is **100-fold
to 2,700-fold**.

### 1.3 There is no gold-standard test — and the field says so in its own documents

**Established, and this is the most defensible section of the brief.**

- The **SVS reporting standards** define nTOS as ≥3 of 4 criteria, one of which
  is *absence of other pathology explaining the symptoms* (a diagnosis of
  exclusion) and one of which is *response to a scalene block* (a therapeutic
  trial, not a test). No imaging, electrodiagnostic, or laboratory criterion
  exists. `[External]` Illig KA, et al. *J Vasc Surg* 2016;64(3):e23-35. PMID 27565607.
- `[External]` Sanders RJ, Hammond SL, Rao NM. *Neurologist* 2008;14(6):365-73. PMID 19008742:
  *"there is no standard objective test to confirm clinical impressions... To
  date there is still no reliable objective test to confirm the diagnosis."*
- **Cochrane:** *"TOS is one of the most controversial diagnoses in clinical
  medicine."* The review *"had to rely exclusively on the diagnosis of TOS by the
  investigators in the reviewed studies."* Only **two RCTs worldwide** met a
  6-month follow-up bar. `[External]` Povlsen B, Hansson T, Povlsen SD. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2014;(11):CD007218. PMID 25427003.
- **Wilbourn vs Roos, *Muscle Nerve* 1999** — "TOS is overdiagnosed" and "TOS is
  underdiagnosed," published back to back in the same issue of the same journal.
  `[External]` PMID 9883870 / PMID 9883869.
- Even at a **dedicated TOS center**, only **150 of 183 (82%)** of new nTOS
  referrals met an established criteria set. `[External]` Balderman J, et al. *J Vasc Surg* 2017;66(2):533-544.e2. PMID 28735950.
- A Delphi expert consensus published in 2026 — a decade after the SVS standards —
  exists precisely because criteria remain unsettled. **Its fifth criterion is
  "participation in work/exercise requiring repetitive arm stress (overhead
  athletes, throwing sports)."** `[External]` Wagner ER, et al. *Orthop J Sports Med* 2026;14(5):23259671261440925. PMID 42169800.

  **Read that last one twice.** Being an overhead athlete is itself a formal
  diagnostic criterion for nTOS. For a program comparing its softball TOS rate
  to peers, that is circular: athlete status is an *input* to the diagnosis.

**False-positive rates of provocative tests in people who do not have TOS:**

| Test | Rayan 1995 (200 asymptomatic limbs) | Plewa 1998 (53 healthy) | Nord 2008 (normals) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adson's | 13.5% vascular / 2% neuro | 11% pulse | 9–20% |
| Costoclavicular | 47% vascular / 10% neuro | 11% pulse / 15% paresthesia | 16% |
| Wright's / hyperabduction | **57% vascular** / 16.5% neuro | — | — |
| **Roos / EAST** | — | **62% pulse, 36% paresthesia, 21% pain** | **47%** |
| **≥1 test positive** | — | — | **56% of normals** |

`[External]` Rayan GM, Jensen C. *J Shoulder Elbow Surg* 1995;4(2):113-7. PMID 7600161.
`[External]` Plewa MC, Delinger M. *Acad Emerg Med* 1998;5(4):337-42. PMID 9562199.
`[External]` Nord KM, et al. *Electromyogr Clin Neurophysiol* 2008;48(2):67-74. PMID 18435210.
`[External]` Warrens AN, Heaton JM. *Ann R Coll Surg Engl* 1987;69(5):203-4. PMID 3674682 — 58% of volunteers positive on ≥1 maneuver, in a group where only 17% had any symptoms.

Nord 2008 adds the decisive number: in **carpal tunnel patients** — who by
definition do not have TOS — Roos/EAST was positive in **77%**, and **94% had at
least one positive TOS maneuver.** Gillard 2001 reduces it to one figure:
provocative tests have **mean specificity 53%**. `[External]` Gillard J, et al. *Joint Bone Spine* 2001;68(5):416-24. PMID 11707008.

**Practical implication.** The Roos/EAST test — the one most likely to be used in
an athletic training room — is positive in roughly **half of people who do not
have TOS**. Screening a 35-athlete roster with EAST would be expected to flag
16–20 healthy athletes.

### 1.4 TOS is not coded in the NCAA ISP — the peer comparison cannot be made

**Established.**

- The ISP data model releases **body part or system affected**, **specific
  injury**, and **type of injury**. The body-part list is coarse and anatomical:
  head/face, neck, shoulder, arm or elbow, hand/wrist, trunk, hip/groin, thigh,
  knee, lower leg, ankle, foot, other. **There is no "thoracic outlet" site and
  no vascular or peripheral-nerve category.** `[External]` Kerr ZY, et al. *J Athl Train* 2014;49(4):552-60. PMID 24870292.
- The diagnosis taxonomy is **injury-type** based — strains, sprains, contusions,
  inflammatory conditions, concussion — not disease-entity based.
- **TOS appears nowhere** in the two most recent 5-year NCAA baseball and
  softball epidemiology reports, or in the decade-long report.
  `[External]` Boltz AJ, et al. *J Athl Train* 2021;56(7):742-9. PMID 34280265. / Veillard KL, et al. *J Athl Train* 2021;56(7):734-41. PMID 34280289. / Wasserman EB, et al. *J Athl Train* 2019;54(2):198-211. PMID 30951387.
- The ISP denominator is **athlete-exposures, not athletes or rosters**, drawn
  from a **convenience sample** — an annual average of ~34 collegiate baseball
  teams — which the authors themselves warn "should be interpreted with caution."

**Honest limit:** the Datalys ISP documentation page returned HTTP 404, so I
cannot prove no internal code string exists. What is demonstrable: TOS is not
*reported* in any published NCAA ISP baseball or softball output and is not in
the released taxonomies. A program wanting certainty should submit a data
request to the Datalys Center.

**The one usable collegiate denominator** comes from a chart review, not the ISP:
`[External]` Talutis SD, et al. *J Vasc Surg* 2025;82(1):155-62. PMID 40132734 —
**15,609 NCAA Division I athletes, 21 sports, 7 years, 76 TOS cases (0.48%).**

| Sport | TOS prevalence |
|---|---|
| Tennis | 2.6% |
| **Softball** | **1.3%** |
| Rowing | 1.3% |
| All sports | 0.48% |

90.8% neurogenic, 9.2% venous. **96.0% managed without surgery; 95.4% returned
to unrestricted competition.** Softball sits above the all-sport baseline but
below tennis — and the outcomes are good.

### 1.5 Why a roster-level comparison is statistically meaningless

Using Illig's estimate of roughly 4 per 100,000 per year for all TOS combined, a
35-athlete roster observed for one season has an expected count of **0.0014
cases**.

- **Zero cases tells you nothing.** By the rule of three, 35 athlete-years with
  zero events puts the 95% upper bound at 3/35 ≈ **8.6% per athlete-season** —
  four orders of magnitude above the true rate. A program with no cases has no
  evidence it is below average. `[External]` Hanley JA, Lippman-Hand A. *JAMA* 1983;249(13):1743-5. PMID 6827763.
- **One case looks catastrophic and means almost nothing.** The exact Poisson 95%
  CI on a single observed event is (0.03, 5.6) events. The interval admits
  essentially any hypothesis.
- **Two cases is a plausible chance cluster.** Poisson 95% CI on 2 events:
  (0.24, 7.2). A peer program with 0 and a peer program with 3 are not
  statistically distinguishable at these counts.
- **Ascertainment dominates everything.** 56% of normals screen positive on at
  least one provocative maneuver, and being an overhead athlete is a formal
  diagnostic criterion. Any observed between-program difference is far more
  likely to measure the **referral pathway** — whether the school has a
  relationship with a TOS center — than the underlying biology.

**The framing to take into the room:** TOS is rare enough that no single athletic
program generates a sample large enough to estimate its own rate, let alone
compare it to a peer's. The meaningful question is not *"is our rate high?"* but
*"is our diagnostic and referral pathway appropriate, and are our modifiable
exposures managed?"*

### 1.6 Outcomes, for reassurance

Five independent professional-baseball return-to-play studies exist, and they
converge tightly: **74–81% return to play at 9–11 months.**

| Study | n | RTP | Performance conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thompson 2017 | 13 MLB pitchers | 77% at 10.8 mo | 75% of 72 PitchF/x metrics unchanged; max velocity −0.6 mph (P=.047) |
| Gutman 2022 | 27 MLB pitchers | 74% at 297 d | ERA worse (3.66→4.50, P=.03); velocity unchanged |
| Arnold 2022 | 26 MLB pitchers | 81% at 10 mo | No difference vs matched controls |
| **Chauhan 2024** | 52 pro (88% pitchers) | **79% at 9.5 mo** | **Declines present, but no faster than matched healthy controls aging normally** |

`[External]` Thompson RW, et al. *Ann Vasc Surg* 2017;39:216-27. PMID 27522980. / Gutman MJ, et al. PMID 33472488. / Arnold MT, et al. *Orthop J Sports Med* 2022;10(4):23259671221079835. PMID 35494495. / Chauhan A, et al. PMID 38702964.

**The narrative that TOS surgery collapses a pitcher's career is supported by
exactly one study** (Gutman's unmatched pre/post ERA comparison) **and
contradicted by the two that used matched controls.** Chauhan 2024 — the only one
using MLB's internal Health and Injury Tracking System rather than press reports —
concluded the decline after TOS surgery was no faster than in healthy pitchers
aging normally. An unmatched pre/post design on a cohort with mean age ~29–30
will manufacture a decline out of ordinary aging.

Funakoshi 2024, the largest overhead-athlete series (79 athletes: **baseball 67,
tennis 4, softball 3, volleyball 3, swimming 1, handball 1**), reported **97%
return to play and 81% return to the same level** at 7.3 ± 5.1 months, with
**91.1% excellent/good** Roos scores in athletes vs 62.8% in non-athletes.
`[External]` Funakoshi T, et al. *JSES Int* 2024;8(3):620-9. PMID 38707577.

## Section 2 — What actually causes TOS in throwing athletes

### 2.1 The three compression spaces

**Established.**

| Space | Boundaries | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Interscalene triangle** | Anterior scalene (front), middle scalene (back), first rib (below) | Brachial plexus **trunks**, subclavian **artery**. The subclavian vein passes *anterior* to the anterior scalene and is not in this triangle. |
| **Costoclavicular space** | Clavicle and subclavius (above), first rib (below) | Plexus **divisions**, subclavian artery **and vein** |
| **Subcoracoid / retropectoralis minor space** | Pectoralis minor and its coracoid insertion (front), chest wall (behind) | Plexus **cords**, axillary artery and vein |

For **arterial** compression the costoclavicular space is the discriminating
site. Demondion 2006 imaged 44 volunteers and 28 arterial-TOS patients at 0°,
90°, 130° and 170° abduction: no significant arterial stenosis in the
interscalene triangle or retropectoralis minor space in either group; only the
costoclavicular space separated patients from volunteers, at every angle, with
**130° hyperabduction most discriminating**. `[External]` Demondion X, et al. *J Ultrasound Med* 2006;25(2):217-24. PMID 16439785.

The common claim that the **subcoracoid space is the space most implicated in
throwers** is **plausible but unproven** — no study compares the three spaces
head-to-head in throwing athletes. It rests on a general surgical population
(>75% of nTOS patients also had pectoralis minor syndrome; ~30% had it alone)
plus expert opinion. `[External]` Sanders RJ, Rao NM. *Ann Vasc Surg* 2010;24(6):701-8. PMID 20471786.

### 2.2 Structural predisposition — common, and usually silent

**Established.**

- **Cervical rib: 0.74%** of 1,352 London chest radiographs (females 1.09%,
  males 0.42%); corroborated at **0.7%** in 6,571 Nigerian radiographs.
  `[External]` Brewin J, Hill M, Ellis H. *Clin Anat* 2009;22(3):331-6. PMID 19280652. / Ezeofor SN, et al. *Niger J Clin Pract* 2016;19(4):513-6. PMID 27251970.
- **Elongated C7 transverse process: 2.21%** — about three times more common than
  a cervical rib, and the more likely bony contributor in any given athlete.
- **Fibromuscular bands.** Roos described **seven** types in 1976 and **nine** by
  1980 across 1,120 operations. **One third of 58 cadaver thoracic outlets showed
  at least one** — in people who never had symptoms. `[External]` Roos DB. *Am J Surg* 1976;132(6):771-8. PMID 998867. / *Acta Chir Belg* 1980;79(5):353-61. PMID 7223264.
  - *The frequently cited "Roos described 14 types" appears in no Roos primary
    source. Cite nine (1980). Do not repeat the 14 figure.*
- **Scalene anatomy is highly variable.** The textbook arrangement — plexus
  passing between anterior and middle scalene — was present in **only 60%** of 51
  cadavers. `[External]` Harry WG, Bennett JD, Guha SC. *Clin Anat* 1997;10(4):250-2. PMID 9213042.
- **In TOS surgical series anomalies are the norm:** 66% of 200 consecutive cases
  had developmental abnormalities — 20 supernumerary scalenes, 86 scalene and 39
  subclavius variations. `[External]` Makhoul RG, Machleder HI. *J Vasc Surg* 1992;16(4):534-42. PMID 1404675.

**The honest caveat, and it matters.** Predisposition is not causation. Roos
found his bands in a third of *asymptomatic* cadavers. Makhoul & Machleder
concluded morphology alone predicted the clinical syndrome *only* for
Paget-Schroetter. The defensible framing is: **structural narrowing is common,
usually silent, and becomes symptomatic when loaded.** That makes TOS a screening
and load-management problem — not an act of God, and not an exoneration.

### 2.3 First rib stress fracture — a throwing injury inside the outlet

**Established.** 24 first-rib stress fractures in overhead throwing athletes,
mean age 16.8 years, dominant arm, presenting as posterior shoulder pain on
throwing — and "some patients may have symptoms consistent with thoracic outlet
syndrome." `[External]` Funakoshi T, et al. *J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2019;101(10):896-903. PMID 31094981.

Callus after such a fracture has caused frank arterial TOS with cerebellar
infarction in a baseball player. `[External]` Suehara Y, et al. *JBJS Case Connect* 2017;7(3):e64. PMID 29252893.

A 2025 case in a 12-year-old elite pitcher fractured at the **serratus anterior
attachment** and presented as scapular winging and snapping — i.e. a first-rib
stress fracture can masquerade as exactly the scapular control problem a strength
coach would be asked to fix. `[External]` Yoon Y, et al. *Diagnostics (Basel)* 2025;15(19):2437. PMID 41095656.

### 2.4 The scalene histology — why the weight room is the wrong suspect

**Established, and the single most useful finding in this brief.**

Two independent biopsy series of scalene muscles taken at surgery from TOS
patients found the same pattern:

| Finding | Machleder 1986 | Sanders 1990 |
|---|---|---|
| Type I (slow/tonic) fiber predominance | **85.1% ± 5.1%** | **78% vs 53%** in controls |
| Type II fibers | — | **Atrophied** |
| Connective tissue (fibrosis) | — | **36% vs <15%** in controls |

`[External]` Machleder HI, Moll F, Verity MA. *Arch Surg* 1986;121(10):1141-4. PMID 3767646.
`[External]` Sanders RJ, Jackson CG, Banchero N, Pearce WH. *Am J Surg* 1990;159(2):231-6. PMID 2301718.

Machleder's interpretation: the anterior scalene "demonstrates an extraordinary
adaptive transformation and recruitment response in the type 1 fiber system
reflecting chronic increased tone or motor neuron stimulation."

**Why this matters.** Resistance training produces **type II hypertrophy**. The
scalene muscle in TOS shows **type II atrophy plus fibrosis and type I
predominance**. That is the signature of chronic sustained tone and of injury —
not of a training adaptation. If anyone argues that weight-room hypertrophy built
the compressing muscle, the histology does not support it.

Treat the *pattern* as established and the *exact percentages* as study-specific;
both are surgical biopsy series with convenience controls.

### 2.5 Throwing loads the outlet — measurably

**Established.** Shear wave elastography on 30 collegiate baseball pitchers:

| Position | Anterior scalene | Middle scalene |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral | 9.4 ± 2.0 kPa | 10.9 ± 3.4 kPa |
| **ABER (cocked)** | **14.4 ± 3.8 kPa** (P<.001) | **15.8 ± 5.0 kPa** (P<.001) |
| ABER + neck rotation away | **18.1 ± 4.6 kPa** | 15.8 ± 2.6 kPa |

`[External]` Takaba K, et al. *Orthop J Sports Med* 2022;10(8):23259671221114930. PMID 36003968.

The cocked throwing position roughly **doubles** anterior scalene stiffness. This
is the cleanest mechanistic link between the throwing motion itself and loading
of the interscalene triangle. It measures muscle stiffness, not nerve
compression — but it locates the exposure in the throw.

**Corroborating anatomy from the largest athlete series:** mean interscalene
distance in TOS patients **5.6 ± 3.6 mm**, against cadaveric norms of 10.7–15 mm;
and **"hypertrophy of the serratus anterior and posterior scalene muscles was
commonly observed in overhead athletes."** `[External]` Funakoshi T, et al. *JSES Int* 2024;8(3):620-9. PMID 38707577.

*Note which muscles: posterior scalene and serratus anterior. Those are
throwing-driven, not bench-press-driven. This finding is the one a critic could
reach for, and it should be presented honestly and in context rather than
omitted.*

### 2.6 Positional compression is normal — which limits what a positive test means

**Established.** MR imaging of 35 asymptomatic volunteers and 54 TOS patients in
neutral and provoked positions:

- **47% of asymptomatic volunteers** showed positional venous compression at the
  prescalene level (vs 63% of patients); **54% vs 61%** costoclavicular.
- **Arterial compression: 72% of patients, 0% of volunteers.**
- **Nerve compression: 7% of patients, 0% of volunteers.**
- "Except for venous thrombosis, vasculonervous compressions were demonstrated
  only with arm elevation."

`[External]` Demondion X, et al. *Radiology* 2003;227(2):461-8. PMID 12637678.

Compression is a positional phenomenon — it does not exist at rest. And roughly
half of healthy people compress when you put their arm overhead. Only arterial
and nerve compression discriminated.

A separate MRI study confirms the spaces are real, measurable and
position-dependent, with significant positional change in patients and none in
controls. `[External]` Demirbag D, et al. *Arch Phys Med Rehabil* 2007;88(7):844-51. PMID 17601463.

### 2.7 Posture and scapular position

**Established, with an important correction.** "Droopy shoulder syndrome" is
frequently described as costoclavicular narrowing. It is not. Swift & Nichols
described low-set shoulders and a long neck, with symptoms that **worsened with
downward traction and improved when patients elevated their arms** — the opposite
of what compression of a narrowed costoclavicular space predicts. This is a
**brachial plexus traction** mechanism. `[External]` Swift TR, Nichols FT. *Neurology* 1984;34(2):212-5. PMID 6538012.

**Established in healthy non-athletes.** A short pectoralis minor resting length
is associated with a scapula that stays **anteriorly tipped** at higher elevation
angles and is **more internally rotated** at lower angles (50 asymptomatic
volunteers, electromagnetic motion capture). `[External]` Borstad JD, Ludewig PM. *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2005;35(4):227-38. PMID 15901124.

Pectoralis minor length can be measured reliably in the field (coracoid to 4th
rib/sternocostal junction; intrarater ICC 0.86–0.97, between-day 0.95).
`[External]` Borstad JD. *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2008;38(4):169-74. PMID 18434665. / Rosa DP, et al. *Braz J Phys Ther* 2016;20(2):176-83. PMID 26982455.
**No validated numeric cut-off for "short" exists — report the measurement, not a threshold.**

**Scope caveat:** this work was done in healthy, asymptomatic, non-athlete adults
and targeted subacromial impingement, not TOS. Extending it to throwers *and*
from impingement to subcoracoid neurovascular compression is a two-step
inference. Reasonable, but **plausible-unproven**.

Scapular dyskinesis on the throwing side was commonly documented by 3D-CT in the
Funakoshi athlete series — descriptive, not causal.

### 2.8 The cervical spine contribution — and a finding from our own library

**Established in college pitchers.** 49 healthy college baseball pitchers
measured in the 2018 preseason and followed through the season. A Cervical
Flexion-Rotation Test finding of ≤39° on the dominant side carried **9.4× the
risk** of a >7-day time-loss shoulder or elbow injury (RR 9.38, 95% CI
1.28–68.49). Cervical flexion ROM ≤64° carried RR 10.56 (95% CI 1.50–74.34) for
pain and disability. Pitchers with restricted rotation were injured earlier
(83.6 of 112 days played vs 109.4).
`[Zotero: F5YKF6B4, p. 1]` Devaney LL, et al. *Orthop J Sports Med* 2020;8(5):2325967120920556. DOI 10.1177/2325967120920556.

Proposed mechanism, `[Zotero: F5YKF6B4, p. 6]`: reduced upper cervical rotation
requires compensatory mid-cervical motion, and "these combined motions may
decrease the space for the nerve roots in the intervertebral foramina" with
consequent myotomal changes impairing scapular and glenohumeral control.

**Read this carefully.** Devaney is *not* a TOS study — it measured shoulder and
elbow injury. But it establishes, in exactly our population, that **neck mobility
is a modifiable, screenable risk factor for throwing injury**, with a mechanism
adjacent to the TOS story. The wide confidence intervals (n=49, 10 injuries) mean
the 9.4× point estimate should not be quoted as precise; the *direction* and the
screening implication are the usable findings.

**Plausible but unquantified:** no study quantifies cervical foraminal narrowing
as a contributor to nTOS specifically in throwers.

### 2.9 What the literature does *not* support

- **GIRD → TOS: no evidence whatsoever.** Three separate PubMed queries return
  **zero** records. Any such link is speculation. Do not assert it.
- **Resistance training causes TOS: unsupported.** No controlled or cohort
  evidence. What exists is case reports — a weightlifter with Paget-Schroetter
  attributed to bench pressing; a rower/weightlifter with well-developed
  scalenes; a weightlifter with subclavian occlusion *confounded by anabolic
  steroid use*. `[External]` Crossland DT, Overturf MD. *Cureus* 2024;16(6):e62824. PMID 39036114. / Wong DKH, et al. *JBJS Case Connect* 2021;11(2). PMID 33882031. / Meller L, et al. *Cureus* 2023;15(4):e37763. PMID 37213978.
- The closest thing to support for a hypertrophy mechanism in a thrower is a
  Division I pitcher with "intermittent axillary artery compression with the arm
  in abduction resulting from hypertrophy of the pectoralis minor and scalene
  muscles" — attributed to **throwing**, not to a documented lifting programme.
  `[External]` Ligh CA, Schulman BL, Safran MR. *Clin Orthop Relat Res* 2009;467(10):2744-8. PMID 19588212.
- A 2022 systematic review of TOS in sport located 78 articles: **40 case
  reports, 10 clinical studies, 28 reviews**, concluding the field "still lacks
  rigorous diagnostic criteria and management guidelines for athletes." That
  composition *is* the finding. `[External]` Garraud T, et al. *Front Physiol* 2022;13:838014. PMID 35755427.

**The defensible statement:** pectoralis minor and scalene hypertrophy have been
documented as the compressing structure in individual athlete case reports, in
throwing, water polo, rowing and weightlifting. **Whether training-induced
hypertrophy raises TOS risk at a population level has never been tested.**

## Section 3 — Can lack of endurance or muscle capacity cause TOS?

**Direct answer: it is a coherent, mechanistically reasonable hypothesis with no
direct empirical support. Act on it because it is cheap, safe, and independently
justified — not because the evidence establishes it.**

### 3.1 The strongest supporting study

**Established finding, contested interpretation.** 100 nTOS patients vs 100
healthy controls, isokinetic shoulder rotator testing, with an endurance test
defined as 30 repetitions at 180°/s:

| Measure | Medial rotators | Lateral rotators |
|---|---|---|
| Peak strength 60°/s | −21% | −29% |
| Peak strength 180°/s | −23% | −20% |
| **Endurance (30 reps @ 180°/s)** | **−45%** | **−30%** |

All p≤.001, all correlated with QuickDASH. **The endurance deficit is roughly
twice the strength deficit** — the single most striking number in this review.
`[External]` Daley P, et al. *Diagnostics (Basel)* 2021;11(9):1529. PMID 34573871.

**Four constraints block a causal reading. State all four or the claim will not
survive scrutiny:**

1. **Direction of causation is unresolvable by this design.** nTOS *is* brachial
   plexus compression. Reduced fatigue resistance is at least as plausibly a
   *consequence* — partial denervation, pain inhibition, disuse, kinesiophobia —
   as a cause. A cross-sectional symptomatic-vs-control design cannot separate
   them. The authors make no causal claim; they frame it as a diagnostic and
   follow-up tool.
2. **Wrong muscles.** This measured **glenohumeral rotators** — not scapular
   stabilisers, not deep neck flexors, not postural muscles.
3. **Wrong population.** 71% female, mean age 39.4 ± 9.6, clinic population.
   **Every direct capacity study in TOS is a non-athlete, predominantly female,
   ~40-year-old clinic cohort.** Transfer to a 20-year-old pitcher is assumption.
4. **The companion study argues for consequence.** Grip **−30%**, key pinch
   **−19.5%** vs controls — the classic C8–T1 lower-plexus signature. That
   pattern points to nerve compression producing weakness, not the reverse.
   `[External]` Fouasson-Chailloux A, et al. *Diagnostics (Basel)* 2021;11(5):874. PMID 34068245.

### 3.2 The outright evidentiary blanks

- **Scapular stabiliser endurance in TOS vs controls: no study exists.**
- **Deep neck flexor endurance in TOS: zero studies.** Complete blank.
- **EMG evidence of scalene overactivity during breathing in TOS: none.** The
  "accessory breathing → interscalene narrowing" chain is untested. The one
  respiratory study found TOS patients had *reduced* MIP, MEP and respiratory
  muscle endurance — which if anything is the opposite of the hyperactive-
  accessory-breather story, and the authors decline a causal direction.
  `[External]` Saglam M, et al. *J Manipulative Physiol Ther* 2020;43(6):606-11. PMID 32829949.
- **Nobody has ever measured costoclavicular or retropectoralis minor space
  before and after a fatigue protocol.** That single experiment would convert
  this from plausibility to mechanism. It has not been run.
- **No RCT of exercise therapy versus control for nTOS exists.** The only nTOS
  RCT compared surgery to continued conservative care in patients already
  refractory to conservative therapy — and all 25 conservative-arm patients
  applied for surgery at 3 months. `[External]` Goeteyn J, et al. *Eur J Vasc Endovasc Surg* 2022;64(1):119-27. PMID 35537641.

### 3.3 The indirect chain — fatigue alters scapular position

**Established (in healthy subjects, acute lab fatigue, framed around subacromial
impingement — not the thoracic outlet):**

| Study | n | Fatigue protocol | Scapular result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tsai 2003 | 30 healthy | External rotation | ↓ posterior tilt, ↓ external rotation, ↓ upward rotation |
| Ebaugh 2006 (JOSPT) | 20 healthy | External rotation | ↓ posterior tilt early; **↑** upward rotation mid-range |
| Ebaugh 2006 (JEK) | 20 healthy | Repetitive overhead | **↑** upward and external rotation |
| Joshi 2011 | 25 overhead athletes | GH external rotation | ↓ lower trapezius activity, **↑** upward rotation |
| Harrison 2021 | 30 | Serratus anterior | ↓ posterior tilt, ↑ internal rotation, no change in upward rotation |

`[External]` Tsai NT, McClure PW, Karduna AR. *Arch Phys Med Rehabil* 2003;84(7):1000-5. PMID 12881824. / Ebaugh DD, et al. *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2006;36(8):557-71. PMID 16915977; *J Electromyogr Kinesiol* 2006;16(3):224-35. PMID 16125416. / Joshi M, et al. *J Athl Train* 2011;46(4):349-57. PMID 21944066. / Harrison N, Garrett WZ, Timmons MK. *J Sport Rehabil* 2021;30(8):1151-7. PMID 34294607.

**Two honest observations:**

- **What replicates is decreased posterior tilt (increased anterior tilt).** Four
  of five studies show it. Combined with increased internal rotation, that is the
  consistent fatigue signature.
- **Upward rotation does not replicate.** One study found it decreased, three
  found it *increased*, one found no change. **Do not assert a consistent
  upward-rotation deficit.**

**The inferred chain — label it as inferred:**

> scapular muscle fatigue → increased anterior tilt and internal rotation
> *(measured, replicated)* → coracoid moves inferoanteriorly, clavicle approaches
> the first rib *(anatomically reasoned)* → narrowing of the costoclavicular and
> subcoracoid spaces *(spaces are real and position-dependent, but never measured
> under fatigue)* → brachial plexus compression *(never demonstrated)*

### 3.4 What the treatment protocols actually target

A 2026 scoping review screened 1,381 studies and included 29. Exercise appeared
in 17/19 (90%) of programmes:

- **Stretching (n=15).** Main targets: **scalenes and pectoralis (n=10)**.
- **Strengthening (n=14).** Main targets: **scapula (n=9), trapezius and serratus
  anterior (n=5)**.
- Posture (n=13), manual therapy (n=10), neural mobility (n=7), activity
  modification (n=7).

`[External]` O'Sullivan J, et al. *Hand Ther* 2026;17589983251411877. PMID 41657761.

**So the premise is confirmed at the level of muscle selection — and fails at the
level of muscle quality.** The review's own conclusion: *"The reporting of
rehabilitation techniques for NTOS is generally poor, particularly regarding
treatment intensity."* **No published nTOS protocol specifies sets, reps, loads
or time-under-tension in a way that would let you classify it as endurance
training.** Anyone asserting "the protocols target endurance specifically" is
reading intent into silence.

The canonical masterclass describes the core as *"the graded restoration of
scapula control, movement, and positioning at rest and through movement"* — that
is **motor-control language, not endurance language**. `[External]` Watson LA, Pizzari T, Balster S. *Man Ther* 2010;15(4):305-14. PMID 20382063.

**Conservative success rates span a wide, selection-driven range:**

| Study | n | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Novak 1995 | 42 | 25 improved / 10 same / 7 worse (~60%); only 16/42 back to full work + recreation |
| Lindgren 1997 | 119 | 88% satisfied at ~25 mo; 73% returned to work (uncontrolled, subjective endpoint) |
| **Balderman 2019** | 150 | **Only 40 (27%) satisfactory with PT alone**; 60% went to surgery |
| Fouasson-Chailloux 2025 | 182 | 3-week inpatient rehab: **rotator endurance +35–39%**, QuickDASH 57→30 (uncontrolled) |

`[External]` Novak CB, Collins ED, Mackinnon SE. *J Hand Surg Am* 1995;20(4):542-8. PMID 7594276. / Lindgren KA. *Arch Phys Med Rehabil* 1997;78(4):373-8. PMID 9111456. / Balderman J, et al. *J Vasc Surg* 2019;70(3):832-41. PMID 30852035. / Fouasson-Chailloux A, et al. *Ann Vasc Surg* 2025;121:603-12. PMID 40907821.

27% and 88% are not in conflict — they are opposite ends of a **selection
spectrum**. Neither is the number for an athlete. **No cohort anywhere reports
what fraction of throwing athletes with nTOS resolve with rehabilitation alone.**
For a strength-and-conditioning audience, that is the most important absence in
the entire literature.

### 3.5 Does injury-prevention programming work in overhead athletes?

**Mixed, and weaker than our field generally believes. Know this before citing it.**

- **The risk-factor study:** 206 elite male handball players — obvious scapular
  dyskinesis OR 8.41 (95% CI 1.47–48.1), external rotation strength OR 0.71 per
  10 Nm (0.44–0.99). `[External]` Clarsen B, et al. *Br J Sports Med* 2014;48(17):1327-33. PMID 24948083.
- **The replication failed.** Same research group, 329 players — the paper is
  subtitled *"previous findings could not be confirmed."* `[External]` Andersson SH, et al. *Br J Sports Med* 2018;52(18):1191-8. PMID 28784621.
- **The flagship RCT** (45 teams, 660 players) reduced *any* shoulder problem —
  OR 0.72 (0.52–0.98), p=.038 — but **not substantial** shoulder problems: OR
  0.78 (0.53–1.16), **p=.23**. `[External]` Andersson SH, et al. *Br J Sports Med* 2017;51(14):1073-80. PMID 27313171.
- **The mechanism failed to move.** An RCT of 8 weeks of external rotation band
  strengthening produced a between-group difference of **0.06 N/kg (95% CI −0.01
  to 0.14)**. Conclusion: *"The ER strength program improved neither the ER
  strength nor the ER:IR ratio."* `[External]` Fredriksen H, Cools A, Myklebust G. *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2021;51(4):174-87. PMID 33789429.
- **The pooled result is null.** 3 RCTs, n=747: **OR 0.73 (95% CI 0.45–1.17)**.
  `[External]` Yonneau J, et al. *BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med* 2025;11(3):e002416. PMID 41035524.
- **Baseball's positive RCT used different content.** 16 teams / 237 youth
  players, 12-month follow-up, HR 1.940 (95% CI 1.175–3.205), p=.010 favouring
  intervention — but the programme was **5 stretching + 2 dynamic mobility + 2
  balance exercises, with no scapular strengthening at all.**
  `[External]` Sakata J, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2019;47(11):2709-16. PMID 31336051.

### 3.6 Field tests a strength coach could actually use

| Test | Normative data | Reliability | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deep Neck Flexor Endurance** | Men 38.9 ± 20.1 s; women 29.4 ± 13.7 s | ICC(2,k) 0.66 (0.34–0.86) — **moderate at best** | Age and activity level did not affect performance |
| **Scapular Muscle Endurance** | **None published** | ICC 0.67, **MDC 30.1 s** | MDC is large relative to hold times — **not good for tracking an individual** |
| **CKCUEST** | Collegiate baseball reference values exist | — | Closed-chain stability, not endurance |
| **Upper Quarter Y-Balance** | HS baseball/softball: composite 87.2 ± 8.9 %AL, no throwing/non-throwing asymmetry | — | Mobility/stability, not endurance |

`[External]` Domenech MA, et al. *PM R* 2011;3(2):105-10. PMID 21333948. / Edmondston SJ, et al. *J Manipulative Physiol Ther* 2008;31(5):348-54. PMID 18558277. / Roush JR, Kitamura J, Waits MC. *N Am J Sports Phys Ther* 2007;2(3):159-63. PMID 21522211. / Butler RJ, et al. *Int J Sports Phys Ther* 2014;9(4):518-24. PMID 25133080.

**The decisive caveat: not one of these has ever been validated against TOS.**
None has been administered to a TOS population; none has a cut-point for TOS
risk. Use them as general upper-quarter capacity monitoring with defensible
normative data. **Presenting any of them as a TOS screen would be unsupported.**

### 3.7 Verdict

**(a) Direct causal evidence that a muscular endurance deficit contributes to
TOS: none.** Not weak — absent. No prospective study has measured capacity at
baseline and observed who subsequently developed TOS.

**(b) Treatment-implies-mechanism reasoning is the strongest strand, and it is
structurally circular.** Treatment content tells you what clinicians believe, not
what causes the disease.

**(c) Pure plausibility carries the rest, and it is well-built plausibility.**
Fatigue demonstrably alters scapular position; the outlet spaces are real and
position-dependent; anterior tilt plus internal rotation plausibly narrows those
corridors. Every link past the kinematics is inference.

**The line to use:** *"Building scapular and postural capacity is a
mechanistically coherent, low-cost, low-risk intervention that is independently
justified by general shoulder health. The claim that it prevents TOS specifically
has no direct empirical support."*

## Section 4 — "Don't lift the pitchers before they throw": is it supported?

**Direct answer: no. There is no research supporting this recommendation, and the
adjacent evidence leans against it.**

### 4.1 The direct evidence does not exist

Three structured PubMed queries returned **no baseball study** examining
resistance training performed before throwing and its effect on velocity,
accuracy, mechanics, kinematics, kinetics, or injury rates. On TOS specifically:
**zero papers connect resistance training timing to thoracic outlet syndrome.**

### 4.2 The adjacent evidence leans the other way

| Study | Protocol | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Water polo, n=12 | 3×6 @ 80% 1RM (bench, row, half squat) **15 min before** in-water testing | **No difference** in throwing velocity (14.67 vs 14.91 m/s), accuracy, tethered force, swim, or jump (all p>0.05) |
| Tennis | 3×6 @ 75% 1RM, maximal intended velocity, not to failure | **No change** in serve/forehand/backhand velocity or accuracy at **3 min, 24 h, or 48 h** |
| Trained throwers, n=14 | Morning total-body resistance session | Afternoon overhead throw distance **improved** (p<0.05), 4–6 h later |

`[External]` Malliaros I, et al. *Sports (Basel)* 2025;13(11):378. PMID 41295761 — authors state such training "can be safely implemented 15 min prior to in-water training sessions."
`[External]` Terraza-Rebollo M, Baiget E. *PLoS One* 2021;16(12):e0260825. PMID 34882729.
`[External]` Ekstrand LG, et al. *J Strength Cond Res* 2013;27(1):101-6. PMID 22395279.

### 4.3 The one study showing a decrement — and why it undercuts the rule

16 adolescent baseball pitchers: push-ups **to failure** and repeated CMJs **to
failure** each dropped pitch velocity ~2 km/h (p=0.005).
`[External]` Tremblay M, et al. *J Strength Cond Res* 2024;38(8):1447-52. PMID 38838214.

1. It used **fatigue to failure**, not a programmed lift.
2. The **recovery interval before re-test was not quantified**. Pilot,
   non-randomised.
3. **Lower-body fatigue dropped velocity as much as upper-body fatigue.** If the
   mechanism were loading the throwing arm, squatting should not have mattered
   equally. This contradicts the rationale the rule rests on.

### 4.4 The mechanistic chain breaks at the end

- **Fatigue degrades shoulder proprioception** — robust and replicated.
  `[External]` Carpenter JE, Blasier RB, Pellizzon GG. *Am J Sports Med* 1998;26(2):262-5. PMID 9548121. / Tripp BL, et al. *J Athl Train* 2004;39(4):316-20. PMID 15592603.
- **But the deficit does not become functional.** A **160% increase in joint
  repositioning error** produced **no change** in motor control (Y-Balance Upper
  Quarter) or performance (Athletic Shoulder test). `[External]` Hadjisavvas S, et al. *Hum Mov Sci* 2024;98:103299. PMID 39461122.
- **Recovery is fast.** After throwing-to-fatigue in 16 collegiate players,
  scapulothoracic, glenohumeral, elbow and endpoint acuity **recovered within 4–7
  minutes**. The one exception: **glenohumeral acuity in the arm-cocked position
  had not recovered at 10 minutes.** `[External]` Tripp BL, Yochem EM, Uhl TL. *J Athl Train* 2007;42(4):452-7. PMID 18174932.
- **Counterweight:** strength training is associated with *better* shoulder
  proprioception chronically. `[External]` Salles JI, et al. *J Athl Train* 2015;50(3):277-80. PMID 25594912.
- All the fatigue-alters-pitching-mechanics literature concerns fatigue **from
  throwing itself**. `[External]` Murray TA, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2001;29(2):137-42. PMID 11292037. / Birfer R, Sonne MW, Holmes MW. *PeerJ* 2019;7:e7390. PMID 31392098.

That arm-cocked exception is the **single most defensible mechanistic hook** for
the athletic training staff's concern. It is narrow, and it is about the cocked
position specifically.

### 4.5 The defensible version

| Rationale | Support |
|---|---|
| (a) Manage **total daily/weekly load** | **Best supported**, but indirectly — the literature is about cumulative *throwing* load |
| (b) Avoid **heavy loading of the throwing shoulder** specifically | **Not supported, actively undercut** |
| (c) **Proximity in hours** | **Weak** — 15 min fine, 3 min fine, 4–6 h beneficial |
| (d) Nothing at all | **Closest to the truth for the blanket rule** |

**Recommended reframe:** *avoid taking the upper body to failure, and avoid novel
heavy eccentric upper-body work, in the hours before a high-intent throwing
session.* A dose-and-proximity-to-failure statement. Every trial using a dosed,
sub-failure lift found no impairment.

Present the current rule as **conservative professional judgment — a legitimate
basis for a decision — but not as research-backed.** There is no NSCA or ACSM
baseball-specific position statement, and no MLB/ASMI consensus, prescribing
lifting-relative-to-throwing order.

## Section 5 — Softball-specific evidence and workload

### 5.1 The softball TOS literature barely exists

PubMed `"thoracic outlet"[TIAB] AND softball[TIAB]` returns **exactly 6 records**:

- **2 case reports in softball athletes**, both **structural/congenital first-rib
  pathology** — a fracture nonunion and a congenital pseudarthrosis. **Neither
  identifies the patient as a pitcher.** `[External]` Young BL, et al. *J Shoulder Elbow Surg* 2016;25(11):e353-7. PMID 27742249. / Gibson TW, Carson L. *JBJS Case Connect* 2021;11(3). PMID 34329202.
- **4 cohort studies where softball is pooled with baseball** or given only as a
  prevalence.

A systematic review of Paget-Schroetter syndrome in athletes **does not contain
the word "softball"** — zero softball effort-thrombosis cases in the systematic
literature. `[External]` Keller RE, et al. *J Shoulder Elbow Surg* 2020;29(11):2417-25. PMID 32868012.

**Net: no peer-reviewed paper attributes TOS or effort thrombosis in a softball
pitcher to the pitching motion.** The design ceiling across the entire softball
TOS literature is the single case report. Every mechanistic claim must be
extrapolated from baseball, and the extrapolation should be stated.

### 5.2 The windmill is not load-sparing

| Measure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder distraction, youth | **94% body weight** | Werner 2005, n=53 |
| Shoulder distraction, elite/Olympic | **80% BW** | Werner 2006, n=24 |
| Peak compression, shoulder & elbow | **70–98% BW** | Barrentine 1998, n=8 |
| Shoulder extension/abduction torque | **9–10% BW × height** | Barrentine 1998 |
| **Peak biceps activation, windmill vs overhand (velocity-matched)** | **38% vs 19%** | Rojas 2009 |
| Scapular excursion through the pitch | 5–22° per plane | Holtz 2026, n=17 |

`[External]` Werner SL, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2005;33(4):552-60. PMID 15722291; 2006;34(4):597-603. PMID 16282576. / Barrentine SW, et al. *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 1998;28(6):405-15. PMID 9836172. / Rojas IL, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2009;37(3):558-65. PMID 19174551. / Holtz KA, et al. *Int J Sports Med* 2026;47(1):72-7. PMID 40738124.

Three independent sources state windmill shoulder and elbow loads are **similar
to baseball pitching**. Our own library says the same:
`[Zotero: FGQAC8DL, p. 1]` — *"it is continually reported that the shoulder stress
endured from the softball pitch is analogous to that of the baseball pitch,"* and
shoulder injury rates and patterns are "comparable with those reported in the
sport of baseball." Oliver GD, et al. *Orthop J Sports Med* 2019;7(8):2325967119865171.

**The "windmill is a natural motion" belief is explicitly named as a
misconception in the peer-reviewed literature.** At matched ball velocity, biceps
demand is **double** the overhand throw.

**Hyperabduction — label as inference.** The windmill carries the arm through
full overhead elevation once per pitch, transiently and at high angular velocity.
**No windmill study reports maximum shoulder elevation angle or time above 90°.**
The plausible outlet link is cumulative repetition, not sustained positional
narrowing.

**The actionable lever is proximal.** Lower-extremity screening alone predicted
**49.9%** of peak shoulder distraction variance in 46 collegiate pitchers, rising
to **56.2%** with hip rotation strength. `[External]` Ulman SM, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2025;53(3):565-70. PMID 39844736.

Our library adds the trunk side: at foot contact, increased trunk rotation toward
the pitching arm, increased stride length and a posteriorly shifted COM explained
**~33% of variance** in upper-extremity pain among 37 D1 softball pitchers.
`[Zotero: FGQAC8DL, p. 1 and p. 4]`

### 5.3 Workload — the strongest part of the case

**Rules, verified: NCAA softball has NO pitch count limit and NO mandated rest.**
Confirmed three ways (Zaremski 2025 states "no established pitch count guidelines
for collegiate softball"; Freetly et al. Table 2 lists NCAA for both sports as
"No restrictions / None"; the NCAA softball playing-rules page lists no such
document). NCAA baseball likewise has none — but **NFHS Rule 6-2-6 requires every
state association to adopt a baseball pitch-count restriction with mandated
rest**, while NFHS softball is capped only by *innings*.

*Caveat: the current NCAA rulebook PDF would not retrieve. Confirm against the
current rulebook before publishing a rules claim.*

**Collegiate workload** — 40 softball and 40 baseball College World Series teams,
2016–2021:

| | Softball | Baseball | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Innings pitched/season (median) | **84.1** | 29.2 | **2.9×** |
| Appearances/season | 23.0 | 16.0 | 1.4× |
| Innings/game | **3.6** | 1.6 | 2.3× |
| Pitchers per roster | **4.5 ± 1.1 (21.0%)** | 15.7 ± 2.3 (44.2%) | — |
| **% exceeding 100 IP/season** | **43.7%** | 7.35% | **6×** |

All p<0.0001. `[External]` Freetly T, Albright P, Feeley S, Feeley B, Chambers C.
Increased Utilization of Pitchers in NCAA Women's Softball Compared to Men's
Baseball. *J Womens Sports Med* 2024;4(2):1-8. DOI 10.53646/khdyxt64.

**That roster structure is the one-pitcher-carries-the-staff pattern,
quantified:** a softball staff is under one-third the size of a baseball staff,
and each pitcher absorbs nearly 3× the innings.

**The single most actionable workload finding:** 8,248 pitches tracked over 89
varsity outings. Counting game pitches only, **25.6%** exceeded recommendations.
**Including bullpen and warm-up: 51.2%** (P=0.001). **Roughly half of true pitch
volume is invisible to a box score.** `[External]` Kamarajugadda S, et al. *J Sports Med Phys Fitness* 2025;65(10):1306-10. PMID 40673787.

**Volume thresholds (youth/HS only — no collegiate pitch-count study exists):**
>85 pitches/game → **>2× likelihood of seeking medical attention**; shoulder pain
associated with **429 vs 219 pitches/week** and **19,195 vs 8,876 pitches/year**.
`[External]` Gooch B, et al. *Sports Health* 2022;14(5):702-9. PMID 34672828.

**Fatigue — mixed direction, read carefully:**

- After **99 ± 21 pitches**: dominant-side deficits **11–22%** across hip,
  scapula, shoulder, elbow, wrist; **velocity −3.4%**. `[External]` Corben JS, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2015;43(8):2035-41. PMID 26109610.
- Kinetics **decreased** at shoulder, elbow and wrist in the last and doubleheader
  innings — the pitcher produces *less* force, not more. `[External]` Fava AW, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2024;52(12):3118-29. PMID 39344907.
- Consecutive-day tournament pitching raises fatigue (2.0 → 3.5) and pain
  (1.3 → 2.5). `[External]` Skillington SA, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 2017;45(6):1413-9. PMID 28298058.
- But post-game stride-leg knee extension and trunk flexion strength
  **increased** in one study. `[External]` Pletcher ER, et al. *Sports Biomech* 2024;23(9):1210-8. PMID 34134606.

Consistent reading: **proximal control and output degrade within and across
days**, while isolated post-game strength tests can move either way. Do not
present fatigue as a clean monotonic decline.

### 5.4 Injury epidemiology

- **NCAA softball overall: 3.92 per 1,000 AE. Shoulder is the #1 body part at
  15.2%.** `[External]` Veillard KL, et al. PMID 34280289.
- Game 4.30 vs practice 2.67 per 1,000 AE; **preseason practice 3.65 vs in-season
  1.68.** `[External]` Marshall SW, et al. *J Athl Train* 2007;42(2):286-94. PMID 17710178.
- **Collegiate pitchers specifically: 72.8% injured in one season** (n=181); 92 of
  131 injuries chronic/overuse, 33 shoulder-related. `[External]` Hill JL, et al. *J Strength Cond Res* 2004;18(3):426-31. PMID 15320672.
- **20 of 24 collegiate pitchers had injuries/complaints; 82% of time-loss
  injuries were upper extremity.** `[External]` Loosli AR, et al. *Am J Sports Med* 1992;20(1):35-7. PMID 1554071.

### 5.5 Prevention — an open field

Effectively **no softball-specific prevention evidence exists.** A systematic
review screened 1,605 articles, included 7, and found **only 1 described an
injury-prevention program for softball players** — with no efficacy trial.
`[External]` Paul J, Brown SM, Mulcahey MK. *Sports Health* 2021;13(4):390-5. PMID 33535878.

Uptake is poor: **<46% of fastpitch coaches run any prevention program**, with
unfamiliarity and staffing cited as barriers. `[External]` Nurrenbern J, et al. *Int J Sports Phys Ther* 2023;18(4):958-68. PMID 37547836.

### 5.6 A number to stop repeating

**"1,200–1,500 pitches in a 3-day tournament (vs 100–120 for baseball)."** This
appears in a peer-reviewed paper — but is cited to a biomechanics study of 53
youth pitchers that **reports no tournament volume at all**. The citation does not
support the figure. The only other peer-reviewed tournament number, **95–381
pitches/weekend**, is an order of magnitude lower and also describes youth
pitchers. **Do not use 1,200–1,500.**

## Section 6 — Mitigation, mapped to who owns each lever

The point of this table is that **most of the modifiable exposure does not sit in
the weight room.** That is not a deflection; it is where the evidence points.

| Lever | Owner | Evidence strength | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Count all pitches — bullpen and warm-up included** | Pitching coach + AT | **Strong** — half of true volume is uncounted (Kamarajugadda 2025) | Track every pitch. This is the highest-yield, lowest-cost change available. |
| **Innings distribution across the staff** | Head coach | **Strong** — 43.7% of collegiate softball pitchers exceed 100 IP/season on a 4.5-pitcher staff | Develop staff depth. No rule will do this for you. |
| **Consecutive-day and doubleheader exposure** | Head coach | **Moderate** — fatigue and pain accumulate across tournament days (Skillington 2017) | Build rest into tournament plans. |
| **Cervical mobility screening (CFRT, cervical flexion ROM)** | AT + S&C | **Moderate, in our exact population** — RR 9.38 for time-loss injury `[Zotero: F5YKF6B4]` | Add to preseason screen. Cheap, fast, defensible. |
| **Pec minor resting length** | S&C | **Moderate mechanism, no TOS validation** | Measure (reliable method exists). Do not publish a cut-off. |
| **Hip and trunk capacity / lower-extremity mechanics** | S&C | **Moderate** — LE screening predicts ~50–56% of shoulder distraction variance (Ulman 2025) | This is the strongest evidence-aligned S&C target for shoulder load in softball pitchers. |
| **Scapular control and postural capacity** | S&C | **Plausible, unproven for TOS** | Do it — low cost, low risk, independently justified. Do not claim it prevents TOS. |
| **Upper-quarter capacity monitoring (DNF endurance, SME, CKCUEST, UQYBT)** | S&C | **Weak for individual tracking** (SME MDC = 30.1 s) | Use as group screens. Never present as a TOS screen. |
| **Lifting timing relative to throwing** | S&C | **No evidence either way** | Avoid upper-body work to failure and novel heavy eccentrics near high-intent throwing. Drop the blanket rule. |
| **Diagnostic and referral pathway** | Sports medicine | **Strong** — ascertainment drives counts; 47–62% false-positive rate on EAST | Agree on criteria before counting cases. Consider the SVS ≥3-of-4 standard. |
| **Year-round throwing / travel ball history** | Recruiting + sports medicine | **Moderate** | Capture prior throwing volume at intake. |

## Appendix — what to say, and what not to say

**Say:**

- "TOS is not separately coded in NCAA injury surveillance, so there is no peer
  benchmark to compare against."
- "With a population incidence around 2–3 per 100,000 per year, a roster our size
  cannot generate a statistically meaningful rate. Two cases is within chance."
- "The provocative test used most often is positive in roughly half of people who
  don't have TOS. Case counts track how hard you look."
- "Windmill pitching produces shoulder loads equal to baseball, and our pitchers
  throw about three times the innings on a staff a third the size, with no pitch
  count limit."
- "About half of true pitch volume is bullpen and warm-up that nobody counts."
- "Scalene muscle changes in TOS are type II atrophy and fibrosis — the opposite
  of a weight-training adaptation."
- "There is no research supporting a ban on lifting before throwing. The
  defensible version is avoiding upper-body work to failure near high-intent
  throwing."
- "Building capacity is worth doing on general shoulder-health grounds. I won't
  claim it prevents TOS, because that hasn't been shown."

**Do not say:**

- "TOS incidence is 3–80 per 1,000." *(Traces to a non-indexed source.)*
- "Roos described 14 types of congenital bands." *(No Roos primary says 14.)*
- "GIRD contributes to TOS." *(Zero literature. Three queries, no records.)*
- "Softball pitchers throw 1,200–1,500 pitches per tournament weekend."
  *(Citation does not support the figure.)*
- "Droopy shoulder syndrome narrows the costoclavicular space." *(It is a
  traction mechanism; symptoms improve with arm elevation.)*
- "Scapular endurance deficits cause TOS." *(No study has measured scapular
  endurance in a TOS population at all.)*
- "TOS surgery ends pitching careers." *(Matched-control studies find decline no
  faster than normal aging; RTP 74–81% at 9–11 months.)*

## Open questions worth answering locally

1. **What is our actual diagnostic pathway?** Which test findings triggered each
   diagnosis, and did they meet the SVS ≥3-of-4 standard?
2. **What are our real pitch counts**, bullpen and warm-up included? No
   collegiate pitch-count figure exists in the literature — it has to be
   measured locally.
3. **What is each pitcher's prior throwing history** (travel ball volume,
   year-round participation, previous first-rib or clavicle injury)?
4. **Has anyone imaged for structural predisposition** in the affected athletes —
   cervical rib, elongated C7 transverse process, first-rib abnormality?
