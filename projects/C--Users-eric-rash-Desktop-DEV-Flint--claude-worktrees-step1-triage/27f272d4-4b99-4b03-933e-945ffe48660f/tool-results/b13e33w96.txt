# Building the Annual Plan: A Nine-Step Sequence, Anchored on Target Competitions

**What it is** — Issurin gives a fixed nine-step sequence for constructing an annual
program (Table 4.2, PDF p. 138, printed p. 127), illustrated stepwise in Figures 4.1-4.4
(PDF pp. 139-141, printed pp. 128-130; verified against the page images): (1) determine
the target and mandatory competitions, normally taken from the international/national/
regional calendar; (2) determine the terms and duration of training stages and periods
("in terms of target competitions and methodology"); (3) divide training stages into
several meso- and microcycles; (4) plan additional competitions and trials to "finalize
the training stages and to diversify the preparatory program"; (5) plan training camps
(objectives, terms, places), which also covers altitude preparation (Ch. 5); (6) plan
medical and sport-specific tests, with the initial medical exam early season; (7)
calculate integrative workload characteristics per month (total mileage, number of
lifts, throws, etc.); (8) calculate the same for the full annual cycle, compared against
previous years to reveal "weak" points; (9) correct the plan following that review ("as
a rule, the first version needs correction and this operation is obligatory"). Figures
4.1-4.4 show competitions plotted on four levels (Level A = the single targeted event;
Levels B-D = mandatory events of descending importance: national championship,
national cup, regional trials, winter cup), against a month-by-month grid that only
fills in stages/periods, meso/microcycles and training camps in the later figures as each
step is completed. The **optimal design places important competitions at the end of a
training stage** (Fig. 4.2 caption, PDF p. 139, printed p. 128). The plan is not
considered final at step 9: "it must first be reviewed by the administration, colleagues
and athletes and with repeated analysis, will lead to corrections" (PDF p. 141, printed
p. 130) before it "receives the final version status that is used for realization."

Layered on this procedure is a seasonal content trend (Table 4.3, PDF p. 142, printed p.
131; verified against the page image): early season favors continuous/uniform/
alternating methods in the accumulation mesocycle and a wide range of fitness
exercises, more individual workouts, and general restorative means (stretching,
relaxation, low-intensity aerobics); mid-season narrows toward interval methods, a
restricted exercise range, and adds physiotherapy/massage/mental training/nutritional
supplements; late season is "strictly programmed interval exercises" in both
accumulation and transmutation mesocycles, mostly specialized exercises, strictly
programmed workouts, and the "most effective individually selected restorative
means." The general rule: "training stages within the annual cycle should be similar
but not identical," with mid- and especially late-season stages "more rigorous, more
strictly programmed and better managed" (PDF p. 142, printed p. 131).

Issurin also assigns competition an explicit developmental role even early in the
season, for three stated reasons (PDF p. 143, printed p. 132): it completes each
training stage as "a compulsory component of the training program"; it "breaks the
usual routine and brings out the very important element of athlete spirit"; and it
"provides athletes with extraordinary training stimuli that can not be obtained from
regular workloads." The competitive program itself should be diversified early season,
narrowed toward event-specific standards mid-season, and strictly event-specific late
season.

**Why it works** — The author's own stated rationale for reserving the most rigorous,
best-managed workloads for the pre-competition stages is two-part (PDF p. 142,
printed p. 131): physiologically, it "gives the benefit of higher training responses
prior to the most important events"; psychologically, "it heightens the athletes'
self-confidence as they know that they have additional reserves during the most
stressful phase of their preparation." No further mechanism is given for why the
nine-step sequence itself (as opposed to some other ordering) produces a better plan
than ad hoc planning; the author states only that "coaches put together annual
programs based on their own experience, knowledge and common sense" and that
Block Periodization "implies specific demands to planning" without benchmarking the
sequence against an alternative (PDF p. 138, printed p. 127).

**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college football calendar** — The Level
A/B/C/D competition tiers map reasonably well onto a football master calendar: Level
A as the postseason target (playoff/bowl window), Level B as rivalry or
conference-championship-implication games, Level C/D as the rest of the regular-season
slate. Step 1 (fix the target and mandatory events) and step 5 (plan camps, including
fall camp) are directly usable planning anchors for Applied Performance's annual
calendar-building work with the sport coaching staff. The step-7/8 "integrative
workload characteristics per month, compared with previous years" is a concrete,
citable structure for what a season-over-season GPS/load report should track, distinct
from (and a possible template for) the currently undocumented "standardized
weekly/monthly coach-facing reporting" gap named in `method-baseline.md` (Data,
technology and reporting section, line 159).

**When NOT to apply** — This is Issurin's own applied planning template for an
individual/dual-sport Olympic calendar with a single clearly ranked target competition
determined well in advance; it is not derived from any comparative study of planning
methods (no control condition, no outcome data for the sequence itself). **Inference,
population caveat:** college football's postseason destination (which bowl, or which
playoff round) is not known when the annual plan is first built, which weakens step 1's
premise that the target competition can be fixed at the top of the sequence the way an
Olympic Trials date or a World Championship date can be; the plan has to accommodate
a moving Level-A target rather than a scheduled one. The "optimal design places
important competitions at the end of a training stage" (Fig. 4.2) also assumes the coach
controls stage boundaries around the competition calendar; in football the
competition calendar (one game almost every week, in season) is closer to Issurin's
"competitive microcycle" pattern described elsewhere in this book than to a plan with
discrete preparation stages ending in isolated target events.

**Example** — **Inference:** using the nine-step sequence as a working checklist for a
Baylor football annual calendar: fix the bowl/playoff-window target and the
conference-championship-implication games (step 1); block out fall camp, the
regular-season in-season maintenance stage, and the bowl-prep stage (step 2-3); slot
non-conference "trial" games and any scrimmages (step 4); place fall camp and any
summer training windows (step 5); align the pre-participation physical and preseason
testing battery to early season (step 6); build a month-by-month GPS/load-volume
summary (step 7-8) and review it against the prior season before finalizing (step 9).

# The Quadrennial Cycle: Four Generic Year-Directions, Individualized by Stage Objectives

**What it is** — Issurin frames multi-year planning around the four-year Olympic
cycle, triggered by post-Games analysis: "the challenge of post-Olympic analysis is to
internalize positive experiences and reveal the reasons for sub-par performances. Based
on the findings, a new preparatory plan is worked out" (PDF p. 143, printed p. 132).
He gives a worked example: the Russian national gymnastics team's quadrennial cycle,
subdivided into **eight half-year stages**, each with its own general objective (PDF p.
144, printed p. 133, cited to Arkajev & Suchilin, 2004): (1) enhancement of
sport-specific motor fitness; (2) acquisition of new, highly complex technical skills and
their hybrids; (3) updating of competition combinations; (4) further increases in
sport-specific motor fitness; (5) enhancement of technical mastery and attaining stable
performances; (6) increase in performance quality, stability and stress tolerance; (7)
final compilation of competition combinations, endurance for an entire program, and
selection of a duplicate Olympic squad; (8) modeling expected competitive conditions,
attaining high competitive reliability, and final selection of the Olympic team. Issurin
is explicit that this eight-stage division is sport-specific ("a matter of professional
competence" depending on conditions), and that "the division of the quadrennial cycle
into four annual cycles is the most popular" in general (PDF p. 144, printed p. 133).

For the four-annual-cycle version, Table 4.4 (PDF p. 144, printed p. 133; verified
against the page image) gives generic year-by-year directions, irrespective of sport:
**Year 1** — engagement and examination of new candidates, selection of coaches and
staff, updating/correcting training means and methods, remodeling the training
repertory, forming team spirit and camaraderie; **Year 2**: further candidate selection
and approval, acquisition of new techno-tactical skills, increased training workloads,
improved training quality; **Year 3**: attaining maximal training-workload level,
approving the Olympic-season annual cycle, determining individual performance
characteristics per team member; **Year 4**: stabilizing team make-up (usually with a
double Olympic squad), stabilizing workloads at the prior year's level, and approving
and stabilizing competitive behavior and performance. Issurin states the third and
fourth years "should be very similar since coaches simulate the annual pre-Olympic
training design one year before" the Games, though the pre-Olympic (fourth) year's
workload is "usually higher than the year before for various reasons," including
"greater motivation, better conditions for training and restoration, greater budgets"
(PDF p. 146, printed p. 135).

**Why it works** — The author gives no controlled-comparison mechanism for why
this generic four-year sequence outperforms an alternative structure; it is presented as
observed common practice ("the division of the quadrennial cycle into four annual
cycles is the most popular") rationalized post hoc by the logic of squad-building
(candidate selection and cohesion before workload escalation, workload escalation
before stabilization). **Inference:** the underlying logic parallels this library's existing
long-term athletic development material: building foundational capacity (team
identity, basic skill acquisition) before layering higher, more specific workloads, but
Issurin does not state that connection explicitly in this passage.

**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college context** — College football has no
literal Olympic quadrennium, but the four-year (or five-year, with a redshirt) eligibility
window is a structurally similar bounded multi-year cycle with its own roster-cohort
logic: Year 1 (true freshman/transfer intake) mapping to Table 4.4's Year 1 emphasis on
candidate evaluation and cultural integration; Years 2-3 to skill/technical acquisition
and rising training-workload tolerance; a "walk year" (senior or draft-eligible year)
to Table 4.4's Year 4 emphasis on stabilized, individualized performance. The
gymnastics eight-stage example is a useful illustration that objective-setting per stage
should be concrete and sport-specific rather than generic, which is directly usable
guidance for building a position-group or class-cohort development plan.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference, population caveat:** both the eight-stage
gymnastics example and the four-year Table 4.4 are drawn from Olympic individual/
team-technical sports with a single fixed quadrennial target and squad continuity
controlled by national-team selection, not from a college roster with annual signing
classes, transfer portal turnover, and no shared four-year entry cohort (a program's
"Year 1" and "Year 4" athletes are present simultaneously every season, unlike a
national team's cycle-wide cohort). The table's per-year directions are the author's own
applied synthesis of common practice, not a study with an outcome measure; no
evidence is given that following this sequence produces better long-term results than
an alternative one. The Table 4.4 vs Fig. 4.5 age-cohort content (older/experienced vs.
younger-athlete workload differentiation within this same quadrennial frame) is
treated separately in
[[heuristic--older-vs-younger-athlete-quadrennial-workload-differentiation]].

**Example** — **Inference:** a position group's four-year development plan borrowing
Table 4.4's shape: Year 1 focused on evaluating and culturally onboarding new signees
and transfers; Year 2 on techno-tactical (scheme/position) skill acquisition and raising
training-load tolerance; Year 3 on maximizing training workload and identifying each
player's individual performance profile; Year 4 on stabilizing role, workload, and
game-performance consistency for players entering their final season or a likely draft
evaluation.

# Older, Experienced Athletes Enter a New Quadrennial Cycle Lighter and Climb Back Slower

**What it is** — Within the quadrennial frame, Issurin differentiates preparation for
"highly experienced top-level athletes" (who "have a remarkable advantage in terms of
basic and sport-specific knowledge, emotional control, stability of technical,
techno-tactical skills and competitive behavior," and are usually "high-authority
persons who positively affect social climate and team spirit") from younger athletes new
to the Olympic cycle (PDF p. 145, printed p. 134). The stated disadvantage of the older
group: they are "approaching their biological limits in regard to training responses,"
show "less reactivity to training stimuli," prefer habitual programs, need longer
transition periods, and, in the first annual cycle of a new quadrennial plan, train at
"a substantially lower total volume of exercises, about 15-20% less, than in the
previous Olympic year" (PDF p. 145, printed p. 134; text quote). Younger athletes new
to Olympic preparation show the opposite pattern: heightened motivation, often better
training conditions (more camps, more qualified partners), and a substantial workload
increase in year one relative to the prior year, continuing to rise at a diminishing rate
through years two and three.

**Verified against the page image (Fig. 4.5, PDF p. 145, printed p. 134):** the chart
plots "total workloads, referring units" across five time points (prior Olympic Games,
then Years I, II, III, and the next Olympic Games) for both groups. Younger athletes
rise steadily from about 7.4 at the prior Games to about 10.0 at the next Games.
Experienced athletes fall sharply from about 10 (at the prior Games, their own peak
taper year) to about 7 at Year I: **a drop of roughly 30%, not the "about 15-20%
less" the text states**, then climb through about 8.1, 8.6 to about 9.0 at the next
Games, ending below the younger group's 10.0. This is a text-vs-figure inconsistency
of the same kind found in the Chapter 3 review (Fig. 3.10's "69.3%" versus the plotted
value); it is recorded here rather than silently resolved. The qualitative pattern the text
describes, older athletes drop, then climb back more slowly than they descended,
finishing below younger athletes, matches the figure regardless of the exact percentage.

Table 4.5 (PDF p. 147, printed p. 136; verified against the page image) generalizes this
across the whole career, not just the first quadrennial year: **total training volume**
10-30% less than younger athletes ("older athletes train more conscientiously and
produce longer training residuals"); **annual cycle structure**: longer transition
period ("additional time for psychological and medical rehabilitation"); **organizational
forms**: higher share of individual (vs. group) training ("aged athletes need less
supervision, show more initiative and independence"); **restoration**: increased use of
restorative means ("older athletes recover slower and often suffer from previous
injuries"); **equipment**: more insistent, creative selection ("their own demands...
serious and consistent during their examination"); **individual progress**: "motor
potential doesn't increase but can be utilized more effectively," i.e. "stabilization and
reduction of several functions are compensated for by higher efficiency of athletic
activity."

**Why it works** — The author gives no physiological mechanism for the biological
"approaching their limits" claim beyond naming it; the entry that follows in Table 4.5
attributes the compensating higher performance to behavioral factors (conscientiousness,
individualized training, restoration, equipment care) and to "higher efficiency of
athletic activity" rather than to any stated biological process. **Inference:** this
pairs naturally with this library's existing residual-training-effect material: a more
trained system plausibly needs a smaller absolute training dose to hold a given
adaptation, but Issurin does not make that specific connection in this passage.

**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college roster** — A fifth-year or
graduate-transfer player entering a new coaching cycle, offseason, or scheme after
several years in a program is a rough structural analogue to Issurin's "experienced
athlete entering a new quadrennial cycle": expect (and program for) a lower initial
training volume than a true freshman or new transfer, a longer individualized
restoration/return-to-training window, and a training emphasis on utilization and
technical/tactical efficiency over raw volume increases, consistent with Table 4.5's
"motor potential doesn't increase but can be utilized more effectively."

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference, population caveat:** this is Issurin's own applied
generalization about elite, Olympic-cycle, mostly-individual-sport athletes typically in
their late 20s to 30s; it is not derived from a controlled or longitudinal study with
football-specific data, and "older" in this context (returning multi-Olympiad veterans)
does not map cleanly onto a 22-23-year-old college fifth-year player, whose biological
age is nowhere near the "biological limits" the text describes for career-long elite
athletes. The 15-20%/10-30% figures are the author's own stated bands, not derived
from a cited dataset in this passage, and (per the figure check above) the book's own
chart does not match its own 15-20% claim for the first-year drop.

**Example** — **Inference:** a fifth-year offensive lineman returning after a coaching
staff or scheme change is deliberately started at a lower total training-volume target
than the incoming freshman class, with a longer individualized ramp and an emphasis
on technical efficiency (leverage, hand placement, pass-set footwork) rather than
chasing the volume numbers of his own earlier seasons, consistent with the
"motor potential doesn't increase but can be utilized more effectively" framing.

# Klementiev: Training Volume Fell for 19 Years While Podium Results Kept Coming

**What it is** — A single-athlete, 19-year longitudinal case: Ivan Klementiev, USSR
then Latvian Olympic canoeist, competed across four Olympic cycles, earning one gold
and two silver Olympic medals and seven World Championship golds (PDF p. 148,
printed p. 137). His total annual paddling volume was tracked by age (Figure 4.6, PDF
p. 149, printed p. 138; **verified against the page image**: the chart's peak at age 21,
roughly 4,900 km, matches the text's "maximal training volume was attained at age 21
when he trained with the USSR national squad"; volume then oscillates with periodic
late-quadrennial upticks but trends down through the following years, with the lowest
point at age 37, about 1,500 km). Issurin quantifies the decline by quadrennial-cycle
average, relative to the first cycle as 100%: **ages 21-24 = 100%; ages 25-28 = 88.2%;
ages 29-32 = 77.4%; ages 33-36 = 61%** (PDF p. 148, printed p. 137; text quote).
Transition periods lengthened over the same span, "from one to three months during
his career." Despite the steady volume decline, "after age 30 [he] won four gold medals
at the World Championships and two Olympic silver medals" (PDF p. 149, printed p.
138). Issurin's own conclusion: "workload reduction was effectively compensated for
by a higher quality of training," which he associates with the athlete's own account
(Klementiev, 1994) of "better preparation exploiting Block Periodization, a more
conscious and effective selection of exercises, avoidance of non-compatible workload
combinations, constant attention to restoration, and the use of a logical program of
competition." One deviation in the record is flagged as non-methodological: at age 24
(1984), "because of political, not methodological circumstances," Klementiev raced a
substitute regatta of socialist countries rather than the Los Angeles Olympics, cutting
that season short by a month (Figure 4.6 caption, PDF p. 149, printed p. 138).

**Why it works** — The author's own causal claim ("workload reduction was
effectively compensated for by a higher quality of training") is stated, not
demonstrated: no comparison group, no measured "training quality" variable, and no
separation of the Block Periodization approach from the other listed factors (exercise
selection, compatibility management, restoration, competition-program logic) or from
simple accumulated technical/tactical experience. **The author gives no mechanism**
connecting reduced kilometers specifically to Block Periodization as opposed to any
other explanation for late-career success, including selection effects (an athlete who
stays competitive to age 36 across four Olympiads is, by construction, one of the rare
athletes for whom this pattern worked).

**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college context** — Useful as a
citable, named-athlete illustration that declining training volume is not, by itself,
evidence of declining performance capacity in an experienced athlete, and that
tracking quality/specificity of training rather than raw volume may be the more
relevant monitoring question for a veteran player. Pairs directly with
[[heuristic--older-vs-younger-athlete-quadrennial-workload-differentiation]]'s Table
4.5 claim that "motor potential doesn't increase but can be utilized more effectively."

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is untested.** This is an n = 1, uncontrolled,
retrospective case with no comparison athlete who trained at a constant or rising
volume across the same span; it cannot separate the effect of reduced volume, Block
Periodization specifically, accumulated technical mastery, opponent turnover, or
survivorship (only athletes who remained competitive despite aging appear in this kind
of retrospective record; athletes for whom volume reduction did not work are absent
from it by definition). It is also a single elite paddler in an individual, cyclic-endurance
Olympic sport with a fixed quadrennial target; nothing here generalizes to a college
football roster's age range (18-23), position-specific demands, or annual (not
quadrennial) competitive cycle. Do not cite this case as evidence that reducing training
volume improves or preserves performance in general.

**Example** — **Inference:** when a program has a small number of veteran players
whose measured training volumes are trending down year over year, Klementiev's case
is a reason to check training quality, technical execution, and competition-readiness
markers before treating the volume decline itself as a red flag, rather than a license to
assume volume reduction is automatically beneficial.

# Armstrong's Seven-Point Physiology Case: Read Only With the Doping Context the 2008 Book Does Not Give

**Historical context absent from the source, stated first because it governs how the
rest of this entry should be read:** this book was published in 2008. Lance Armstrong
was stripped of all seven Tour de France titles (1999-2005) by the USADA "Reasoned
Decision" in 2012, following a finding of systematic doping (EPO, blood transfusions,
testosterone, corticosteroids) across the span this case study covers. That finding is not
in this book and could not have been reflected in it. This is stated as historical fact, not
as this entry-writer's opinion or as Eric's judgment; it is not labeled "Inference" because
it is not a reasoned inference from the text but an external, independently documented
fact about the case's subject. It means the physiological changes below cannot be read
as evidence of natural, doping-free long-term adaptation, and the case should not be
cited in that way.

**What it is** — Issurin presents cyclist Lance Armstrong as a case study in long-term
physiological change, based on Coyle (2005), a published laboratory case-report of
serial testing from age 21 to 28 (PDF p. 150, printed p. 139; text quote): "From ages 21
to 28 he was examined in a physiology laboratory for body composition, maximal
oxygen uptake, maximal blood lactate and mechanical efficiency of pedaling." At age 24
he was diagnosed with testicular cancer and underwent surgery and a two-year course
of treatment including chemotherapy; from ages 27-32 he won the Tour de France six
times (per the book's count at time of writing). Over the seven-year span, body weight
rose slightly (+0.8 kg) while lean body weight rose more (+1.1 kg). **Verified against
the page image (Figure 4.7, PDF p. 151, printed p. 140):** the chart is two twin-axis
panels across nine unevenly spaced age points (21.1, 21.4, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28), a
hollow-star marker flagging the "medical treatment including chemotherapy" period
around age 24-25. Top panel: Heart Rate (left axis, 196-208 beats/min) and VO2max
(right axis, 60-85 ml/kg/min); bottom panel: mechanical efficiency (right axis,
20-23.5%) and power output at a VO2 of 5 L/min (left axis, 370-420 W), the latter two
rising together and roughly linearly across all nine points. The two top-panel series are
not unambiguously distinguishable from the rendered image alone (their in-panel labels
sit near line peaks without a clear leader-line to one series), so the values below are
taken from the book's stated text, not read independently off the chart: **maximum
heart rate decreased by 6 beats/min** over the seven years; **VO2max "reached peak
value at age 23, declined after medical treatment and did not reach the highest level
until age 28"**; **mechanical efficiency increased by 8.8%**; **power output at 5 L/min
VO2 increased by 18%**. The book flags that Armstrong's final VO2max, 71 ml/kg/min,
is "inferior to the data of elite cyclists which approaches the level of 80 ml/kg/min
(Padilla et al., 2000)," and speculates this partly reflects body-weight reduction before
races raising relative VO2max further than the absolute lab value shows. Issurin's stated
conclusion: "the individual progress of this great athlete was determined not by
enlargement of his physiological potential but by its more efficient utilization,"
listing hypothesized (not demonstrated) contributors: "pronounced hypertrophy and
improved contractility of slow muscle fibers, alteration in myosin ATPase activity,
increased cycling efficiency induced by altitude training and enhanced pedaling
technique" (PDF p. 150, printed p. 139). The book explicitly calls the "physiological
mechanism of this improved muscular efficiency... still unclear."

**Why it works** — **The author gives no confirmed mechanism**; he states this
directly ("the physiological mechanism of this improved muscular efficiency is still
unclear") and lists candidate physiological explanations as speculative, not
established. **Inference (post-2012 context, not the book's):** the interpretation
problem is worse than an unresolved mechanism, because the underlying data-generating
process (systematic doping across this exact age-21-to-32 span, per USADA 2012) is
absent from both the book's account and from Coyle (2005), the primary source Issurin
cites. Any of the listed changes (mechanical efficiency, power at a fixed VO2, the
partial VO2max recovery after chemotherapy) could in principle be influenced by
doping practices not measured or disclosed in the primary source; this entry does not
assert that they were, only that the book's "individual creativity, self-confidence and
athletic wisdom" framing (PDF p. 151, printed p. 140) is not a complete account of the
known history.

**When to apply** — Limited. The chemotherapy-recovery data point (VO2max
declining after cancer treatment, then partially recovering over several years) is the
one piece of this case with plausible standalone value, as an illustration that returning
to elite endurance performance after major medical treatment is a multi-year process,
not a single-season one. Do not use any other part of this case (the "better utilization,
not enlarged potential" conclusion, the specific percentage changes, the athlete as a
role model for long-term development) without disclosing the doping context above.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is untested**, and that ceiling is generous
given the context problem. This is an n = 1 case study, drawn from a primary source
(Coyle, 2005) that has itself drawn public scientific criticism for the same reason
(interpreting serial lab data from an athlete later found to have doped throughout the
tested period) independent of anything in this book. Do not cite Armstrong's
mechanical-efficiency or power gains as evidence for a "utilization, not enlargement"
training principle, and do not use this case in any coach- or athlete-facing material
without the 2012 finding attached. The Klementiev case
([[case-study--klementiev-multiyear-workload-decline-with-podium-longevity]]) is the
cleaner example of this book's "long-term progress through utilization, not raw
capacity" theme, without the same documented confound.

**Example** — **Inference:** if this case is referenced at all in Applied Performance
material (e.g., illustrating return-to-elite-performance timelines after major medical
treatment), it should be paired with an explicit note on the athlete's subsequent USADA
finding, rather than presented as a clean physiology-of-aging or physiology-of-training

# Four Stages of Long-Term Preparation, Dosed by Hours and Broken Out by Domain

**What it is** — Issurin gives the same four-stage long-term development model
already in this library from Bompa
([[periodization--bompa/framework--ltad-sequential-model]]), but adds two things
Bompa's entry does not have: an explicit dosing table and a domain-by-domain
(technical/physical/tactical/mental) content breakdown per stage. He opens by
scoping the topic down: "the athletic career... is highly dependent on the earlier period
of long-term preparation that usually begins in childhood. The purview of this chapter
restricts consideration of this matter, which is worthy of its own book" (PDF p. 152,
printed p. 141). Table 4.6 (PDF p. 152, printed p. 141; **verified against the page
image**: the raw text extraction of this table initially dropped a cell, recovered by
re-reading the rendered page) gives, per stage: **Preliminary preparation**: 1-3
years, 3-4 workouts/week, 45-60 min/workout, 120-170 hours/year; **Initial
specialization**: 2-3 years, 4-5 workouts/week, 75-90 min/workout, 250-300
hours/year; **Advanced specialization**: 2-3 years, 6-9 workouts/week, 60-120
min/workout, 500-750 hours/year; **Sports perfection**: duration "individually
dependent," 6-12 workouts/week, 70-150 min/workout, 750-1400 hours/year (cited to
Issurin, 1994). Favorable starting ages vary widely by sport (Figure 4.8, PDF p. 153,
printed p. 142; verified against the page image): gymnastics and figure skating
start roughly ages 7-9; swimming roughly 9-11; team/dual sports ("ball games"),
sprint track and field, weightlifting and fencing roughly 11-14; kayaking roughly
12-15; rowing roughly 13-16; marathon roughly 13-17; triathlon roughly 13-18
(adapted from Platonov & Sakhnovsky, 1988, updated by expert interview). Issurin
notes the general trend toward earlier specialization across sport is driven by better
child-scaled equipment, training conditions, media popularization, and visible early
starters, and that these ranges reflect "worldwide practice" not individual exceptions
(PDF p. 153-154, printed p. 142-143).

Each stage's content is broken out by four domains in Tables 4.7-4.10 (PDF pp.
154-157, printed pp. 143-146): **Preliminary preparation** (Table 4.7): technical:
sport-specific and general skill/coordination acquisition; physical: multilateral,
all-round development with sport-specific emphasis, raising general training capacity;
tactical: acquaintance with basic tactical demands; mental: consolidating interest,
forming motivation, "adoption of fundamental moral principles of 'fair play', team
spirit." **Initial specialization** (Table 4.8): technical: further sport-specific
development, selecting the most favorable discipline/event; physical: more specialized
motor-ability development, adapting to sport-typical workloads; tactical: deeper rules
and tactics knowledge; mental: self-confidence, will power, "consciousness and
responsibility." **Advanced specialization** (Table 4.9): technical: attaining effective,
individualized technique, eliminating technical drawbacks; physical: further
sport-specific motor-ability gains supporting individualized technique; tactical:
individualized tactical/techno-tactical formation; mental: motivation toward
excellence, emotional control, mental-regulation skill acquisition. Issurin adds a
caution here specific to this stage: "despite increased technical and motor abilities,
juniors at this stage are still not completely mature and the use of maximal workloads
should be restricted. In particular, administration of highly intense anaerobic glycolitic
exercises requires complete control because highly qualified juniors can be overly
ambitious in their training routines but not experienced enough in self-regulation of
metabolic and muscular reactions" (PDF p. 155-156, printed p. 144-145). **Sports
perfection** (Table 4.10): technical: further enhancement with individual style;
physical: highest sport-specific ability level and its "improved utilization"; tactical:
tactical creativity, automatized favorite skills; mental: total commitment, highest
self-confidence, mood self-regulation, mental toughness. This fourth stage is "the most
variable in duration": "almost twenty years" for career-long stars like Klementiev and
Armstrong, "four-seven years" for most high-performance athletes (PDF p. 156, printed
p. 145).

**A cross-source contradiction worth recording, not silently resolving:** this
library's Verkhoshansky/Supertraining entry
([[supertraining--verkhoshansky/claim--russian-sport-school-age-guidelines-table]])
gives Soviet sport-school starting ages for some of the same sports that are
considerably earlier than Issurin's Figure 4.8 bands: women's gymnastics 4-5 vs.
Issurin's gymnastics 7-9; swimming 3-7 vs. Issurin's 9-11; weightlifting 10-11 vs.
Issurin's 11-14. Both tables are cited to different Soviet-era sources (Table 1.2 in
Supertraining is unsourced beyond "the Soviet sports-school system"; Figure 4.8 here
is adapted from Platonov & Sakhnovsky, 1988, plus later expert interviews) and neither
book reconciles the gap. Record both; do not average them into a single number.

**Why it works** — Issurin gives no outcome data for the dosing table (Table 4.6)
itself: no citation, no study comparing athletes trained at these hour/frequency bands
against alternative bands. **Inference:** the domain-by-domain content sequencing
(general before specific, tactical/mental development paced alongside physical)
matches this library's existing multilateral-before-specialization rationale
([[periodization--bompa/principle--multilateral-before-specialization]]), but that
connection is this entry's own synthesis, not stated by Issurin in this passage.

**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a Baylor context** — The domain-by-domain
tables (4.7-4.10) are a usable template for structuring what "developmental content"
means at each career stage of a college program even though college football players
enter well past Issurin's "preliminary preparation" age band: an incoming freshman's
program content (movement-skill consolidation, general work capacity, tactical
acquaintance with the defense/offense) resembles the "initial specialization" /
"advanced specialization" domain mix more than "sports perfection," which better fits
an established starter. The advanced-specialization caution against maximal
anaerobic-glycolytic loading in not-yet-mature, "overly ambitious" young athletes is a
directly relevant caution for redshirt-freshman and true-freshman conditioning design.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference, population caveat:** Table 4.6's specific
hour/frequency bands and Figure 4.8's starting ages are drawn from Olympic individual
and technical sports (gymnastics, swimming, rowing, weightlifting, etc.); American
football is not listed in either this table or the Verkhoshansky comparison table, and no
data source here addresses a contact team sport with a 100+ player roster and
heterogeneous training ages within a single class. The stage-duration bands are the
author's own applied synthesis (cited only to "Issurin, 1994," his own earlier work, not
an independent study), and the domain content lists, while plausible, carry no cited
outcome evidence distinguishing them from an alternative sequencing.

**Example** — **Inference:** using Tables 4.7-4.8's domain content as a checklist for a
true-freshman's first developmental year: technical (movement-pattern competency
before position-specific technique refinement), physical (multilateral work capacity
before narrow specialization), tactical (scheme literacy before disguised assignments),
mental (culture and buy-in before performance-outcome pressure), while applying
Table 4.9's caution against maximal-intensity anaerobic-glycolytic work for players who
are still not fully mature and may be "overly ambitious" relative to their
self-regulation capacity.

# Sensitive Periods: Ages Where a Motor Ability Trains Fastest, By Sex

**What it is** — Issurin defines "sensitive periods" as ages when an individual is "more
trainable for certain motor abilities than in other times," resting on two stated
physiological facts (PDF p. 158, printed p. 147): (a) natural development of motor
abilities in children/youth is non-uniform, so sensitive periods yield "the most
favorable rate of improvement in certain abilities"; (b) acceleration/deceleration
periods for different abilities "do not coincide chronologically." He is explicit that
**chronological determination of sensitive periods "remains open to dispute"**: "each
fitness component can be characterized by various indices, which can give different
(and at times contradictory) chronological trends. This explains the variance in data
from different sources" (PDF p. 158, printed p. 147). Figure 4.9 (PDF p. 159, printed p.
148; **verified against the page image**, based on Meinel & Schnabel 1976, Martin
1980, Volkov 1986) plots the favorable-improvement window per ability, separately for
girls and boys, ages 8-18: general coordination roughly 9-12 for both sexes; flexibility
roughly 7-10 for both; speed (reaction time) roughly 9-11 for both; speed (movement
frequency) roughly 11-13 for both; explosive strength (high/long jump) roughly 11-13
in girls but extending to roughly 13-17 in boys; maximal strength roughly 13-17 in
girls, roughly 14-18 in boys; strength endurance roughly 11-14 in girls but extending to
roughly 11-17 in boys; aerobic endurance roughly 14-17 in girls, roughly 15-18 in
boys. The book's running text gives point estimates matching the chart: general
motor coordination peaks ages 9-12; flexibility ages 7-10; maximal-speed
improvement ages 11-13 in both sexes; reaction time ages 9-11; explosive-strength
(jump) improvement peaks 11-13 in girls, 13-17 in boys; aerobic-development
practice benefit ages 9-12, with the most favorable sensitive window from age 14 in
girls and 15 in boys.

The chapter gives partial, explicitly qualified mechanisms for the strength-related
periods specifically (PDF pp. 159-160, printed pp. 148-149): jump performance
depends on "powerful muscular contractions and body mass," and body mass "increases
most noticeably in mid-puberty girls aged 13-15" while their maximal improvement
rate occurs earlier, at 11-13; strength gains in mid/late puberty are "directly affected
by hormonal changes (maturation) and an increase in muscle mass (growth)"; but
"training-induced hypertrophy is much more pronounced in adults in comparison to
mid-pubertal or late-pubertal children. Therefore, improvement in neural regulation is
the main source of increased explosive and maximal strength" in youth. Androgenic
hormones (e.g. testosterone), which "affect anaerobic power production and muscle
hypertrophy," are "low in children and only start to increase at ages 12-13 in girls, and
13-14 in boys." Aerobic-endurance sensitivity is attributed to "increased body size, and
in particular, muscle mass, increased heart volume, total blood volume, and a higher
hemoglobin concentration." The chapter closes this section with an explicit safety
caution: "the higher sensitivity of children and youth can lead to overload and even
injury. This is particularly relevant in training for maximum strength and in power
exercises" (PDF p. 160, printed p. 149).

**Why it works** — The neural-regulation-over-hypertrophy explanation for youth
strength gains, and the testosterone-onset ages for anaerobic power/hypertrophy, are
standard, independently established developmental physiology, consistent with this
library's existing maturation material; that specific mechanism claim can be treated at
the `proven` ceiling **for the underlying physiology itself** (androgen-driven
hypertrophic capacity rising at puberty; neural adaptation predominating over
hypertrophic adaptation before it). The overall age windows in Figure 4.9, however, stay
at `supported`: the author himself states the chronological boundaries are disputed
across sources, and the figure is a synthesis of three secondary citations (Meinel &
Schnabel 1976; Martin 1980; Volkov 1986), none independently checked in this ingest.

**When to apply — Inference** — Directly relevant to Applied Performance's role in
any camp or clinic work with pre-collegiate athletes, and as background for
understanding why a college freshman's training-age deficits in particular abilities
(e.g., poor general coordination, limited flexibility) may reflect a missed
developmental window rather than a simply "untrained" quality that responds at the
same rate an early-developed quality would. The sex-specific timing differences
(explosive strength and strength endurance windows running longer in boys; aerobic
endurance starting a year later in boys than girls) are a concrete reference for
age-and-sex-differentiated youth programming, where Applied Performance interacts
with prospective-athlete or camp populations.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference, population caveat:** every current Baylor
football player is already past every window shown in Figure 4.9 (which stops at age
18); this framework describes developmental timing, not a claim about how to train
already-matured collegiate athletes, and should not be used to argue that any current
ability gap is now unfixable. The author's own caveat applies directly: chronological
sensitive-period boundaries are disputed, vary by which index is measured, and come
from three secondary sources not independently verified here. Treat the specific age
ranges as a useful planning heuristic for youth-facing work, not a precise biological
clock.

**Example** — **Inference:** a youth combine or camp screening tool that flags,
rather than penalizes, a 9-year-old with below-average flexibility or a 14-year-old boy
with below-average explosive-strength improvement rate as "inside a sensitive
window where targeted work is likely to pay off fastest," consistent with the chapter's
framing that these are periods of highest trainability, not fixed ceilings.

# Four Talent Factors, Graded by How Fixed They Are

**What it is** — Issurin defines giftedness as "a predisposition to and a higher
trainability for a given activity," treated as "genetically transmitted properties of an
individual," such that "a gifted child is potentially a talented athlete" (PDF p. 160,
printed p. 149). Citing Williams & Franks (1998) and Williams & Reilly (2000), he
organizes talent prediction into four factors, each graded by genetic determination
(Table 4.11, PDF p. 161, printed p. 150; **verified against the page image**):
**Body build and body composition**: body height, extremity length, foot size
(strong); shoulder width, thigh circumference, muscle mass (medium); total body fat
(low). **Physiological**: alactic anaerobic power, peak blood lactate, space
orientation (high); glycolytic anaerobic power, strength endurance/acidity resistance,
flexibility (medium). **Psychological**: self-confidence, anxiety control, motivation,
concentration (medium-low), citing Plomin et al. (1994) and Saudino (1997) for a
partial heritable component. **Sociological**: parental support, socio-economic
background, cultural background, coach-child interaction (heredity independent,
though the text adds: "this doesn't mean, however, that they can be changed easily if
necessary"). Issurin's practical point in presenting the table this way: "some
anthropometrical and physiological variables are highly dependent on heredity and as
such, can not be compensated for by other personal traits. Thus, they can restrict
progress in a given sport" (PDF p. 160, printed p. 149): the high-heritability rows
function as hard constraints on which sports/positions an athlete can realistically excel
in, while the low-heritability rows are the ones training and environment can move.

The unchangeable-predictor approach is operationalized through "model
characteristics" (Bulgakova, 1986): favorable anthropometric/physiological
combinations by age category, built from longitudinal study of a large athlete group
where a sub-group reaches elite level (citing Vorontsov et al. 1999 and Falk et al.
2003 as examples of this being done despite its difficulty), or more commonly from
cross-sectional studies comparing less- and more-successful youngsters. Issurin flags
the model characteristics' central limitation directly: "the main limitation of this
evaluation is in the differing levels of maturity of children that are examined to
determine giftedness. Children with slower rates of maturation may be found inferior
to more mature teammates but may have greater potential for further progress" (PDF
p. 160, printed p. 149). Genetic contribution to training response itself is not constant
across a career: citing a young-adult twin study (Bouchard et al., 2000), "the
contribution of heredity related factors to training response in an endurance program
is different for earlier and later stages of preparation. The initial preparation is less
dependent on heredity. However, as athletes progress and approach higher workloads,
the genetic control of their training response becomes much stronger" (PDF p. 167,
printed p. 156), meaning genetically gifted endurance athletes can be genuinely hard
to identify at the earliest stage, not just hard to identify due to maturation confounds.

**Why it works** — The heredity-grading in Table 4.11 is presented without a
citation for most individual cells (the table itself has no source line beyond the
chapter's general references to Williams & Franks/Reilly); it functions as the author's
synthesized taxonomy rather than a reported finding with its own effect sizes. The twin
study citation (Bouchard et al., 2000) is the one mechanism-level claim in this section
with a named, checkable primary source, though it was not independently verified in
this ingest.

**When to apply — Inference** — Directly relevant to how Applied Performance and
recruiting staff frame "traits we can develop" versus "traits we are selecting for":
Table 4.11's strong/medium/low-heritability split is a usable lens for separating
recruiting criteria (anthropometrics, alactic power, space orientation: closer to fixed)
from developmental targets (glycolytic capacity, strength endurance, flexibility,
psychological skills: more trainable), while the maturation-confound caution is a
direct argument for tracking training age and biological maturation alongside any
"talent" read on a young or first-year athlete, consistent with
[[periodization--bompa/heuristic--biological-vs-training-age]].

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference, population caveat:** this taxonomy and its
heritability gradings are drawn from Olympic individual-sport talent-ID research
(swimming, track, rowing-type literature per the cited authors); American football's
position-specific demands are not addressed, and no cell in Table 4.11 is football-
specific. The heritability labels ("strong," "medium," "high," "medium-low") are
qualitative and unsourced at the individual-cell level in this passage; treat them as the
author's organizing synthesis, not as quantified heritability estimates. The
maturation-confound caution applies most directly to youth/pre-collegiate evaluation
and is less relevant once an athlete has finished growth, though the same caution about
mistaking current-training-age effects for fixed talent still applies to a true freshman.

**Example** — **Inference:** using the four-factor split as a talking-point framework
when a coach asks whether a trait is coachable: anthropometrics and alactic power
profile are closer to "select for, don't expect to build"; strength endurance,
flexibility, and psychological-skill factors are closer to "build through training and
sport psychology support," while sociological factors (family support, coach-athlete
relationship) sit entirely outside a strength staff's typical intervention but are named
here as real, modifiable contributors to development.

# Giftedness as Two Measurements: Where You Start, and How Fast You Improve

**What it is** — Issurin presents a practical, "dual approach" to identifying gifted
youngsters, attributed to Bril (1980) with his own modifications. It splits giftedness
into two components (Figure 4.11, PDF p. 164, printed p. 153; **verified against the
page image**): **predisposition to a certain activity**, which determines the athlete's
**initial level** of relevant fitness (speed, endurance, agility, etc.), and
**trainability**, which determines the **improvement rate** during initial preparation.
The diagram also names two confounds feeding into those same two outcomes:
**previous experience** (prior training, test familiarity) feeds initial level alongside
predisposition, and **preparation quality** feeds improvement rate alongside
trainability. Issurin states this directly as a limitation, using a worked illustration
(PDF p. 165, printed p. 154): testing eight-year-olds' swimming fitness, "the behavior
of these children in water will be very different and previous experiences will affect
the results much more strongly than the children's real predisposition to competitive
swimming," because some children already have aquatic experience with parents,
others have had a few lessons, and others none at all. Similarly, improvement rate
"depends not only on an individual's trainability but also on the quality of the
preparation," which is only controlled for "when comparing... athletes training in one
group with the same coach" (PDF p. 165, printed p. 154).

Combining the two axes yields a 2x2-plus-diagonal grid (Figure 4.12, PDF p. 166,
printed p. 155; verified against the page image, based on Bril 1980 and the author's
modifications): High initial level x High improvement rate = "very gifted"; Medium x
Medium = "ordinary gifted"; Low x Low = "not gifted"; the off-diagonal combinations
(High/Medium, Medium/High, Medium/Low, Low/Medium) are graded "gifted" or
"moderately gifted" depending on direction. This diagnosis method "was first
implemented in team and dual sports mostly because of practical needs (Bril, 1980)"
(PDF p. 165, printed p. 154).

Issurin gives explicit, sport-type-differentiated guidance on how long an evaluation
period needs to be to read improvement rate reliably (PDF pp. 166-167, printed pp.
155-156, six points, quoted/paraphrased): (1) absolute unsuitability for a sport (e.g.
"tall and heavy candidates in gymnastics; small children in basketball") can be
recognized quickly; (2) non-gifted candidates are usually recognizable within "about
three-four months" of short-term preliminary preparation; (3) diagnosing giftedness
in maximal speed and power sports needs "a relatively short period... usually... up to
one year"; (4) in highly coordinated sports (gymnastics, figure skating), identification is
limited by the earlier initial-preparation window itself and "takes between 1-2 years";
(5) in team and dual sports, where systematic preparation starts later, very gifted
athletes "can be identified faster (two-three months) but this process usually takes
about one year"; (6) "perhaps the longest period for giftedness identification is needed
in endurance sports, where many world-level athletes are recognized as potential elite
athletes after three-four years of systematic preparation."

**Why it works** — The framework's internal logic (separating a starting-point
measure from a rate-of-change measure, and explicitly naming the confounds on each)
is methodologically sound as a testing design principle and is the author's own stated
reasoning, not an unstated mechanism. **The author gives no outcome data** validating
that athletes graded "very gifted" by this 2x2 method go on to elite performance at
higher rates than those graded lower; the six-point duration guidance is presented as
practical experience-based judgment, not derived from a cited study with a stated
sample.

**When to apply — Inference** — Directly usable as a testing-design principle for any
Applied Performance evaluation of prospective or first-year athletes: separate an
athlete's **starting-point** test score from their **rate of improvement** over a defined
evaluation window, and explicitly track (rather than ignore) how much of the starting
score is explained by prior training exposure versus untrained predisposition. The
sport-type duration guidance (short window for speed/power sports, longer for
technically complex or endurance sports) is a citable reference point for how long a
true-freshman evaluation period should reasonably run before drawing conclusions
about developmental trajectory, and directly supports keeping "trainability" judgments
provisional during a player's first months in the program rather than fixed at
arrival.

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference, population caveat:** the six-point duration
guidance and the underlying dual-approach method are built from and validated (to the
extent they are validated at all, which the author does not claim with data) on
youth-sport identification in individual, technical and endurance Olympic sports, plus
one general mention of "team and dual sports" without football-specific detail. No
duration guidance for American football specifically is given. This method also still
depends on being able to isolate "quality of preparation" (same coach, comparable
program) to make the improvement-rate reading meaningful, which is harder to
guarantee in a large-roster college setting with multiple position coaches and strength
staff than in Bril's presumed small-group youth-sport context.

**Example** — **Inference:** a true-freshman evaluation protocol that records each
player's testing-day starting values (with a note on prior training background,
per the swimming illustration above) separately from their improvement rate across
the first off-season, and treats "trainability" as unresolved until that improvement-rate
window has actually elapsed, rather than reading it off the initial test alone.

# 320 US Select Swimmers: Early Maturers Look Best Young, Late Maturers Stay in the Sport

**What it is** — Issurin cites a cross-sectional study of 320 swimmers, ages 11-18,
participants in the USA Select Program, examined for "biological age, anthropometric
status, muscle strength and power, swim-specific abilities and performance time" (PDF
p. 162, printed p. 151, citing Troup et al., 1991). The stated finding: "the best athletes
at younger ages are usually more mature than their less successful counterparts while
the best athletes in the older ages are mostly on-time maturers (80% of the US
National team) or late maturers (18% of the team)." The book's own gloss on the
authors' interpretation: "the authors suggest that high performing late maturers have
better chances of remaining involved with the sport for longer periods" (PDF p. 162,
printed p. 151).

**A tension worth recording, not resolving silently:** this case sits in some
tension with the chapter's own general framing of talent identification around
"unchangeable predictors, most often associated with anthropometric and
physiological factors" (Table 4.11 and the surrounding text,
[[framework--athletic-talent-four-factor-heredity-model]]). If the athletes who look
best at younger ages (by early anthropometric/physiological maturation) are
disproportionately *not* the ones who remain in, or lead, the sport by the older age
bracket, then early anthropometric standing is a poor stand-alone predictor of who
eventually succeeds, which is consistent with, and directly reinforces, Issurin's own
separately stated maturation-confound caution ("children with slower rates of
maturation may be found inferior to more mature teammates but may have greater
potential for further progress," PDF p. 160, printed p. 149) rather than contradicting it.
The tension is between the chapter's early framing (identify talent via largely fixed,
heredity-linked traits) and this case's finding (the early-identified "best" are not
reliably the eventual best), and the author does not explicitly connect the two passages
himself.

**Why it works** — **The author gives no mechanism** beyond restating the study
authors' own suggested interpretation (late maturers "remaining involved... for longer
periods," implying either better long-term retention, a longer competitive runway once
peers catch up physically, or both). No causal pathway (psychological, physiological, or
selection/dropout-driven) is specified in the passage.

**When to apply — Inference** — Directly relevant caution for any recruiting or
early talent-evaluation process: a young athlete who is currently outperforming peers
due to early physical maturation is not reliably the athlete who will still be
outperforming peers once maturation differences equalize, and a currently
underperforming, physically less-mature athlete of the same chronological age may
have a longer developmental runway. This is a concrete, citable data point supporting
[[periodization--bompa/heuristic--biological-vs-training-age]]'s general caution against
treating early-maturer dominance as durable talent.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is untested.** This is a single cross-sectional
study (not longitudinal: it compares different athletes at younger and older ages, not
the same athletes tracked over time) in one sport (swimming), one country's development
pipeline (USA Select Program, 1991), with no report of effect size, statistical test, or
confidence interval in the passage as retold. It should not be read as showing that any
individual late-maturing young athlete will become elite, only that the population-level
pattern the study reports runs counter to naively selecting only the most physically
advanced young athletes. The finding has not been checked here against the original
Troup et al. (1991) publication; only the book's retelling was read.

**Example** — **Inference:** when evaluating a cohort of high-school or youth
prospects, treating early physical dominance as one input rather than the deciding
factor, and tracking (rather than deprioritizing) athletes who are chronologically
age-matched but physically less mature, consistent with this case's reported pattern
that late maturers were disproportionately represented among the eventual national
team.

# 35 Elite Soviet Paddlers: What Age-14-15 Performance Looked Like Before They Were Elite

**What it is** — Issurin cites a retrospective interview study of 35 "world leading
canoe-kayak paddlers who took part in the USSR National team and earned medals in
the Olympic Games and World Championships within eight years prior to the study"
(PDF p. 163, printed p. 152, citing Sozin, 1986). Respondents were interviewed about
their official performance results from ages 14-15, "the end of their first year of
long-term preparation"; athletes who began preparation later were excluded from the
analysis. **Verified against the page image (Figure 4.10, PDF p. 163, printed p. 152):**
performance time on a 500-m course is plotted by two-year age band (14-15 through
18-19) for three groups (kayak-males, kayak-females, canoeists), all three showing a
steep improvement curve from roughly 140-152 seconds at 14-15 down to roughly
102-120 seconds by 18-19, with kayak-males consistently fastest across every age band
and kayak-females and canoeists tracking close together. The book frames the result as
a usable historical benchmark rather than a fixed standard: "despite the substantial
improvement of paddles and boats in the two decades since this study was conducted,
the results achieved by outstanding athletes in the earlier stages of their preparation
remain relevant to this day for evaluation of giftedness in today's young athletes" (PDF
p. 163, printed p. 152).

**Why it works** — **The author gives no mechanism** connecting age-14-15
performance time to eventual elite status; the case is presented purely as a descriptive
benchmark curve, not as a validated predictive model (no reported correlation,
sensitivity, or false-positive/false-negative rate for using these times to screen
today's youngsters). Issurin's own claim that equipment-era changes do not invalidate
the benchmark is asserted, not tested against any comparison of era-adjusted times.

**When to apply — Inference** — As a template, not as transferable numbers: the
approach of interviewing an already-elite cohort about their own performance at a
fixed early-career age point, to build a retrospective reference curve for evaluating
today's youngsters, is a reusable talent-identification method independent of the
specific canoe-kayak times. It illustrates the general "model characteristics" approach
named earlier in the chapter (Bulgakova, 1986,
[[framework--athletic-talent-four-factor-heredity-model]]) with a concrete worked
example of how such a benchmark curve is actually built and reported.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is untested.** This is a retrospective,
survivorship-selected sample: all 35 respondents were already-successful medalists at
the time of interview, so the study cannot say what fraction of 14-15-year-olds with
similar performance times failed to reach the same level (no comparison group of
similarly-fast 14-15-year-olds who did not become elite). It is also sport- and era-specific
(1970s-80s Soviet canoe-kayak, single national federation, pre-modern boat and
paddle equipment) with no cited data from any other sport or country, and the book's
own claim that "the results... remain relevant to this day" is the author's assertion, not
a re-tested comparison against a current cohort. Do not use the specific second-count
benchmarks as a screening cutoff for any current sport without independent
verification.

**Example** — **Inference:** the method (not the numbers) could be adapted for a
sport Applied Performance evaluates by asking current or recent elite alumni for their
own testing numbers at a fixed early developmental checkpoint (e.g. end of a true
freshman year), building an internal reference curve for how "what elite eventually
looked like at this checkpoint" compares to a current prospect, while explicitly
tracking that such a curve, built only from athletes who succeeded, cannot say how
many similarly-scoring peers did not.
