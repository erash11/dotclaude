# Residual Training Effects Last Different Numbers of Days for Different Motor Abilities

**What it is** — The residual training effect is defined as "the retention of changes
induced by systematic workloads beyond a certain time period after the cessation of
training," a term Issurin attributes to Brian and James Counsilman (1991) (PDF p. 33,
printed p. 22). Issurin & Lustig (2004) is cited as the source of Table 1.6 (PDF p. 35,
printed p. 24), giving residual duration ± SD in days after cessation of training for each
motor ability: **aerobic endurance 30 ± 5 days**, **maximum strength 30 ± 5 days**,
**anaerobic glycolytic endurance 18 ± 4 days**, **strength endurance 15 ± 5 days**,
**maximum speed (alactic) 5 ± 3 days**. Table 1.6's own physiological-background column
lists aerobic endurance's residual as resting on increased aerobic enzymes, mitochondria,
muscle capillaries, hemoglobin capacity, glycogen storage, and fat-metabolism rate, and
maximum strength's on improved neural mechanism and muscle hypertrophy from fiber
enlargement, with no percentages attached in the table itself. Later in the text (PDF p. 35,
printed p. 24), Issurin gives a specific figure for one of those table items: aerobic
enzymes increase by 40-90% with training (Volkov, 1986; Fox et al., 1993; Wilmore & Costill,
1993). He contrasts this with much smaller local adaptations from sprint training:
phosphocreatine storage (+2-5%), peak lactate accumulation (+10-20%), and anaerobic enzymes
(+2-20%), given without a citation on that page. On the following page (PDF p. 36, printed
p. 25), Thorstensson (1988) is cited specifically for "relatively small increases" in
quickly available energy sources, ATP and phosphocreatine, and enzymes such as creatine
kinase, from highly concentrated sprint training.

**Why it works** — Issurin states the mechanism directly (PDF pp. 35-36, printed pp. 24-25):
aerobic ability, supported by pronounced morphological and biochemical changes, is retained
"for a number of weeks at near-peak level," while anaerobic abilities, particularly maximum
sprint speed, "retain near-peak levels for much shorter periods" because peak speed's gains
are smaller and rest on "very delicate and highly precise neuro-muscular interactions, which
are relatively unstable and cannot be maintained at the highest level without specially
organized training." Maximum strength's comparably long residual is attributed to pronounced
morphological, biochemical, and neural changes: enlargement of muscle-fiber cross-sectional
area, hyperplasia, recruitment of previously inactive motor units, synchronization of their
activity, and increased motor-neuron discharge frequency (Zatsiorsky, 1995). Strength
endurance's residual is stated to depend on event duration and the degree of
anaerobic-resource mobilization; longer-duration strength endurance has a relatively longer
residual thanks to aerobic adaptation.

**When to apply** — Sequencing which ability to develop first in a training stage (see
[[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]]). **Inference:** an ability
with a long residual (aerobic endurance, max strength, 30 ± 5 d) can be built early and
carried further without direct work than a short-residual one, but a stage longer than about
30 days after accumulation ends needs a retention dose (see Example); max speed (5 ± 3 d)
needs re-exposure close to competition. **Eric's baseline directly echoes half of this table:** "Training
residual effects set the retraining cadence: aerobic capacity and max strength both need
re-exposure roughly every 30 days; speed needs re-exposure roughly every 5 days or tissue
readiness for max-velocity work degrades" (`method-baseline.md`, "Periodization and
programming," citing `01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md` p.27). This 30/30/5-day
pattern matches Issurin's 30±5 / 30±5 / 5±3 figures because it is this table:
week1-lecture.md (marker p.66) reproduces Table 1.6 directly, under a slide block that names
"Issurin (Block Training Text)" as a source (marker p.64), and gives strength endurance as
~12 days against Issurin's 15±5, the one value where the course departs from the book.

**When NOT to apply** — Treat the numbers as a synthesized rule of thumb
(±5, ±4, ±5, ±3 day spreads are themselves wide relative to the means), not a precise
per-athlete clock; Issurin does not report the sample or method behind Issurin & Lustig
(2004) in this text, so the table's evidentiary base cannot be checked further from this
source alone. Issurin's own surrounding text also notes limits on how far residual-effect
thinking travels: maximum oxygen uptake in highly trained endurance athletes falls when
weekly training volume drops below a certain level (Steinacker, 1993; Steinacker et al.,
1998), and large volumes of highly intense exercise do not prevent detraining and loss of
aerobic endurance during a taper (Mujika, 1999) (PDF p. 34, printed p. 23).

**Contested, flag this directly to Eric:** a *different* part of the same baseline corpus
states the opposite ordering for detraining rate: "the traits easiest to build (aerobic) are
lost fastest under detraining; the traits hardest to build (alactic/speed) are lost slowest"
(`method-baseline.md`, "Conditioning and energy systems," citing `class-4-2026.md` p.47).
This conflicts with Table 1.6 (aerobic residual is the *longest*, tied with strength, at
30±5 days, while alactic speed is the *shortest* at 5±3 days). The class-1 transcript is
not a third, independent data point against class-4-2026.md: it also states that aerobic
capacity is gained and lost very quickly (marker pp.25, 28), gives its 30-day retention
windows as conditional on "a small retention load" continuing during that window (marker
p.28), a different construct from Issurin's residual *after cessation* of training, and
ties its 5-day speed figure to connective-tissue and nervous-system readiness ("otherwise
the nervous system doesn't change a whole lot," marker p.28) rather than to speed itself
decaying in 5 days. The course's own numbers come directly from this table (week1-lecture.md
marker pp.64, 66, strength endurance given there as ~12 days against Issurin's 15±5). Resolve
with Eric: this may be residual effect after cessation (Issurin, Table 1.6) versus rate of
loss without any retention dose (class-4-2026.md), two different constructs the corpus does
not itself reconcile. Do not harmonize these; record all three with their locators and keep
`contested`, with this as the stated reason.

**Inference:** this table also converges with, but is not identical to, the vault's
Verkhoshansky-staging entry on heterochronic adaptation
([[supertraining--verkhoshansky/claim--heterochronic-biochemical-adaptation-sequence]],
staging), which describes a *different* mechanism (order of biochemical sub-processes
gained/lost) rather than absolute day-counts per motor ability; both describe
heterochronicity in de-training, but at different levels of description; do not treat them
as restating the same specific claim.

**Example** — **Inference:** in a training stage that opens with 4 weeks of accumulation
(building aerobic base and max strength) followed by 3 weeks of transmutation and 2 weeks of
realization, competition falls 35 days after accumulation ends: at the outer edge of the
aerobic and strength residuals' 30 ± 5-day window, not safely inside it. A coach relying on
this table would want a maintenance dose of both qualities somewhere in transmutation, not
an assumption that accumulation-phase gains are still fully in hand. Maximum speed work
needs re-introduction within about 5 days of the target competition, or its residual will
have decayed well below peak by competition day.

**Related** — [[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]],
[[periodization--bompa/heuristic--fatigue-fitness-3to1-ratio]] (a ratio-based rule for
fatigue-vs-fitness decay, distinct from this table's absolute per-ability day counts:
cross-reference, do not conflate),
[[supertraining--verkhoshansky/framework--fitness-fatigue-modeling-and-taper-timing]] (staging),
[[supertraining--verkhoshansky/claim--heterochronic-biochemical-adaptation-sequence]] (staging).

# Realization Mesocycle: One to Three Weeks, Much Lower Load, Built Around Maximal Speed and Rehearsed Competition Behavior

**What it is** — The realization mesocycle is "traditionally... termed the taper" (PDF p. 122,
printed p. 111; verified against page image). In traditional periodization the taper precedes
important competitions (the book does not itself say "only"); under Block Periodization it is
broader, forming the
concluding phase of **every** training stage (see
[[framework--training-stage-and-multi-peak-annual-cycle]]), so a season contains several
realization mesocycles of differing weight depending on the upcoming competition's importance.
Athletes enter this mesocycle fatigued from the preceding transmutation mesocycle, so the first
objective is restoration toward supercompensation by competition day; the targeted abilities
(maximal speed/quickness, event-specific tactics, competition readiness) all require high CNS
reactivity, available energy resources, and mental concentration, states only available to a
well-rested athlete, which is why workload reduction is described as "of paramount importance"
at the mesocycle's start. Table 3.12 (PDF p. 123, printed p. 112; verified against page image)
gives the full prescription: **aims**, peak-performance attainment, full restoration before
competition, and completing the training stage's overall program (peaking demands scale with
the competition's level/importance); **targeted abilities**, maximal speed/quickness,
event-specific tactics, readiness to compete; **workload level**, "much lower than in
preceding mesocycles," with various approaches to how that reduction is achieved (the
"valid" qualifier is the writer's, not the book's);
**duration**, **one to three weeks**, depending on competition importance and sport-specific
factors; **techno-tactical improvement**, via sport-specific modeled exercises/tasks that must
build genuine competitive performance (the "not just rehearsal" framing is the writer's own
gloss on this requirement, not the book's wording); **emotional tension/anxiety**,
elevates as competition approaches, more so for higher-stakes competition; **monitoring**,
follow-up time-trials, techno-tactical execution quality, training responses, and restoration
level, focused on integrative sport-specific estimates and each athlete's most important
individual traits; **nutrition**, deliberate use of supplements and energy-intake control to
produce an ergogenic effect and prevent unwanted body-composition change. On workload-reduction
timing: a **short** mesocycle demands a fast decrease; a **longer** one allows gradual
reduction; duration itself is "extremely important and sensitive" because a too-long taper
risks detraining while a too-short one may not restore and develop event-specific abilities
adequately (a contradiction Issurin attributes to Mujika et al., 2004). Techno-tactical
simulation must (1) reproduce the competitive situation as closely as possible, (2) approximate
competitive concentration levels, and (3) repeat enough to build stable, reliable skill. Issurin also gives the taper's own mood evidence, separate from the anxiety discussion below:
"workload reduction during the taper causes a remarkable improvement in mood associated mainly
with improved recovery" (citing a review by Mujika et al., 2004, PDF p. 124, printed p. 113).
Table
3.13 (PDF p. 124, printed p. 113) gives a worked dosage for a **two-week realization mesocycle
for qualified kayakers**: quasi-competitive race performance, 4-6 total; race simulation in
broken series (four quarters, 20s breaks), 8-12; selective simulation of the initial race
quarter, 10-16; selective simulation of the two mid-race quarters, 8-12; simulation of the
pre-event warm-up program, 3-5. On emotional tension: pre-competition anxiety can cut either
way, moderate anxiety facilitates performance, excessive anxiety detracts from it, and elite
athletes are reported to benefit more from increased anxiety than sub-elite athletes, while
mid-class athletes can be "sharpened" by emotional tension (Raglin & Wilson, 2000, stated this
way in the source; the sub-elite/mid-class distinction is not further clarified there). Table
3.14 (PDF p. 125, printed p. 114) gives realization-specific monitoring indicators: sprint-
repetition and simulation totals; maximal-speed and event-simulation test results; effort
perception; HR and blood lactate after relevant exercises; CPK and urea; resting HR; sleep
quality and mood questionnaire; body mass and fat component. Issurin also states four benefits
of keeping the monitoring program consistent across successive realization mesocycles within a
season (PDF p. 125, printed p. 114): coaches can validate the test set against real
competitive outcomes over time; individual (not just group) norms can be established for each
athlete; athletes themselves learn to read their own responses; and self-regulation and
body-mass management methods can be set in advance rather than improvised. Issurin adds a
specific nutrition warning, illustrated by a case
study: **20 highly qualified male long-distance triathletes**, tracked across a 4-week
high-workload mesocycle into a **2-week taper mesocycle** (PDF pp. 126-127, printed pp.
115-116, based on Margaritis et al., 2003): average energy intake stayed roughly
flat. The text states energy expenditure "decreased... by up to 69.3%" during the taper (PDF
p. 126, printed p. 115); Figure 3.10 (PDF p. 127, printed p. 116; re-verified against the page
image, 2026-09-27) instead plots reduced-workload expenditure at about **69% of** the
high-workload level, i.e. roughly a 31% fall, not a 69% one (the chart's own axis, 50-110%,
could not plot a fall to about 31% of baseline). This is a source inconsistency, not a
transcription error on this entry's part; the figure reading is the more plausible one given
the axis range, and both are recorded here rather than silently harmonized. Mean body mass
didn't change, but body fat mass **increased by 4.3%** (about 104.3% of baseline, matching the
figure). Issurin's point: unchanged body mass can mask
a real fat gain (or muscle loss) if eating habits don't adjust downward with the drop in
training energy cost, so body mass alone is "not always a reliable" anthropometric indicator
during a taper. **Inference, from the primary abstract (Margaritis et al., 2003, *Journal of
the American College of Nutrition* 22:147-156):** this was a double-blind antioxidant-
supplementation trial with a supplemented group (n = 7) and a placebo group (n = 9), 16
athletes analyzed, not 20 as the book states; the abstract does not report the energy-balance
or body-composition figures the book cites, so those specific numbers could not be verified
against the primary source and should be treated as the book's own retelling.

**Why it works** — The core mechanism is the same reduced-load-toward-supercompensation logic
already given for the pre-competitive microcycle
(see [[framework--pre-competitive-microcycle-tuning]], which is this mesocycle's dominant
microcycle type): the targeted abilities need a well-rested nervous system and available energy
stores, so load must come down before those specific qualities can actually be trained and
expressed. The duration-sensitivity point (too long detrains, too short under-restores) is
Issurin's own applied synthesis, though he cites Mujika et al. (2004), a real, checkable
taper-physiology review, as the source flagging this contradiction in the field generally,
not something Issurin resolves with his own data here.

**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college football calendar** — **Bowl prep** is the
clearest college-football analog to a full one-to-three-week realization mesocycle: workload
should come down substantially from whatever preceded it (regular-season maintenance load, or a
short rebuild block if the last-game-to-bowl gap was used as a mini accumulation/transmutation
sequence, see [[framework--prolonging-residual-effects-with-mini-blocks]]), practice time
should shift toward maximal-speed/explosive work and heavily rehearsed, opponent-specific
game-plan simulation (the Table 3.13 dosage pattern, adapted from race-quarters to game-plan
segments, e.g., specific down-and-distance packages, specific special-teams looks), and staff
should watch emotional tension deliberately given a long layoff and a non-conference,
lower-frequency opponent-preparation rhythm. **The gap between the final regular-season/
conference-championship game and the bowl game** is exactly the scheduling problem Issurin's
duration-sensitivity warning describes: too long a "taper-only" approach across that whole gap
risks detraining (per Mujika et al., 2004, and consistent with
[[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]]'s ~30-day residual windows for
basic abilities), which is the direct rationale for treating the early part of that gap as a
short accumulation/transmutation rebuild and only the final 1-3 weeks before the bowl as this
realization mesocycle. The nutrition warning (unchanged eating during a lower-workload window
risks fat gain even at stable body mass) is directly transferable to any football taper or bowl
prep window and worth flagging to Performance Nutrition specifically, since body-composition
oversight sits with that separate Health & Wellness unit per Eric's baseline
(`method-baseline.md`, "Nutrition and body composition"). **Eric's baseline** gives no duration
band or nutrition-adjustment guidance for a taper window anywhere in `method-baseline.md`; the
closest material is the general in-season rule "maintain intensity, reduce volume," citing
`01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md` pp.20-21, which is compatible in direction but supplies
none of this entry's specificity (the one-to-three-week band, the competition-importance
scaling, or the body-composition-during-taper warning).

**When NOT to apply** — **Inference, population caveat:** every piece of
supporting data here
(the kayaker simulation dosage, the triathlete nutrition case) comes from individual endurance
sports with single, clearly-defined competitive efforts; football has no equivalent single
"race" to simulate, and a "game" cannot be rehearsed at full competitive intensity the way a
kayak race segment can. The triathlete case (per the primary abstract) is n=16 (7+9), one study,
one sport, with no reported statistical test of the 4.3% fat-mass change's significance in this
text: treat the direction (unadjusted eating during a taper risks fat gain) as a plausible,
citable caution, not a precise, guaranteed effect size for a football population with very
different body-composition targets and energy demands than a long-distance triathlete. The 1-3
week duration band and the anxiety-by-athlete-level claim (Raglin & Wilson, 2000) are stated
without further detail on that study's own population or method in this text. This mesocycle's
emotional-tension guidance assumes a single identifiable peak competition; it does not address
a multi-game bowl-adjacent context (e.g., a conference championship immediately followed by a
short turnaround into a bowl selection process) where emotional tension may need to be managed
across two closely spaced high-stakes events rather than one clean taper. **Inference, from the
staging Verkhoshansky fitness-fatigue entry:** Fitz-Clarke et al. (1991) found "presently
accepted taper protocols may be too rigorous and short for best results"
([[supertraining--verkhoshansky/framework--fitness-fatigue-modeling-and-taper-timing]],
staging). This sits in tension with Issurin's own position that a short realization mesocycle
"demands a fast workload decrease" (PDF p. 123, printed p. 112): Fitz-Clarke's caution and
Issurin's fast-decrease prescription for short tapers point in opposite directions on how
aggressive a short taper's load cut should be, and this text does not resolve that tension.

**Example** — **Inference:** a three-week bowl-prep plan could open with a lighter first week (still
carrying some team-practice volume, closer to a short transmutation-mesocycle tail) that
tapers hard into a genuine one-to-two-week realization phase immediately before the game,
with practice time shifting toward explosive work and rehearsed, opponent-specific situational
football, energy intake reviewed alongside the drop in practice volume, and staff watching for
either excess complacency (too little anxiety, given the layoff and often lower-stakes framing
of some bowl games) or excess anxiety in players for whom the bowl carries personal stakes
(e.g., a senior's final game, an NFL draft evaluation window).

# A Two-Mini-Block Weekly Structure for In-Season Team Sports, Naming American Football Directly

**What it is** — Issurin addresses the specific constraint that team and dual sports (he
names **soccer, American football, rugby, volleyball** explicitly) run a 20-35 week season
where "the possibility of inserting highly concentrated training blocks is very limited"
(PDF pp. 108-109, printed pp. 97-98). He cites Fleck and Kraemer (1997): players typically
maintain strength near pre-season levels for 14-16 weeks, after which strength variables
decrease dramatically. Because the usual one-week gap between games is too short to work
effectively on every training target (metabolic conditioning, maximal/explosive strength,
injury prevention, techno-tactical skill, game-specific endurance, active restoration) at once,
Issurin proposes adapting three BPC approaches at the *week* level: high concentration of
specialized workloads within **mini-blocks**, separating mini-blocks by training modality, and
determining **key-workouts** within each mini-block. The worked example (Figure 3.8, PDF p.
109, printed p. 98, "based on Baker, 2001," a real cited source, not an uncited synthesis)
splits a standard Monday-Sunday week into two mini-blocks. **Mini-block 1 (Monday-Wednesday)**
targets sport-specific maximal and explosive strength as the dominant training aim: it opens
Monday with an active-recovery session (medium/low load, aerobic and general conditioning),
then delivers the strength emphasis across **two key-workouts** (Tuesday and Wednesday
mornings, maximal strength plus explosive strength as the secondary modality), combined with a
medium-load team practice (Tuesday afternoon) and a conditioning-restorative workout
(Wednesday afternoon). This sequencing leaves **28-30 hours of recovery** after the Wednesday
strength key-workout before mini-block 2's team-practice key-workout begins, to let the
anabolic/restorative process run before the next demand.
**Mini-block 2 (Thursday-Saturday)** targets sport-specific team practice and immediate game
preparation: it includes two obligatory team practices, one before the day off (Thursday
afternoon, itself the microcycle's **third key-workout**, "substantial or big" load, intended
to deliver a pronounced game-specific stimulus) and a second, load-restricted session
immediately before the game itself (Saturday, mostly techno-tactical drills tied to the
specific forthcoming opponent). Figure 3.8 shows a total of three key-workouts across the
week, not two. Issurin notes some highly motivated teams skip the day off and add an optional
Friday-morning conditioning or techno-tactical session instead, trading recovery time for
extra preparation; he states the tradeoff explicitly without endorsing either choice. The
chart gives priority to maximal-strength development over explosive-type work within the week,
which Issurin explains as protecting "somatic, functional and anabolic status," while noting a
cited counter-example (Newton et al., 2006) of sequencing classic high-resistance training with
ballistic explosive-type microcycles to enhance jump performance instead.

**Why it works** — Issurin's own stated reason (PDF p. 111, printed p. 100): weekly
competition stress "restrict[s] the benefits of utilizing BP mesocycle sequencing. However,
the benefits of accentuating dominant training targets, in a mini-block structure, and
designing key-workouts can be attained." **Inference:** this is the chapter's direct answer to
the constraint stated in [[principle--concentration-of-training-workloads]] (BPC needs
concentrated, sequenced blocks) meeting the constraint that team sports cannot suspend
competition long enough to run full-length accumulation/transmutation/realization mesocycles
(see [[framework--accumulation-mesocycle-duration-and-monitoring]] and
[[framework--transmutation-mesocycle-duration-and-overtraining-monitoring]] for what those
normally require in duration); splitting the week itself into two purpose-specific mini-blocks,
each with its own key-workouts, is a way of keeping some of BPC's concentration benefit even
though the underlying block can only be one week long, not several, but the author frames it
as attaining benefits under a restriction, not as a full substitute for mesocycle sequencing.

**When to apply: this is the entry most directly answering the in-season half of the
strength-and-conditioning miss-log's "peaking phase structure for collegiate football" gap
(logged 2026-09-03)** — Issurin explicitly names American football as a sport this structure
is meant for. **Eric's baseline's** in-season rule, "maintain intensity, reduce volume"
(`method-baseline.md`, "Strength and power," citing `01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md`
pp.20-21), is compatible with and gets a concrete weekly shape from this framework: the
early-week mini-block is where intensity (maximal/explosive strength) gets its two key-workouts,
while the late-week mini-block is deliberately techno-tactical and load-restricted heading into
the game, which *is* the volume reduction the baseline rule calls for. **Inference:** the
28-30 hour recovery window between the strength mini-block and the practice-heavy mini-block
gives a specific, citable number for how much gap a college program might target between its
heaviest in-season lift and the next high-intensity team session; Issurin does not himself
generalize this figure beyond the Figure 3.8 worked example.

**When NOT to apply** — The specific day-by-day chart (Figure 3.8) is built from
one cited source, Baker (2001), a study of professional and college-aged rugby league players'
in-season concurrent training: a real, checkable citation, but a single study in rugby league,
a sport with a different weekly game/practice rhythm than college football, which layers
additional constraints (NCAA-mandated practice-time limits, travel, and a Saturday-only game
schedule rather than rugby league's varying weekly structure). **Population caveat:** none of
this section's evidence comes from American football itself; Issurin lists
football alongside soccer, rugby, and volleyball as sports the *problem* applies to, but the
worked example and its supporting citation are rugby-specific. **Inference:** the specific
"two key-workouts, 28-30 hours recovery" prescription should be treated as a structural
template to adapt to football's own practice-count and padded/unpadded-practice rules, not a
ready-made in-season lift schedule. Issurin gives no guidance here for bye weeks, short
(Thursday-game) weeks, or the postseason gap before a bowl game; those fall outside this
weekly-cycle framework and are closer to the pre-competitive/realization material in
[[framework--pre-competitive-microcycle-tuning]] and
[[framework--prolonging-residual-effects-with-mini-blocks]].

**Example** — **Inference, following Figure 3.8's actual pattern (PDF p. 109, printed p. 98)
for a Sunday game:** Monday AM active recovery/conditioning; Tuesday AM maximal-strength
key-workout (plus explosive strength), Tuesday PM medium-load team practice; Wednesday AM
second maximal-strength key-workout (plus explosive strength, bigger load), Wednesday PM
active-recovery/conditioning; Thursday PM key team practice (substantial-to-big load); Friday
off; Saturday AM team practice/technical work (medium load); game Sunday. For a Saturday-game
week, this pattern would need to shift roughly one day earlier across the board (e.g., the two
strength key-workouts moving to Sunday-Monday and the key team practice to Wednesday), since
Issurin's own worked example assumes a Sunday game and does not itself address the shift.

# When a Training Stage Outruns the 30-Day Residual Clock, Insert a Mini-Block, Don't Just Add Workouts

**What it is** — This section directly answers the mismatch already flagged in
[[framework--transmutation-mesocycle-duration-and-overtraining-monitoring]]: the accumulation
mesocycle's basic-ability residual runs out at about **30 days** (Table 1.6; see
[[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]]), which caps how long the
following transmutation-plus-realization stretch can run before basic abilities decay below
target. But Issurin notes many sports need their transmutation mesocycle to run **3-4 weeks**
for anaerobic glycolytic development, and the realization mesocycle (taper) can run **about two
weeks**: together, roughly **five weeks**, which already exceeds the ~30-day strength-aerobic
residual (PDF pp. 129-130, printed pp. 118-119; verified against page image). Issurin's stated
fix: do **not** simply add extra aerobic or strength workouts into the transmutation mesocycle
to "top up" the decaying residual. This fails because the highly intense transmutation
mesocycle actively suppresses the immediate effect of aerobic training, and the anabolic
strength stimulus needs recovery time the transmutation mesocycle cannot spare. Instead, insert
a **special compact mini-block (a short microcycle)** dedicated to the decaying ability. Figure
3.12 (PDF p. 130, printed p. 119; re-verified against the page image, 2026-09-27) diagrams the
resulting pattern for a training stage, in sequence: **accumulation** (high-volume basic
aerobic-strength workloads, **20-25 days**), then **transmutation** (highly intensive specific
workloads, **20-25 days**), then **realization** (taper and maximal speed, **10-16 days**), then
**competition**. The figure's "Mini-block inclusion" callout points to more than one insertion,
not a single one: an accumulation-type (aerobic-strength) patch sits inside the transmutation
span, a transmutation-type (highly intensive specific) patch sits inside the realization span,
and a second accumulation-type patch also sits inside the realization span: three mini-block
insertions total across the diagram, not one. Issurin gives a worked contrast of two
real scheduling situations (PDF p. 131, printed p. 120): when two targeted competitions are set
**a five-week interval** apart (his cited example: kayaking's European and World Championships,
which happen to fall five to six weeks apart in a typical calendar),
the fix is to compress the whole stage (10-14 days accumulation, 10-14 days transmutation, 8-10
days realization), short enough that the accumulation mesocycle's own residual still covers the
whole stage, so **no mini-block is needed at all**. When circumstances instead force a longer,
**7-9 week** training stage (his example: pre-Olympic preparation, where the international
calendar has no tune-up competitions before the Games), the recommended answer is to plan longer
mesocycles **and** include the mini-block, because a stage that long cannot otherwise carry the
accumulation-phase residual all the way to competition. **Inference:** the chapter's own summary
restates the placement rule concretely: an **aerobic mini-block can be inserted inside the
transmutation mesocycle**, and a **highly intense anaerobic mini-block can be inserted inside
the realization mesocycle** (PDF p. 132, printed p. 121); reading this alongside Figure 3.12's
three patches, each mesocycle gets a short dose of whichever ability it does not otherwise
train, timed to arrive before that ability's own residual clock runs out (this synthesis
across the figure and the summary sentence is the writer's own connection, not stated by
Issurin in one place).

**Why it works** — The author gives a reason why *added workouts* fail, but hedges the
proposed remedy rather than explaining why it succeeds. His stated reason additional workouts
fail, scoped to the transmutation mesocycle specifically: the transmutation mesocycle
"strongly suppresses the immediate effect of aerobic training," and added strength work
"require[s] sufficient recovery that cannot be provided" inside a mesocycle built for a
different, fatiguing purpose (PDF p. 130, printed p. 119). For the remedy itself, Issurin
writes only: "**Apparently**, what should be inserted in the mesocycle is not several
additional workouts but a special compact mini-block (short microcycle), in order to prolong
training residuals" (PDF p. 130, printed p. 119), his own hedge word, not a stated mechanism
for why a discrete mini-block succeeds where added workouts fail. No outcome data for the
mini-block approach is presented in this section; Figure 3.12 cites Issurin & Lustig (2004), a
coaching-practice article in *Leistungssport*, not a controlled study. **Inference:** a
plausible reason a discrete mini-block might succeed is that it is short enough to insert
without abandoning the surrounding mesocycle's main purpose while still being its own coherent
loading-then-recovery unit rather than scattered single sessions, but this is the writer's own
extrapolation, not something Issurin states.

**When to apply: this is the entry most directly responsive to the strength-and-conditioning
miss-log's "peaking phase structure for collegiate football" gap (logged 2026-09-03), and the
clearest football calendar mapping in this chapter, Inference throughout** —

- **The gap between the final regular-season or conference-championship game and a bowl game**
  (commonly roughly 3-6 weeks depending on bowl selection and game date) is structurally the
  same scheduling decision Issurin poses in his worked example. A **short** gap (roughly
  5-6 weeks or less) should be handled like his compressed-stage case: run a short
  accumulation-transmutation-realization sequence sized to fit inside the gap (e.g., 10-14 days
  rebuild / 10-14 days sharpen / remaining days as realization/taper), in which case a
  strength-aerobic residual built at the very start plausibly survives to the bowl game without
  needing a mini-block. A **longer** gap (closer to 6+ weeks, which happens for some
  earlier-selected bowls) more closely resembles his 7-9 week pre-Olympic case: plan a longer
  rebuild phase **and** insert an aerobic (or strength) mini-block partway through the
  bowl-prep transmutation-equivalent work, specifically to protect the basic conditioning base
  built early in the gap from decaying below target by game day.
- **In-season, week to week:** [[case-study--popov-competition-shortens-residual-effects]]
  already shows that a hard game can itself accelerate residual decay; a bye week, or a week
  after an unusually taxing game, is a natural, calendar-given opportunity to run something like
  Issurin's aerobic mini-block (a few days of concentrated aerobic-strength work) to "top up" a
  basic-ability residual before it drops further, rather than treating every week's training
  content as identical regardless of how the prior weeks' games affected the athletes.
- **Eric's baseline** does not currently name a mini-block concept or an explicit bowl-prep
  periodization structure at all. **Inference:** this is the writer's own assessment, not
  Eric's: among the Chapter 3 entries produced so far, this framework reads as the single most
  direct, numerically specific answer this ingest has produced to that gap, and should be
  flagged to Eric as a candidate structure to react to, not an already-adopted practice.

**When NOT to apply** — **Population caveat, repeated deliberately because this is
the chapter's central prescriptive claim:** every piece of evidence behind this framework
(the 30-day residual table, the kayaking/pre-Olympic worked examples, the Popov case) comes from
individual, endurance-or-technical Olympic sports with a fixed, known international competition
calendar set months or years in advance. Football's bowl-selection process is itself uncertain
until late in the season (a team does not always know its exact bowl date and opponent far in
advance), which makes pre-planning an exact-length rebuild-then-taper sequence harder than in
Issurin's kayaking example, where the championship dates are fixed well ahead of the season.
**Inference:** none of Issurin's specific day-counts (20-25 / 20-25 / 10-16 days; the 5-6 week
vs. 7-9 week contrast) were derived from or tested against a team-contact-sport calendar; they
should be treated as a structural template to adapt to football's specific bowl-selection
timeline and roster fatigue state at season's end (which is itself compounded by an already-long
season, unlike Issurin's examples where the "prior mesocycle" is a dedicated, undisturbed
accumulation block rather than 12+ weeks of in-season competition). The mini-block's own
duration is not specified numerically in this section (Issurin calls it a "short microcycle,"
consistent with the 2-4 day aerobic contrast mini-block already given in
[[framework--transmutation-mesocycle-duration-and-overtraining-monitoring]]'s Table 3.10):
treat 2-4 days as the applicable figure by cross-reference, not as independently stated here.
Issurin gives his own explicit limit on this whole residual-clock logic, immediately before
this section: "if the training design is based solely on the length of training residuals, it
will be extremely rigid and inflexible. There are also other factors influencing training
residuals" (PDF p. 127, printed p. 116); the training stage itself he defines as three
consecutive mesocycle-blocks "vary[ing] from 4-10 weeks" (same locator). This entry's
day-count-driven Example below should be read against that caveat, not as a rigid formula.
Issurin's own later peer-reviewed review states that block-periodization evidence "does not
supply validated block durations or effect estimates for collegiate American football"
(Issurin, 2016, *Sports Medicine*, vault
`research-papers/issurin-2016-benefits-and-limitations-of-block-periodized-training-approaches-to-athletes-preparation-a.md`).

**Example** — **Inference:** for a **4-week gap** between a team's last game and its bowl game,
Issurin's own worked 5-week case (10-14 / 10-14 / 8-10 days) states plainly that "you need not
include mini-blocks because the training residuals following the accumulation mesocycle
surpass the competition" (PDF p. 131, printed p. 120); a 4-week gap is shorter still, so by
the same logic a plan could run roughly 10 / 10 / 8 days (accumulation / transmutation /
realization) with **no mini-block needed at all**, following Issurin's compressed-stage case
rather than requiring one. For a **6-week gap**, closer to but still short of Issurin's 7-9
week pre-Olympic case, a plan following this framework could run roughly 14-16 / 14-16 / 10-12
days **with** an aerobic mini-block inserted partway through the transmutation-equivalent
phase, since a stage approaching that length is closer to where Issurin says the accumulation
residual alone cannot cover the whole stage; note that Figure 3.12's own 20-25 / 20-25 / 10-16
day figures apply to the longer 7-9 week stage, not to a 6-week gap, so they should not be used
as the target durations here. Per the caveat above, treat both of these as a structural
template to react to, not a rigid formula: "there are also other factors influencing training
residuals" (PDF p. 127, printed p. 116).

# Competition Cut Popov's Aerobic-Mesocycle Gains Roughly in Half (V-AT) Within About Ten Days

**What it is** — Issurin addresses whether competitions inside a training stage (not the
final target competition) help or hurt (PDF pp. 128-129, printed pp. 117-118). His position:
mid- and low-level competitions inside accumulation or transmutation mesocycles add useful
emotional diversity and elicit training responses that enrich adaptation, but highly intense
competitive efforts also trigger a stress-hormone response. The supporting case: Alexander
Popov's (Russia, multi-Olympic/World swimming champion) preparation was tracked with an
incremental stepwise swimming test measuring anaerobic threshold speed, V-AT (blood lactate =
4 mM) and V-La8, the speed producing 8 mM lactate (the stated lower limit of highly intense
glycolytic work). Measurements were taken at the start of a highly concentrated aerobic
mesocycle, again 10 days later, and again a few days after Popov competed several times
(Figure 3.11, PDF p. 129, printed p. 118; re-verified against the page image, 2026-09-27,
adapted from Pyne & Touretski, 1993). Both velocity markers rose over the mesocycle's first
~10 days (V-AT 1.57 to 1.63 m/s; V-La8 1.64 to 1.72 m/s), then fell after the competition
(V-AT to 1.60 m/s at day ~21; V-La8 to 1.70 m/s), a decline the book itself calls "a
substantial decrease" (its own wording, not "erased" or "fatigued"). About half of the V-AT
gain and about a quarter of the V-La8 gain were lost; both markers remain above their starting
values. Issurin attributes this decline to shortened training residuals "induced by the
previous program" (PDF p. 128, printed p. 117), i.e. to the competition's effect on the
residual clock, not to any stated change in the training content itself.

**Why it works — the author's own attributed mechanism, not independently re-derived here** —
The book gives two separate chains, not one merged chain (PDF p. 128, printed p. 117): (a)
stress hormones (catecholamines, cortisol) reinforce the catabolic process (Viru & Viru, 2001),
so "muscle mass and maximum strength can decrease"; and (b), cited to a different source,
"the highly intense anaerobic glycolitic efforts typical in competition suppress the activity
of aerobic enzymes and the oxidative process in the mitochondria" (Volkov, 1986). The book's
conclusion 3 (PDF p. 129, printed p. 118) ties the shortened residual to stress hormones in
general; Volkov is specifically the citation behind the glycolytic-suppression chain (b), not
the hormonal chain (a). **Inference:** chain (b) is the same physiological substrate underlying
aerobic endurance's normal ~30-day residual
(see [[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]]), so a hard competition can
be read as an accelerant on that decay clock rather than a separate, independent event; the
book does not state this link explicitly. Issurin also notes the field's practical response:
"prominent coaches" have "added special aerobic workloads to restore and revitalize the
athletes after competition" (PDF p. 128, printed p. 117), a plain author statement, not this
entry's own inference.

**When to apply** — **Inference:** as a citable, named-athlete illustration for why a program including
in-season or in-training-stage competitive tests should expect some real cost to basic-ability
residuals, not treat competing as a free additional training stimulus. Directly supports the
caution already logged for the transmutation mesocycle
(see [[framework--transmutation-mesocycle-duration-and-overtraining-monitoring]]) about why
"including competition" is listed as an *option*, with tradeoffs, rather than an unqualified
recommendation. **Inference:** for football, this is a reason to expect that a physically
brutal, high-intensity early-season or mid-season game can measurably eat into whatever aerobic
or strength base was built in the preceding weeks, not just add acute fatigue that resolves in
a few days, which argues for the mini-block "topping up" logic in
[[framework--prolonging-residual-effects-with-mini-blocks]] being applied proactively after
unusually hard, high-intensity games, not only on a fixed calendar schedule.

**When NOT to apply** — This is a single elite swimmer's data, reported secondhand
(Pyne & Touretski, 1993, published via Issurin & Lustig, 2004), with no comparison athlete who
trained the same mesocycle without competing, and no statistical test of the pre/post-competition
change reported in this text: the chart (Figure 3.11) shows one athlete's trajectory, not a
group result. **Inference:** swimming's anaerobic-threshold/lactate-based test protocol has no
direct football analog; football has no simple single-modality repeated field test that isolates
"aerobic threshold" the way a stepwise swim test does, so this exact monitoring approach cannot
be copied, only the underlying caution (hard competition costs some residual) can be carried
over. This case does not address whether the effect is dose-dependent on competition intensity,
duration, or the athlete's fitness level entering the competition.

**Example** — **Inference:** a strength staff noting that a physically demanding, high-effort game (e.g., a
triple-overtime contest, or a game following an unusually taxing travel/short-week schedule)
likely cost the team some of the conditioning base built the prior weeks has a named precedent
(Popov's threshold-speed decline) for treating that as a real, not just anecdotal, training-load
event worth a deliberate light aerobic "top-up" in the following week, rather than assuming a
normal recovery day fully addresses it.

# The Pre-Competitive Microcycle: Cut Volume Hard, Raise Maximal-Speed and Sport-Specific Work, Keep Frequency

**What it is** — The pre-competitive microcycle forms the content of the realization
mesocycle (see [[framework--realization-mesocycle-taper-prescription]]), and it is the
realization mesocycle itself, not this microcycle, that the book calls "traditionally...
termed the taper" (PDF p. 122, printed p. 111) (PDF pp. 104-105, printed pp. 93-94; verified
against page images, re-checked 2026-09-27). It must satisfy three demands (PDF p. 104, printed p. 93): sport-specific
exercises/tasks that simulate the forthcoming competition and build mental readiness and
toughness; development of maximal speed/power and sport-specific quickness; and full (or
partial) restoration after the preceding transmutation mesocycle's fatiguing workloads. Table
3.5 (PDF p. 105, printed p. 94, verified against page image) gives seven prescriptive
characteristics: **workload volume**, substantially reduced, to create conditions for full
recovery; **volume of intense exercises specifically**, substantially reduced versus the
prior mesocycle, so total drill volume falls but quality rises; **contribution of maximum
speed/power exercises**, substantially **increased** (well-rested athletes respond better to
maximal-speed drills, and maximal-speed training residuals are the shortest of any ability, per
[[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]]); **contribution of
sport-specific tasks**, substantially increased (simulative tasks improve adaptation to
expected competitive stressors); **workout frequency**, usually kept **similar** to the
previous mesocycle (subdividing the same total work into more, smaller portions raises
quality per session even as volume falls); **organization**, a rational mix of group,
individual and mixed workouts, weighted somewhat more toward individual work than usual, so
athletes can attend to personal technical detail and coach rapport; **restoration**,
deliberately favorable conditions and an increased volume of restorative exercises/workouts,
since athletes have more free time and motivation to use it. Separately from the Table 3.5
list, Issurin states that in qualified athletes, "particularly during the pre-competitive
training camp," maintaining frequency in practice means **eight to ten workouts per week**
even as volume per workout drops (PDF p. 106, printed p. 95; text, not a Table 3.5 cell).
Workout timing should match the expected competition's daily biological-rhythm window
(example given: Olympic canoe-kayak/rowing regattas run only in the morning, so pre-Olympic
training is scheduled to place peak-effort sessions at that same morning time, PDF p. 107,
printed p. 96). Figure 3.7 (PDF p. 108, printed p. 97; re-verified against the page image,
2026-09-27): sport-specific-simulation key-workouts fall on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday
mornings (the expected competition time), alactic and technical work on the other mornings,
maximal or explosive strength on Monday and Thursday afternoons at medium load, and low-load
rest or alactic sessions on the other afternoons. The morning sport-specific simulations are
the point of the design, because the chart is built for morning peak performance (PDF p. 107,
printed p. 96); strength sits on two afternoons, not on a late-week ramp. On strength work
specifically, Issurin notes a real disagreement in the field: many athletes (particularly
swimmers, per Platonov & Fesenko, 1990, and also reported among volleyball and tennis players)
report high-resistance work pre-competition degrades fine technical skill, arguing for
reducing or excluding it; but sport-specific maximal/explosive strength work is also argued to
maintain the technical force component (Bompa & Carrera, 2003) and to prevent uncontrolled,
stress-hormone-driven muscle-mass loss pre-competition. The muscle-mass-protection point
specifically ("proper exercises for muscular hypertrophy prevent the uncontrolled reduction of
muscle mass induced by stress hormones") is given uncited in the text, separate from the
Bompa & Carrera citation for the technical-force-component point (PDF p. 108, printed p. 97).

**Why it works** — Reduced total load is stated as the "principal condition" for reaching
supercompensation before competition (PDF p. 106, printed p. 95); the two things this
recovered state is then spent on, maximal-speed drills and sport-specific simulation, are
each chosen because their quality specifically depends on a well-rested nervous system and
energy-resource availability (maximal speed) or on the athlete's capacity to fully attend to
technical/tactical detail under simulated competitive stress (sport-specific tasks). Issurin gives both sides on keeping frequency while cutting volume, not a single "quality
lever" argument: reduced frequency "can be considered as a way to decrease total workloads and
to find more time for restoration," but frequency is nonetheless usually kept because
"additional free time... can be a serious disadvantage" for athletes, alongside the more,
shorter, higher-quality-session logic (PDF p. 106, printed p. 95).

**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college football calendar** — **Bowl prep:** the
week(s) immediately before a bowl game map most directly onto this framework: cut total
practice/lift volume hard, hold practice frequency roughly where it has been, shift the
practice mix toward maximal-speed/explosive work and heavy game-plan (sport-specific)
simulation, and weight walkthroughs/individual work more than a normal week. **In-season
tuning week:** the standard in-season game week already functions as a compressed version of
this pattern (practice volume tapers toward the game, Thursday/Friday sessions shift toward
walkthrough-intensity techno-tactical work); see
[[framework--in-season-team-sport-mini-block-microcycle]] for the specific team-sport
adaptation Issurin gives later in this chapter. **Season opener after fall camp:** the final
camp week before the opener is a pre-competitive microcycle relative to the whole camp block:
volume should come down from camp peak even though frequency (practice count) may stay similar.
The strength-during-taper disagreement Issurin reports (reduce high-resistance work vs. keep
sport-specific strength to protect force and prevent hormone-driven muscle loss) is directly
relevant to the recurring question of whether to lift heavy in bowl-prep week; this text does
not resolve it and presents both positions as live in the field. **Eric's baseline** gives no
session-frequency rule for a taper anywhere in `method-baseline.md`; the closest material is
the general in-season rule "maintain intensity, reduce volume" (`method-baseline.md`, "Strength
and power," citing `01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md` pp.20-21), which agrees directionally
(reduce volume, don't reduce intensity/speed work) but does not specify a frequency-holding
rule, an eight-to-ten-sessions-per-week figure, or an explicit sport-specific-task ramp; this
entry supplies that missing numeric structure, not something already adopted.

**When NOT to apply** — Every numeric particular in Table 3.5 (the
"substantially reduced/increased" volume and speed contributions) and the separately-stated
8-10 workouts/week figure are Issurin's own applied synthesis with no cited dose-response
study behind the specific numbers; he acknowledges directly that "many contradictions exist
regarding workout duration and frequency of high intensity exercises" in the
pre-competitive/taper literature generally (PDF p. 105, printed p. 94, citing Kubukeli et al.,
2002). **Inference, population caveat:** the worked example
(Figure 3.7) and both named case examples (Olympic canoe-kayak/rowing morning scheduling;
swimmer/volleyball/tennis strength-reduction reports) are individual, dual, or non-contact
sports; none address a 100+ player roster with heterogeneous individual readiness, a fixed
practice-week structure set partly by NCAA rules, or the additional load of team walkthroughs
and special-teams reps that a football pre-competitive week must still include. Issurin does
address team sports elsewhere in this same organization discussion: "in team sports...
group workouts are dominant" (PDF p. 106, printed p. 95), which this entry's individual/dual
framing does not capture. **Inference:** the "8-10 workouts per week" frequency figure, stated
for "qualified athletes, particularly during the pre-competitive training camp," assumes an
individual/dual-sport training camp structure and should not be read as a literal
session-count target for a football bowl-prep week. Issurin does not address game-week
travel, opponent scouting load, or academic-calendar constraints anywhere in this framework.

**Example** — **Inference:** a bowl-prep week could hold practice count similar to a normal in-season week
while cutting total practice/lift volume substantially, shifting a larger share of practice
time to explosive/speed work and full-speed game-plan simulation than a mid-season week would
carry, and deliberately expanding individual position-group time (film, technique correction)
relative to full-team periods, while treating the "how much to lift" question as an open
disagreement the source itself does not resolve, not a settled rule.
