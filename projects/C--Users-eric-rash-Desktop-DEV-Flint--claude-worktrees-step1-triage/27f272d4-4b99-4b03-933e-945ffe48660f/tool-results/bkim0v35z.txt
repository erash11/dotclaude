=== claim--eras-constraint-is-vetting-not-volume.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's historical framing, opening the course: sport science and strength-and-conditioning practice grew from Milo of Croton's progressive overload through research institutes, periodization theory, biomechanics, and increasing specialization, then accelerated further with GPS/inertial tracking, force measurement, online coaching content, academic reviews, and social media (Webinar 1 Part I summary, 00:00–09:06). His claim: for practitioners entering the field now, the binding constraint is vetting, filtering, and integrating information, not finding more of it, in contrast to his own earlier reliance on academic sources and experienced coaches (09:06–11:41). He extends this to AI specifically: useful for preliminary research and helping a skilled coder work faster, but short AI summaries can strip away nuance, and practitioners who lack the underlying skill may overtrust AI-generated code, training plans, or rehabilitation plans (same range).
18:**Why it works** — This is Weber's own historical narrative and personal contrast between his early career and now, not a documented survey of practitioner information behavior; the mechanism (more channels producing more unfiltered volume) is plausible but not measured here.
20:**When to apply** — As a framing device when deciding how to train newer staff or interns: the emphasis shifts from finding more sources toward building a filter, knowing which claims to trust, which social-media clips to discount, and which AI outputs need independent verification before use. **Eric's baseline:** Eric's own course material states a skepticism of "viral clip" training trends and cautions that a clip is "just a brief snapshot" stripped of context [method-baseline.md, Philosophy and decision principles]. **Inference:** the two claims are consistent with each other even though they come from different sources.
22:**When NOT to apply** — Treat this as Weber's opinion and personal history, not as an evidenced claim about how practitioners as a group actually behave; the transcript is machine-generated and historical names/dates in this section should be checked against the audio before being repeated as fact (course README caveat). It should not be cited as a finding that AI tools are unreliable in general, only as Weber's specific caution about unverified AI-generated training or rehab content in the hands of someone without the underlying skill to check it.
24:**Example** — Weber's own closing framing for Part I: growth in technology and information has changed how performance departments operate, which he uses to set up the course's later modules on staff capability and decision structure (Webinar 1 Part I summary, 11:41–12:19).

=== framework--data-information-knowledge-wisdom-hierarchy.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber uses the standard data-information-knowledge-wisdom (DIKW) hierarchy to argue that storing measurements and building dashboards (data, information) does not, by itself, help a department make a decision (knowledge, wisdom); each step up the hierarchy requires additional interpretive work, not just more collection (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 03:27–04:42).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's stated mechanism connects directly to communication capacity: a coach or medical colleague handed a complex dashboard is left to do the interpretation themselves, which the lesson summary describes with a pick-up-52 analogy — see [[heuristic--lead-with-the-recipients-question-not-the-dashboard]] — the recipient is handed a scattered deck and asked to reassemble it into an answer (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 04:42–07:44).
20:**When to apply** — Reviewing any recurring report or dashboard against the question of whether it hands the reader raw data or hands them an answer they can act on. **Eric's baseline:** the Playbook's Development Plan already names "standardized weekly/monthly coach-facing reporting templates" as unfinished Phase 2 work [performance-playbook-development-plan.md Phase 2]. **Inference:** this hierarchy is a way to test a draft template before it ships (does it stop at data, or does it reach a stated recommendation).
22:**When NOT to apply** — The DIKW hierarchy is a widely used general communication model, not something original to Weber or specific to sport performance; he offers no sport-science-specific validation of it, only an application. It also does not specify how to move from one level to the next for a particular metric, only that the movement is necessary.
24:**Example** — Weber's hamstring caution, per the lesson summary: treating one favorable test result as a complete return-to-running answer is a caution against stopping short of "knowledge" (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 07:44–08:54). **Inference:** the same test result read on its own is data or information; it becomes knowledge only once checked against other convergent evidence. See [[framework--evidence-stacking-before-acting]] for the fuller version of this same caution.

=== framework--decision-log-owner-action-date-review.md ===
16:**What it is** — A minimal written record Weber attaches to the end of every decision-oriented meeting format in the course: the action taken, who owns it, the due date, and the review method and date (Kaigi-transcript summary, 04:48–05:56). The same four fields recur, worded slightly differently, in every later meeting format, per the lesson summaries: the meeting-typology **decide** mode requires an explicit decision, owner, due date, and review (Lab 3B summary, 01:03–02:14); stoplight storytelling ends with one owner, one action, a due date, and a review point recorded in the decision log (Lab 3C summary, 03:02–03:45); and the 20-minutes-of-discipline format ends with the decision, actions, accountable people, deadlines, and a requested update (Podcast 3 summary, 08:34–09:39).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's stated rationale, per the lesson summary, is that the administrative effort of writing the four fields down creates accountability and reduces later disagreement about what was agreed (Kaigi-transcript summary, 04:48–05:56). **Inference:** without a record, a verbal decision is likely subject to selective memory once circumstances change or the outcome is unwelcome; the log exists specifically to prevent that after-the-fact renegotiation.
20:**When to apply** — The closing step of any decision meeting, regardless of which format (kaigi, stoplight, a 20-minute recurring meeting, or an informal hallway decision that is being made official). **Eric's baseline:** no specific RTP criteria, battery, or phased-return protocol appears anywhere in the reviewed corpus, even though KPI-threshold identification is named as a departmental operating principle [method-baseline.md, Rehab and return to play]. **Inference:** a decision log does not supply the missing criteria, but it does supply the accountability structure once a criterion-based decision is made.
22:**When NOT to apply** — This is a record-keeping minimum, not a full decision framework by itself; it assumes a decision has already been reached through some other structure (a kaigi meeting, a decision gate) and simply captures it. It also does not specify where the log should live or how it should be reviewed at scale — Weber's course gives the four fields, not a system for storing or auditing them over a season.
24:**Example** — Weber's stoplight-storytelling version adds one more requirement to the same four fields, per the lesson summary: every status, including amber, needs a next action or review date (Lab 3C summary, 05:51–06:23). **Inference:** this closes the loophole of an ambiguous "wait and see" outcome with no owner attached.

=== framework--encoding-problem-shared-case-framework.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's observation that different professional groups reviewing the same athlete and the same data routinely see different problems, not because they disagree about the facts, but because those facts are "encoded" differently by discipline: a physiotherapist sees tissue tolerance, healing, and rehabilitation progress; a medical practitioner sees health status and risk; a strength and conditioning coach sees readiness, output, and return-to-performance demands; a sports scientist sees load, exposure, and the numerical pathway to competition; a coach sees availability (can the athlete train, can the athlete play) (Module_6B_Part_A PDF p.1). His proposed fix is a written, shared case framework: document how a given scenario (his example: a hamstring injury) will be assessed, the checkpoints, expected timing, and who acts at each handoff, built collaboratively with medical, physiotherapy, strength, and sport science staff so each group also learns what the others do (Video Lab 6B summary, 02:44–06:37; PDF p.1).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's mechanism: when staff understand what other disciplines are doing, "departments become less defensive, less territorial, and more collaborative... staff are more willing to share information, raise concerns early, and adjust without fear or confusion" (PDF p.1). He is explicit this is not about eliminating disciplinary difference — healthy technical disagreement should be "respected and examined" — but about ensuring differences operate inside a shared structure rather than each discipline silently running its own model (PDF p.2).
20:**When to apply** — Any interdisciplinary case (injury, load management, return-to-play) where different departments have historically produced different read-outs of the same athlete without a documented shared plan. **Eric's baseline:** Applied Performance's stated mission is to enable the high-performance team to answer questions, ask better questions, and discover new ones, operating inside Baylor Athletics Health & Wellness alongside, not above or below, Athletics Performance, Athletic Medicine, Performance Nutrition, and Mental Health Services [method-baseline.md, Department model]. **Inference:** this framework is a concrete method (a written pathway, checkpoints, and criteria per case type) for making that enabling role operational rather than aspirational.
22:**When NOT to apply** — Weber distinguishes healthy technical challenge from a staff member working outside the agreed process; the framework is meant to protect the former and surface the latter, not to suppress disagreement (PDF p.2). His own anecdote — presenting injury-risk evidence that was new and unfamiliar to a coach and doctor who lacked a shared framework for understanding it, followed by the predicted injuries occurring — is a personal account illustrating a communication failure, not evidence that the specific injury-risk claim itself was correct (PDF p.2).
24:**Example** — Weber's own summary of the stakes: "correct data is not enough. If the leader cannot get the information understood across disciplines, the work has failed at the level that matters most" (PDF p.2).
26:**Related** — [[framework--integration-problem-five-break-points]] — the upstream framework this problem sits inside (specifically the language gap). [[heuristic--replace-translation-with-early-education]] — the direct follow-on once an encoding gap has been identified. [[framework--grey-zone-overlap-ownership]] — the applied version of this same shared-framework idea for medical/performance overlap.

=== framework--grey-zone-overlap-ownership.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's term for the overlap between medical/physiotherapy staff and strength/performance staff, where differences in training, priorities, timelines, and risk tolerance become a problem specifically when the overlap has no agreed operating rules (Video Lab 6E summary, 00:00–02:44). His proposed governance: agree the phases, decision points, measures, and criteria the department will use; assign a named operational owner for each phase, define what that person can change independently, and state which decisions require escalation and who must be informed, and how quickly, when a plan changes (02:44–10:43). One example division he describes: medical leadership early in rehabilitation, strength staff taking operational responsibility for return-to-running, with both continuing to contribute — but he is explicit this division should shift when the actual skills and collaboration willingness of the specific practitioners warrant it, since a job title alone, per the lesson summary, does not establish who is best equipped for a specific task (03:59–07:11).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's own stated aim is to make responsibilities and decisions explicit while preserving each discipline's perspective (Video Lab 6E summary, 00:00–02:44). **Inference:** without a named owner per phase, both disciplines can reasonably believe the other is responsible, or both can independently act on the same decision point with different criteria, the same underlying logic as the strategy/tactics model (see [[framework--strategy-as-highway-tactics-as-lanes]]). His team exercise makes the mismatch tangible: walk through one rehabilitation case twice, once from the physiotherapist's priorities and constraints and once from the strength coach's, then compare the two accounts at each handoff and agree ownership, progression criteria, information to transfer, change authority, and notification rules (10:43–13:02).
20:**When to apply** — Any return-to-play pathway where Athletic Medicine and Applied Performance/Athletics Performance both have a legitimate stake in the same phase. **Eric's baseline:** Applied Performance's role in return-to-performance is described as advisory, supplying information that supports Athletics Performance's and Athletic Medicine's decisions, and no specific RTP criteria, battery, or phased-return protocol appears anywhere in the reviewed corpus [method-baseline.md, Rehab and return to play]. **Inference:** this framework's phase-by-phase ownership map is a direct, concrete answer to that named gap.
22:**When NOT to apply** — Weber's phase-division example (medical early, strength for return-to-running) is his own illustration, not a validated or universal division of labor; he states explicitly it should change based on who the actual practitioners are and how they work together, not be applied as a fixed template. This framework also does not resolve clinical criteria for a specific injury type — it governs who owns which decision, not what the decision's medical threshold should be.
24:**Example** — Weber's escalation rule: name an operational owner for each phase, state what that person can change independently, and specify which decisions must be escalated, plus who must be informed and how quickly when the athlete's plan changes (07:11–10:43).

=== framework--kaigi-decision-meeting-structure.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's structured meeting format for use when the question and realistic options are already clear and the team needs to decide, not explore. Elements: prepare before the meeting by defining the question and narrowing it to two or three realistic options, with relevant information available in advance so participants arrive informed; keep evidence precise, stating what was measured, what is known, and what remains unknown; time-box contributions so short speaking limits push people to the critical information, with supporting detail left in the pre-read; and leave a decision log recording the action, owner, due date, and review method (Kaigi-transcript summary, 00:00–05:56; see [[framework--decision-log-owner-action-date-review]]). Brainstorming, complex technical work, and staff development are explicitly carved out as needing a different, slower pace (05:56–07:27).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's own stated priority for this format is speed, clarity, and accountability specifically when the decision space is already narrow (Kaigi-transcript summary, 00:00–02:18). **Inference:** forcing preparation and concise contributions likely works because the hard thinking (narrowing the options, gathering the evidence) has already happened before the room convenes, so meeting time is spent deciding, not discovering.
20:**When to apply** — Recurring operational decisions with a known, bounded option set: Weber's own worked example is an AFL pre-training Go/No-Go review, where a leader names each player, responsible practitioners give concise observations, other staff add relevant information without repeated prompting, and the group combines evidence rather than changing training on one measure alone — aiming for under 30 seconds per player across roughly 50 players, with flagged players receiving assigned follow-up reported back within 20 minutes (Kaigi-transcript summary, 07:28–12:13). Weber also shows the format can be dropped into a stalled meeting mid-stream: switch explicitly into a 10-minute decision phase, state the decision, establish ownership, document the outcome, and move on (12:14–13:23).
22:**When NOT to apply** — Weber is explicit that a decision meeting has different requirements from sharing information, creating solutions, or educating staff (00:00–02:18); **Inference:** using kaigi structure to force a decision before the options are actually narrowed to two or three, or before enough evidence exists, would likely produce a fast but poorly grounded call. His own Go/No-Go numbers (roughly 15–17 staff, under 30 seconds per player, 20-minute follow-up) are his example's parameters, not validated operating thresholds or a recommended clinical protocol. See [[framework--meeting-typology-decide-diagnose-inform-build]] for the broader set of meeting modes; kaigi is specifically the **decide** mode and should not be reached for when the actual need is **diagnose** or **build**.
24:**Example** — Weber's stalled-meeting rescue: when a broader problem-solving discussion stalls, explicitly announce the switch to decision mode, give it a fixed short window (his example: 10 minutes), and require a stated decision, an owner, and a documented outcome before the group moves on (12:14–13:23).

=== heuristic--fix-protocol-before-trusting-a-noisy-metric.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's three-option response when a metric shows high inherent variability, illustrated with rate of force development (RFD): "the takeaway is not 'never use RFD.' The takeaway is: when a metric is noisy, your thresholds must be wider, your protocol must be tighter, or you choose a more stable metric for decision gates" (Lab_2E_The_Critcal_Math_that_can_t_be_Ignored.pdf p.2). He attributes RFD's typical noise to three sources: execution-consistency demands, onset-detection choices, and small timing differences that produce large swings in the calculated value (p.2).
18:**Why it works** — **Inference:** this gives a leader three genuinely different levers instead of one binary (use it or drop it): widening the decision threshold accepts the noise and raises the bar for calling a change real; tightening the protocol (standardizing onset detection, warm-up, instructions) reduces the noise itself; switching metrics sidesteps the problem by choosing a more stable measure of a similar underlying quality. Which lever is right likely depends on whether the noise comes from the measurement process (fixable by protocol) or is closer to genuine biological/mechanical variability (better handled by a wider threshold or a different metric). Weber himself does not spell out this mechanism, only the three-option response (p.2).
20:**When to apply** — Any time a metric is flagged as unreliable during the reliability-vs-sensitivity audit (see [[framework--reliability-vs-sensitivity-mdc-and-swc]]), rather than either dropping the metric outright or continuing to use it with an unexamined threshold. **Eric's baseline:** the department's adopted Hawkin Dynamics ODSF metric-selection framework already says to add "fluffy" force-plate metrics (peak power, average power, RFD) only with "a specific, defensible rationale" [method-baseline.md, Force plate and neuromuscular monitoring]. **Inference:** this heuristic is the concrete follow-up once a metric like RFD is found to be noisy in-house.
22:**When NOT to apply** — Weber does not say how to choose between the three levers for a given case, only that one of them is the right response to noise; this is a decision menu, not a decision rule. It also does not apply to a metric whose problem is not noise but validity (measuring the wrong construct entirely) — that is a different failure mode, addressed by [[framework--measurement-versus-inference]].
24:**Example** — Weber's stated leadership framing for this whole statistical package: "if staff are using tests with unknown reliability, then when they bring you a number, there is no foundation for trust. Statistical rigour gives you credibility, consistent reporting, defensible thresholds, decision gates that work" (p.2).

=== heuristic--lead-with-the-recipients-question-not-the-dashboard.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's pick-up-52 analogy, per the lesson summary: handing a coach or medical colleague a complex, unfiltered dashboard is like scattering a deck of 52 cards on the floor and asking them to pick it up and make sense of it themselves (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 04:42–07:44). His alternative: the communicator should begin with the recipient's actual question and decide which information is relevant to that question before presenting anything, specifying the equipment, standards, and reporting format up front (07:44–08:54).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's own stated point, per the lesson summary, is that a dashboard left for the coach or medical colleague to interpret places that burden on the wrong person (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 04:42–07:44). **Inference:** the recipient is likely the person least equipped to reconcile multiple measures against each other, and least likely to know which differences are within normal error; framing the report around a stated question forces the practitioner, who has that context, to do the reconciliation once instead of the recipient doing it every time.
20:**When to apply** — Designing any recurring coach- or medical-facing report or dashboard. **Eric's baseline:** Baylor's own gap analysis already flags standardized coach-facing reporting templates as unbuilt [performance-playbook-development-plan.md Phase 2]. Also useful when a new coach or staff member joins and needs to be told what they will receive and why, matching the same communication checklist Lab 2C recommends for monitoring and testing (see [[framework--monitoring-versus-testing]]).
22:**When NOT to apply** — This is a design heuristic for the recipient-facing layer of reporting; it does not argue against keeping a full, granular dataset available to the practitioners who need it. Weber gives no template for how to identify the recipient's actual question when it has not been stated, only that it should be established before building the report.
24:**Example** — Weber's own SpeedSig product classification, described as reducing many measures into a simpler signal while keeping detail available for those who need it, is his illustration of the practice, offered as a product example, not independent evidence that the underlying classification is clinically or competitively valid (Webinar 1 Part III summary, 08:54–10:39).

=== heuristic--replace-translation-with-early-education.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's argument that repeatedly explaining the same concept to different stakeholders (medical, physiotherapy, strength and conditioning, sports science, coaching) is not just a communication cost but a symptom that the environment has not been educated to a common operating understanding (Module_6B_Part_B PDF p.1). He calls this a genuine "cognitive cost" and "performance leak inside the organisation," since it drains time and attention that could go to higher-level problems (p.1). His prescribed response: avoid translation where possible and replace it with education, treating a recurring need to re-explain the same idea as useful diagnostic information rather than an inconvenience to absorb (p.1). He offers two implementation routes depending on context: drawing a firm, clearly stated line in how the system will operate (faster, more likely to create friction, appropriate when confusion is already embedded), or building alignment through workshops, internal CPD, and structured educational sessions (slower, better suited where there is more time or openness) (p.1).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's mechanism ties this directly to the discipline-equals-freedom chain (see [[principle--discipline-equals-freedom]]): "the better educated the staff are... the less translation is needed... that frees up time for decision-making, problem-solving, and adaptation in real time" (p.1). He also introduces a leadership-honesty distinction: **technical leadership** (a leader with enough depth to personally teach the framework) versus **structural leadership** (a leader who instead ensures the right technical people are in place to do it); either is legitimate, but "technical coherence cannot be left to chance. Someone must own it" (p.2).
20:**When to apply** — Whenever the same concept has to be re-explained to different stakeholders more than once or twice in a season, treat that repetition itself as the signal to intervene, not just the individual instance to answer. **Eric's baseline:** the proposed intern curriculum deliberately adds sessions on interdisciplinary collaboration with Athletic Medicine, Performance Nutrition, and Mental Health Services, reflecting Baylor's specific department structure [performance-playbook-development-plan.md 5.3]. **Inference:** this heuristic argues those sessions exist specifically to eliminate a recurring translation cost, not just to build general awareness.
22:**When NOT to apply** — Weber is explicit that some explanation will always be necessary, particularly concise executive-level updates for coaches; the goal is reducing unnecessary translation, not eliminating all of it (p.1). This heuristic also assumes the leader has honestly audited whether they personally have the technical depth to lead the education, or need to bring in someone who does (p.2); skipping that self-audit risks a leader attempting technical leadership they cannot actually deliver.
24:**Example** — Weber's pelvis/clam case: a rehabilitation issue had been framed narrowly as a strength problem (poor frontal-plane pelvis control treated with isolated abduction work), until technically grounded leadership reframed it as a motor-control problem, demonstrating the athletes were already strong enough in isolation and that the real issue was stabilizing the pelvis during stance-phase center-of-mass transfer — an example of drawing a line in the sand with explanation and evidence rather than authority alone (Module_6B_Part_B PDF pp.2–3). This is Weber's own account of a single case and does not establish a general diagnosis or treatment rule for similar presentations.
26:**Related** — [[principle--discipline-equals-freedom]] — the underlying chain this heuristic extends into interdisciplinary communication. [[framework--encoding-problem-shared-case-framework]] — the upstream problem this heuristic responds to.

=== heuristic--time-box-recurring-meetings.md ===
16:**What it is** — Weber's "20 minutes of discipline" structure for recurring operational decision meetings: send information roughly 24 hours in advance so attendees can read and consider it, state the required outcome at the start, keep discussion to what is working, what is not, and what needs to change, name who decides when a decision is needed, close with the decision, actions, accountable people, deadlines, and a requested update, start and stop on time (a visible timer where useful), and route useful off-topic items to a "parking lot" with an owner and next step (Podcast 3 summary, 07:20–11:11). "20 minutes" is his target for suitable routine meetings, not a limit he applies to every discussion (00:00–01:28). His challenge to the listener is to tighten just **one** recurring meeting, then ask whether anything valuable was lost and whether its outputs improved (12:16–16:44).
18:**Why it works** — Weber's stated rationale is capacity protection: time saved in a tightened recurring meeting is time preserved for analysis, project work, and the training/treatment schedule itself (00:00–01:28, 02:58–04:24). Pre-reading and a stated required outcome shift the meeting's actual work (absorbing context, forming an initial view) to before the room convenes, so the meeting itself is spent on discussion and decision rather than briefing.
20:**When to apply** — Any standing weekly or daily operational meeting that has grown past its original purpose (a status update that has become a discussion, a discussion that has become a debate). Weber's own examples of what a tightened format looks like, per the lesson summary: a planning meeting using short timed rounds to collect responses to three questions from five people (about 40 minutes, which he says prepared months of work), and short daily stand-ups surfacing completed work, next work, and blockers (01:28–02:58, 11:11–11:26).
22:**When NOT to apply** — Weber explicitly distinguishes this format from brainstorming and relationship-building, and recommends preparation, smaller pieces, and small multidisciplinary groups for genuinely complex problems rather than forcing them into a tight recurring slot (12:16–15:31); see [[framework--meeting-typology-decide-diagnose-inform-build]] for when "decide" mode is the wrong fit. His efficiency claim about the 40-minute planning meeting is his own account, not measured against an alternative.
24:**Example** — Weber's own operational rule for the parking lot: an off-topic issue that is nonetheless useful gets a named owner and a next step, so it is captured without derailing the meeting's actual agenda (10:08–11:11).
