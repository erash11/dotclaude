# Case Study: Siff's First-Hand Account of Serge Reding's Electrostimulation Protocol

**What it is** — This passage is Siff writing in the first person about his own direct
training contact with Serge Reding, a Belgian superheavyweight weightlifter, "who had close
training contact with one of the authors (Siff) while he stayed with him in South Africa in
the early 1970s" (citing Siff, 1973). Reding's post-session electrostimulation (ES) protocol
differed from Kots's (see
[[claim--kots-electrostimulation-strength-claims-not-replicated]]): 0.5 to 2 second, very
high intensity contractions with 10 to 15 second rest periods, for a total of 5 minutes per
major muscle group, with agonists and antagonists of each limb alternately stimulated in
ipsilateral and contralateral sequences. Reding always concluded sessions with a few
repetitions (1-3) of a long-duration (10-15 second), fairly intense tetanic contraction
used as a form of deep massage, followed by about 10-15 minutes of microcurrent stimulation
(less than 200 microamps) applied across the head and spinal cord with a separate
electrostimulator. Elsewhere in the section, Siff again writes in the first person: "the ES
training applied to the author by Serge Reding used a Myogen Parametric stimulator that was
designed to offer external stimulation which in no way simulated the most frequently
occurring movements of Olympic lifting," used as a form of General Physical Preparation
(GPP) rather than sport-specific stimulation; Reding stressed the importance of different
waveforms and regimes for different sport categories, "adding that the method shared by
Kots with Canadian coaches was only one of many possibilities." (PDF p. 220, printed p.
209; PDF p. 226, printed p. 215, "4.2.1.1" and "4.2.1.6 The Use of Electrostimulation in
Training.")

**Why it works** — No mechanism is given for why this specific dosing pattern (brief,
high-intensity contractions; alternating ipsilateral/contralateral agonist-antagonist
sequencing; a closing deep-massage-style tetanic contraction; low-current cranial-spinal
microstimulation) would produce a training effect; the passage is a descriptive account of
practice, not an experiment.

**When to apply** — Inference: use this as a documented example that elite-level ES
protocols in this era varied substantially even among people working with the same broad
technology (faradic/interferential ES devices); it is not evidence that Reding's specific
protocol outperformed Kots's, only that it existed and was applied to a top-level athlete
under Siff's direct observation.

**When NOT to apply** — Untested: this is a single first-person account (n = 1, Siff
observing/administering) with no reported outcome data (no before/after strength numbers),
no control condition, and no independent replication in the text. Do not treat the specific
timings (0.5-2s contractions, 10-15s rest, 5 min per muscle group, 10-15 min microcurrent
under 200 microamps) as a validated prescription; they are a description of what one
coach-athlete pair did.

**Example** — The book's own example is this case itself: Reding's own protocol as directly
witnessed and partly administered by Siff. No further invented example is added here.

contemporaneous Soviet approach Siff also directly observed).

# Kots's 20%+ Electrostimulation Strength Claims Drew Western Interest but Were Not Replicated

**What it is** — American interest in electrostimulation (ES) as a training adjunct was
aroused in 1971, when Kots in Russia reported increases of more than 20% in muscle
strength, speed and power produced by several weeks of "electrotraining." Unable to
produce comparable results, Canadian researchers invited Kots to lecture at Concordia
University in 1977; armed with the detail that Kots used a sinusoidally modulated 2500 Hz
current source applied in a sequence of 10 seconds of contraction followed by 50 seconds of
relaxation, they again tried to duplicate the Russian claims. The authors report that "researchers
continue to disagree on increase in muscle strength" despite this, and separately note that
various track coaches reported success integrating conventional faradic ES into a
carefully periodised training programme (Francis, 1982). (PDF pp. 219-220, printed pp.
208-209, "4.2.1.1 The Physiological Effects of Electrostimulation.")

**Why it works** — The authors give no physiological mechanism for why Kots's specific
protocol (2500 Hz carrier, 10s on/50s off) would or would not replicate; the chapter's
later "Reasons for Conflicting Research" subsection (see
[[heuristic--nine-reasons-electrostimulation-research-conflicts]]) gives the general
methodological reasons ES research disagrees, without resolving the Kots case specifically.

**When to apply** — Inference: treat any single large ES strength-gain percentage (here,
"more than 20%") as a claim tied to one specific, fully-specified protocol (waveform,
frequency, duty cycle) from one lab, not a generic property of "electrostimulation" as a
category; the Canadian replication attempt failed using a different protocol until they
obtained Kots's exact parameters, and even then the authors do not report the Canadian
outcome as a confirmed success.

**When NOT to apply** — This is a single historical claim and a single replication attempt,
both from the 1970s, without independent modern confirmation in this text. Do not cite "20%
plus" as an expected or typical ES strength gain; the chapter itself frames Kots's number as
contested rather than settled, and the following subsection lists nine separate
methodological reasons why ES strength research across studies disagrees.

**Example** — Inference: a strength coach considering NMES for a rehabilitating athlete
should ask what specific waveform, frequency and duty cycle any cited study used, since this
entry shows that even a direct attempt to replicate one well-specified protocol required
obtaining that protocol's exact parameters from its originator before a comparable attempt
could be made.

differently-designed elite-athlete ES protocol the authors set against the Kots/Canadian

# Nine Reasons Electrostimulation Strength Research Disagrees: A Checklist for Reading It

**What it is** — The authors give nine specific methodological reasons why published
electrostimulation (ES) strength-training studies contradict one another: (1) many studies
measure only isometric or isokinetic strength change and neglect power, speed, muscular
endurance, residual muscle tension, muscle growth and rate of neuromuscular recovery; (2)
much research uses injured or rehabilitating patients, untrained individuals, or trained
subjects of differing backgrounds, and strength gains are proportionately larger early in
training or in untrained subjects, with early gains driven mainly by neuromuscular
adaptation and learning rather than hypertrophy; (3) ES effectiveness depends on current
intensity and individual tolerance of discomfort, and no waveform has been found to be
universally most comfortable, with considerable inter-subject variation (Delitto & Rose,
1986; Mannheimer & Carlsson, 1979; Moreno-Aranda & Seirig, 1981); (4) effects depend on
waveform type and frequency, since slow-twitch and fast-twitch fibres have distinct
recruitment patterns and functional characteristics (Lloyd et al, 1986; Mannheimer &
Carlsson, 1979; Pette & Vrbova, 1985), and higher frequency (especially with a carrier
wave) lowers electrode-skin impedance and raises comfort; (5) electrode type, size, number,
location and gel affect delivered energy, resultant contraction force and comfort, and many
studies omit these details, "so valid comparisons largely are meaningless"; (6) most
studies use commercial stimulators that vary only a few of the many possible stimulation
parameters (frequency, waveform, pulse-width, modulation type, impulse interval, energy
delivered), so no single study has examined the full parameter space, even in the more
extensive review by Lloyd et al (1986); (7) ES effects, like plyometric training, may be
enhanced or diminished by how well it is integrated into an overall strength and
sport-specific programme (e.g. Francis, 1982); (8) many training regimes are deficiently
designed, setting ES intensity at a fixed value (or a fixed percentage of maximal voluntary
isometric/isokinetic contraction) that does not progress as the subject adapts, the same
flaw that limits fixed-load weight training; (9) the design and effects of a stimulator
depend on which physiological model of cellular communication it assumes (the
action-potential model versus subthreshold/bioelectric models), a design choice most
commercial stimulators do not make explicit (see
[[claim--macs-mics-integrated-theory-of-electrostimulation]]). (PDF pp. 220-221, printed pp.
209-210, "4.2.1.2 Reasons for Conflicting Research.")

**Why it works** — The authors' own framing: these are methodological confounds (measurement
scope, subject population, dosing, hardware specification, and underlying physiological
model), not evidence for or against ES itself; a study that controls for none of these nine
factors cannot be meaningfully compared with one that controls for a different subset.

**When to apply** — Use this as a checklist before citing any single ES strength-training
study: what outcome was measured (item 1), who were the subjects (item 2), was the current
intensity and waveform reported and justified (items 3-4, 6), were electrode details given
(item 5), was the training programme context described (item 7), and did the dosing
progress with adaptation (item 8)? A study missing several of these items should be treated
as weak evidence regardless of its stated conclusion.

**When NOT to apply** — Inference: this checklist evaluates methodological rigor, not
whether ES "works"; a study can satisfy all nine points and still report a negative or null
result, and the authors elsewhere report both positive and negative ES findings that passed
some of these criteria (see
[[framework--clinical-physiological-effects-of-electrostimulation]]). Do not use this list
to dismiss all ES research wholesale; use it to weight individual studies.

**Example** — Inference: a marketing claim citing "a study showing 20% strength gains from
electrostimulation" should be checked against this list: was the outcome purely isometric
(item 1)? Were the subjects untrained or rehabilitating (item 2)? Was the current intensity
fixed for the whole programme (item 8)? Failing several of these checks should lower
confidence in the claim regardless of the headline number.

[[framework--clinical-physiological-effects-of-electrostimulation]].

# Seventeen Claimed Clinical and Physiological Effects of Electrostimulation, With Their Original Citations

**What it is** — The authors catalog electrostimulation's (ES) reported applications and
effects, each with cited sources: (1) increase in muscle strength, strongest in
incapacitated, unfit or weakest subjects, with ES alone at best matching or trailing
voluntary exercise, though periodised programmes combining ES with training sometimes
increased competitive athletes' strength (Francis, 1982; Kots & Chwilon, 1971; Matteson,
1984; Reding, 1973); (2) re-education of faulty muscle-action patterns, e.g. chondromalacia
patellae, tennis elbow, "frozen shoulder" (Eriksson, 1981; Godfrey et al, 1979; Johnson et
al, 1977; Nirschl & Sobel, 1981); (3) facilitation of contraction when voluntary contraction
is blocked by disuse, pain or injury, reducing tissue wasting; (4) increased muscular and
systemic endurance from prolonged sub-maximal ES, with low-frequency (under 15 Hz),
24-hour continuous stimulation shown in animal studies to shift fast-twitch fibres toward a
more aerobic, fatigue-resistant profile (Jolesz & Sreter, 1981; Pette, 1984; Pette & Vrbova,
1985; Salmons & Vrbova, 1969; Salmons & Henriksson, 1981); (5) increased speed of
contraction from long-term, higher-frequency (over 40 Hz) ES, improving reaction time and
decreasing time-to-peak-tension of fast-twitch fibres (Fleury & Lagasse, 1979; Salmons &
Vrbova, 1967, 1969); (6) increased local blood supply, with measured increases from about
20% to over 200%, greatest below roughly 30 Hz (Currier et al, 1986; Richardson, 1981;
Wakim et al, 1948; Wakim, 1953); (7) a massage-like pumping action on venous/lymphatic
return; (8) pain relief via TENS, using low-frequency (50-100 Hz) stimulation without
contraction for superficial pain, or short, high-intensity bursts (1-150 Hz) with
contraction for deeper pain; (9) reduction of protective muscle spasm and spasticity; (10)
promotion of relaxation and recuperation, attributed to central endorphin/enkephalin
release, used by Russian athletes for post-training recuperation and to aid sleep onset even
with mild thigh-only stimulation; (11) increased range of movement, used alongside PNF
mobilisation; (12) reduction of non-infectious swelling; (13) halting or slowing mild to
moderate scoliotic curvature with ES applied to specific back muscles (Axelgaard et al,
1983; Friedman et al, 1982; Kots & Chwilon, 1971; Schultz et al, 1981); (14) preferential
recruitment of the largest-diameter motor axons first, the reverse of natural recruitment
order, making them a supplementary route to axons that are hard to activate under normal
training (Enoka, 1988); (15) an acute strength increase after a single session: Alon (1985),
studying 14 subjects, found one experimental ES session produced a mean 13% increase in
quadriceps strength; (16) improved metabolic economy: Ratov & Kryazhev (1986) found
electrically stimulating the quadriceps during cycling and speed skating reduced oxygen
consumption by 10-17% and extended time-to-fatigue by 15-20%, and treadmill running with ES
reduced oxygen consumption by about 10%, or by about 5% if the stimulated muscles had just
performed the work; (17) increased contractile speed of skeletal muscle via phasic-type ES.
(PDF pp. 221-224, printed pp. 210-213, "4.2.1.3 Clinical Applications of
Electrostimulation.")

**Why it works** — The authors give a distinct proposed mechanism for each effect
(catalogued above per item) rather than one unifying mechanism; several (items 4-5, 14, 17)
are tied to fibre-type recruitment and conversion, while others (6-13) are tied to blood
flow, central neurochemistry, or mechanical pumping action. Inference: no single theory in
this passage ties all seventeen together; the authors' own attempt at unification is the
separate MACS/MICS theory (see
[[claim--macs-mics-integrated-theory-of-electrostimulation]]).

**When to apply** — Use this as a reference catalog when deciding whether ES could serve a
specific, narrow purpose (e.g. swelling reduction, pain relief, post-training recuperation)
distinct from strength development, which the authors treat as ES's weakest and most
contested claimed benefit (see
[[claim--kots-electrostimulation-strength-claims-not-replicated]]). Inference: for Athletic
Medicine or Applied Performance settings, items 6-13 (blood flow, pain, spasm, swelling,
recuperation, ROM) map most directly onto standard rehabilitation and recovery use cases,
while items 1-5, 14-17 (strength, endurance, speed, recruitment, metabolic economy) are the
more contested performance-training claims.

**When NOT to apply** — Each item rests on citations from roughly 1948-1988, mostly single
studies or small samples (e.g. Alon's 13% figure is from 14 subjects, one session); none are
independently re-validated within this ingest. Item 4's fibre-type conversion evidence is
from animal (cross-innervation, continuous 24-hour stimulation) studies, not directly
transferable to standard clinical ES dosing. Do not treat this list as equally strong
evidence across all seventeen items; some (blood flow, pain relief, swelling) have broader
citation support here than others (scoliosis, acute strength).

**Example** — Inference: a post-training recovery application (item 10, low-current
stimulation to aid relaxation and sleep onset) is a narrower, better-supported use case than
citing this same list to justify ES as a primary strength-training tool (item 1, contested
above); a practitioner should not conflate the two when deciding whether to add ES to a
program.

# The Authors' MACS/MICS Theory of Electrostimulation: A Speculative Synthesis, Not a Settled Mechanism

**What it is** — Building on research showing that very low currents (as little as 10
microamps, far below the milliamp-range currents of standard faradic/TENS devices) can
match or exceed conventional electrostimulation's (ES) benefits without causing contraction,
pain blockade or heating (Matteson & Eberhardt, 1985), the authors propose an "integrated
theory": macroelectric cellular stimulation (**MACS**, currents exceeding one milliamp) acts
as a physiological stressor producing Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome-type alarm
response (citing Eriksson et al, 1981, who found traditional ES's acute effects resemble
intense voluntary exercise; and Gambke et al, 1985, who found in animal studies that
long-term MACS causes some, mainly fast-twitch, fibres to degenerate and be replaced via
satellite-cell proliferation). Applying Selye's model (see 1.16), the body would draw on
"superficial adaptation energy stores" to adapt to MACS via increased strength, endurance,
or fibre-type transformation; if the ES is too intense, prolonged or misapplied, adaptation
might fail or shift toward slow-twitch fibres and reduced strength, and "excessively
demanding MACS conceivably might cause the body to draw on its deep adaptation energy and
lead to permanent tissue damage." Microelectric cellular stimulation (**MICS**, currents
below one milliamp) is proposed not to act as a stressor at all, but to elicit biochemical
changes tied to "resonant attunement" of the stimulus to cellular/subcellular processes,
citing a chain of supporting findings: Becker and colleagues on sub-threshold modulation of
electric fields across nerve and glial cells regulating growth and communication, including
wound-healing acceleration and partial limb regeneration in animals (Becker, 1974; Becker &
Marino, 1982); Nordenstrom's proposed "closed electric circuits" in the body carrying ionic
flow via blood vessels (Nordenstrom, 1983); Pilla's (1974) electrochemical membrane-signaling
model and "cellular windows" that respond most to specific frequencies, pulse widths and
amplitudes (Pilla, 1981); Rodan et al (1978), who found 5 Hz ES stimulated DNA synthesis in
chick cartilage and rat bone by up to 27% (but not in chick skin fibroblasts or rat spleen
lymphocytes) 4-6 hours after 15 minutes of stimulation; and Cheng et al (1982), who found
50-1000 microamp currents increased rat tissue ATP concentration by 300-500% and amino-acid
transport/protein synthesis by up to 40%, while currents above one milliamp depressed ATP
and protein synthesis (traditional ES commonly exceeds 20 milliamps, at which point this
depression is "nearly 50%"). Practical implication drawn from the theory: MICS can be used
far more routinely than MACS, since it "cannot produce overtraining or injury," while MACS
should be dosed and progressed like any other training stressor. (PDF pp. 224-227, printed
pp. 213-216, "4.2.1.4 Further Research Findings" and "4.2.1.5 An Integrated Theory of
Electrostimulation.")

**Why it works** — This is the authors' own proposed synthesis, not a mechanism independently
established elsewhere in the text. It rests on two distinct evidentiary threads of very
different standing: (a) a defensible, mainstream physiological point that MACS-range ES acts
as a training/adaptation stressor subject to a dose-response and overtraining risk (the
Selye/Eriksson/Gambke citations); and (b) a set of bioelectromagnetic claims (Becker's
regeneration work, Nordenstrom's "biologically closed electric circuits," Pilla's cellular
windows) that were speculative even by 1990s standards and have not entered mainstream
sports-science or clinical practice as the general theory of cell communication the authors
present them as. The authors themselves flag some of this as still-developing: "little is
known about electrical fields at the subcellular level," and classical action-potential
theory "does not explain the mechanisms whereby electric and other fields stimulate
processes such as the repair of fractures... and reduction of thrombosis" (printed 210).

**When to apply** — Inference: the (a) thread, that macro-current ES functions as a
stressor requiring progressive dosing rather than a fixed protocol, is a reasonable,
generally-applicable caution consistent with basic training-load principles and does not
depend on accepting thread (b). Do not use this entry to justify low-current "microcurrent"
devices for performance claims (tissue repair beyond standard wound-healing use, cellular
"resonance," or Nordenstrom-style circuit theory) without independent, modern verification
outside this book.

**When NOT to apply** — Marked contested: several supporting claims in thread (b) (glial-cell
signaling as a major regulatory pathway comparable to semiconductor circuits; Nordenstrom's
"closed electric circuits"; "resonant attunement" as the operative principle of MICS) are
not mainstream, independently-replicated physiology as of this ingest, and this entry does
not attempt to verify them against current literature. The Rodan (1978) and Cheng (1982)
findings are animal/cell-culture studies (chick cartilage, rat bone, rat tissue) at specific
narrow current ranges; they do not establish that MICS produces the general "growth,
adaptation and repair" effects the authors attribute to it in human athletes. Do not treat
"MACS" and "MICS" as terms used outside this text; they appear to be the authors' own
coinage for this section.

**Example** — Inference: a practitioner who reads "10-microamp microcurrent devices are as
effective as faradic stimulators without the risks" should recognize this claim rests
primarily on thread (b) above (Becker, Nordenstrom, Pilla), not on independently replicated
modern evidence, and should treat performance or healing claims for such devices with the
same skepticism the authors themselves apply to unverified action-potential-only models.

# Ratov's Functional Electrostimulation (FES): Timing the Current to the Athlete's Own EMG Signal During the Sport Skill Itself

**What it is** — Siff writes in the first person that "little or no mention is made in the
West" of Functional Electrostimulation (FES, called FENS in the West), which in the West
refers narrowly to using ES to produce movement in spinally disabled patients who cannot
voluntarily activate their muscles. In Russia and other Eastern European countries the term
also covers a different application: applying electrostimulation to able-bodied athletes
during natural sporting movements, timed to intensify muscle tension at the most
appropriate phase of the movement itself. Professor Igor Ratov of the State Central
Institute of Sports Science in Moscow developed this from his PhD work on the interrelation
between electrical stimulation and electromyography, using EMG signals from agonist and
antagonist muscles as triggers or control mechanisms to phase electrical stimulation into
the specific phases of a functional sporting movement. Ratov applied this with fencers,
boxers, rowers, shotputters and weightlifters, and, per Siff's first-person account of
witnessing the work in Ratov's laboratory, used concurrent EMG recordings "to phase the FES
correctly into specific sporting movements, most specifically to avoid producing spurious
muscle tension or uncoordinated movement." Over time, athletes learn to contract their
muscles synchronously with the externally applied electrical field, said to produce greater
strength, power and speed; this is called "active electrostimulation" (applied to actively
contracting muscles) as distinct from "passive electrostimulation" (applied to relaxed
muscles), the latter being the method most common in physical therapy. Ratov reportedly
"stresses that this type of functional stimulation is far more consistently successful than
the passive methods attributed to Kots by his Western supporters" (see
[[claim--kots-electrostimulation-strength-claims-not-replicated]]). (PDF pp. 226-227, printed
pp. 215-216, "4.2.1.6 The Use of Electrostimulation in Training," "Sports Functional
Electrostimulation.")

**Why it works** — The stated logic: because the electrical stimulus is phased by the
athlete's own real-time EMG signal into the correct part of the movement (rather than
applied on a fixed, movement-independent schedule as in passive/general ES), it can
intensify muscle tension precisely when and where the sport skill needs it without disrupting
the coordinated movement pattern. Inference: no data (strength gain percentages, EMG
amplitude changes, performance-test results) are reported for this method in this passage;
the claim of superiority over passive ES is Ratov's own stated position, as reported by
Siff, not a cited experimental comparison.

**When to apply** — Inference: relevant to anyone considering EMG-triggered or
movement-phased electrical stimulation for sport-specific training (as opposed to general
or isolated-muscle ES); the distinguishing feature to look for in any modern device or
protocol claiming this lineage is real-time EMG-based triggering synchronized to the
athlete's own voluntary movement, not a fixed timing schedule.

**When NOT to apply** — Untested: no quantitative outcome data are reported for Ratov's
method in this passage, only a description of the approach and Siff's first-hand
observation that it was applied with named elite-sport categories (fencers, boxers, rowers,
shotputters, weightlifters). The claimed superiority over passive ES ("far more consistently
successful") is reported as Ratov's own assessment via Siff, not verified against a cited
trial. Access to this specific method also depended on specialized laboratory equipment (EMG-linked
stimulators) not generally available.

**Example** — The book's own example is Ratov's application with the named sport
categories above (fencers, boxers, rowers, shotputters, weightlifters), reported by Siff as
directly observed; no further invented example is added here.

# Practical Rules for Integrating Electrostimulation Into a Programme: Sequencing, Electrode Placement, Dose Limits

**What it is** — Several Russian researchers (Purvin, Deniskin, Khodykin, cited without
individual dates) studied how electrostimulation (ES) combines with other training means and
found that using ES first, followed by plyometrics, produces a greater training effect than
the reverse order; a still larger effect came from using ES and plyometrics concurrently
with heavy resistance exercise, while the ES-plyometric combination had its smallest
training effect during periods of complete or partial rest. Electrode placement matters:
adjacent (close) electrode attachment localises the contraction and keeps it fairly
superficial, while distant attachment (one electrode placed at a remote or neutral site)
produces a more extensive, deeper contraction; electrode gel or weak saline solution is
needed for comfortable conduction, and skin irritation or prickliness "invariably" results
from the gel drying out. Placing electrodes directly over major nerves increases contraction
intensity but "can be harmful," and the authors restrict this to use by experienced physical
therapists. On dosing: it is "generally not advisable to apply more than 5-10 minutes of
intense ES per major muscle group," due to associated tissue necrosis and prolonged soreness
that can follow (see [[claim--macs-mics-integrated-theory-of-electrostimulation]] for the
macroelectric/microelectric distinction behind this limit); biofeedback TENS devices that
monitor cellular electrical characteristics and adjust parameters automatically reduce
overstimulation risk. The authors' closing recommendation is that "the untutored reader is
not encouraged to use ES," since safe, effective application "requires considerable
theoretical and practical training," and that casual users typically see only sporadic,
short-term progress before discontinuing within a few months; in several countries
(the authors name the USA), ES devices may not legally be sold to the public or used
commercially by anyone without medical or physiotherapy training. (PDF pp. 225-227, printed
pp. 214-216, "4.2.1.6 The Use of Electrostimulation in Training.")

**Why it works** — For the sequencing finding, the authors give no separate mechanism here
beyond the general after-effect logic already stated for training order (see
[[framework--non-commutative-non-associative-training-order]]): ES applied first may
potentiate the neuromuscular state for the plyometric work that follows, and the combined
concurrent condition with heavy resistance stacks all three stimuli within the same session
window. For electrode placement, the mechanism is straightforwardly the current's path
through tissue between the two electrode sites. For the dose limit, the mechanism given
(tissue necrosis, prolonged soreness) implies macro-current ES imposes real mechanical/metabolic
stress on the stimulated tissue, consistent with the MACS-as-stressor framing.

**When to apply** — Use the sequencing finding (ES before plyometrics beats the reverse; ES
plus plyometrics concurrent with heavy resistance beats either combination alone) as a
specific instance of the book's broader non-commutative-training-order argument (see
[[framework--non-commutative-non-associative-training-order]]) when programming ES within a
session. Use the 5-10 minute per-muscle-group ceiling and the electrode-placement guidance
as baseline practical limits if ES is added to a programme, and restrict direct-over-nerve
placement to trained personnel.

**When NOT to apply** — The sequencing citations (Purvin, Deniskin, Khodykin) give no dates,
sample sizes, or effect-size numbers in this passage, only a directional ranking of four
conditions (ES-then-plyometrics; plyometrics-then-ES; ES+plyometrics+heavy resistance
concurrent; ES+plyometrics during rest); treat this as a single-lab directional finding, not
a quantified prescription. The authors' own recommendation against untutored use, and the
legal restrictions they cite, mean this entry should not be read as a self-administration
guide; it summarises the book's own cautions, not a certified protocol.

**Example** — Inference: a strength coach considering adding ES to a plyometric-heavy block
should schedule the ES stimulus before the plyometric work in the same session (per the
cited ranking), keep any single muscle group's intense ES exposure under 10 minutes, and use
adjacent electrode placement for a more localised, superficial target versus distant
placement for a deeper, more extensive contraction.

# Muscle Recruitment Stays Fairly Consistent at Moderate Loads but Shifts Toward Proximal Muscles as Resistance or Velocity Rises

**What it is** — Two related findings on how muscle involvement changes with load and
velocity during repetitive barbell lifting. First, the "most stable indicator" of
coordinated muscular activity in repetitive lifting is the sequential involvement of the
same specific muscles across reps: in an experiment at 60% of 1RM, the muscles taking part
in the work remained the same for 82% of the time across all athletes tested; at 80% of
1RM, consistency was lower (showing greater individuality), but higher among qualified
athletes than novices, and fatigue also disrupted coordination (Pakhomov, 1967). The
relative contribution of participating muscles can also change during repetitive work
(Lazareva, 1966; Kozlov, 1966), and the number of muscle groups involved can either decrease
(Moikin, 1964) or increase (Averyanov, 1963) as a set progresses. Second, movements executed
with little effort or low velocity involve mostly the muscles of the body's distal links
(Tochilov, 1946; Kosilov, 1948; Vinogradov, 1951); against significant resistance or at high
velocity, activity characteristically shifts to the muscles of the proximal links, "which
tend to play a more tonic or postural role." The authors draw a direct implication from this
second finding: "muscle isolation under conditions of significant resistance is impossible,"
a point developed later in the chapter (see
[[claim--muscle-isolation-is-load-dependent-mostly-illusory-under-heavy-load]]). (PDF p.
229, printed p. 218, "4.2.2 Resistance and Strength Training.")

**Why it works** — No explicit mechanism is given for why recruitment consistency at 60% of
1RM (82%) exceeds that at 80% of 1RM, only the observation that qualified athletes show
greater consistency than less qualified ones at the higher load, and that fatigue disrupts
it further. Inference: this is consistent with the general principle that skilled athletes
have more stable, better-rehearsed motor patterns, which higher loads and fatigue both stress
more than lower loads. For the proximal/distal shift, the authors give no mechanism beyond
the empirical citations; Inference: this is plausibly related to proximal muscles' larger
cross-sectional area and postural/stabilising role becoming more necessary as the system
must resist larger external forces or move faster.

**When to apply** — Use the 60%-vs-80%-of-1RM recruitment-consistency finding as a caution
when interpreting EMG or force-plate data collected across a set: consistency of which
muscles are active can itself change with load and fatigue state, independent of the
athlete's technique intent. Use the proximal/distal shift finding when auditing exercise
selection for "isolation" claims at high resistance: expect proximal, postural muscle
involvement to increase, not stay constant, as load or velocity rises. **Eric's baseline:**
this is directly relevant to interpreting force-plate strategy metrics (phase durations,
which segments contribute) that vary with load, per the department's driver/strategy metric
framework (`method-baseline.md`, "Force plate and neuromuscular monitoring," citing
`hd-odsf-metrics.md`).

**When NOT to apply** — These are single-lab, era-specific findings (Pakhomov 1967;
Lazareva/Kozlov 1966; Moikin 1964; Averyanov 1963; Tochilov 1946; Kosilov 1948; Vinogradov
1951) without reported sample sizes, exercise types, or modern replication in this text; the
specific 82% figure applies to Pakhomov's particular protocol at 60% of 1RM, not a general
constant. Do not assume the direction of the number-of-muscle-groups change (increase per
Averyanov vs decrease per Moikin) is reconciled in the text; both are cited as findings
without explanation of why they differ.

**Example** — Inference: an assistant coach comparing an athlete's EMG-active muscle groups
on an early, light-effort warm-up set against a heavy top set within the same exercise
should expect to see a proximal shift in the heavy set (more trunk/hip involvement) even if
technique looks visually similar, per this claim; this is a monitoring inference, not stated
in the source.
