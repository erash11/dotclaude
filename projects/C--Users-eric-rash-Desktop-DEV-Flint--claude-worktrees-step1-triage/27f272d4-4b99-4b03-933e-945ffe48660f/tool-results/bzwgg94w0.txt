    14	# The Pre-Competitive Microcycle: Cut Volume Hard, Raise Maximal-Speed and Sport-Specific Work, Keep Frequency
    15	
    16	**What it is** — The pre-competitive microcycle forms the content of the realization
    17	mesocycle (see [[framework--realization-mesocycle-taper-prescription]]) and is also called the
    18	taper at the microcycle level (PDF pp. 104-105, printed pp. 93-94; verified against page
    19	images). It must satisfy three demands (PDF p. 104, printed p. 93): sport-specific
    20	exercises/tasks that simulate the forthcoming competition and build mental readiness and
    21	toughness; development of maximal speed/power and sport-specific quickness; and full (or
    22	partial) restoration after the preceding transmutation mesocycle's fatiguing workloads. Table
    23	3.5 (PDF p. 105, printed p. 94, verified against page image) gives seven prescriptive
    24	characteristics: **workload volume**, substantially reduced, to create conditions for full
    25	recovery; **volume of intense exercises specifically**, substantially reduced versus the
    26	prior mesocycle, so total drill volume falls but quality rises; **contribution of maximum
    27	speed/power exercises**, substantially **increased** (well-rested athletes respond better to
    28	maximal-speed drills, and maximal-speed training residuals are the shortest of any ability, per
    29	[[claim--residual-training-effect-durations-by-motor-ability]]); **contribution of
    30	sport-specific tasks**, substantially increased (simulative tasks improve adaptation to
    31	expected competitive stressors); **workout frequency**, usually kept **similar** to the
    32	previous mesocycle (subdividing the same total work into more, smaller portions raises
    33	quality per session even as volume falls); **organization**, a rational mix of group,
    34	individual and mixed workouts, weighted somewhat more toward individual work than usual, so
    35	athletes can attend to personal technical detail and coach rapport; **restoration**,
    36	deliberately favorable conditions and an increased volume of restorative exercises/workouts,
    37	since athletes have more free time and motivation to use it. Issurin states that in qualified
    38	athletes, especially during a pre-competitive training camp, maintaining frequency in practice
    39	means **eight to ten workouts per week** even as volume per workout drops (PDF p. 106, printed
    40	p. 95). Workout timing should match the expected competition's daily biological-rhythm window
    41	(example given: Olympic canoe-kayak/rowing regattas run only in the morning, so pre-Olympic
    42	training is scheduled to place peak-effort sessions at that same morning time, PDF p. 107,
    43	printed p. 96). Figure 3.7 (PDF p. 108, printed p. 97) gives a worked chart for a
    44	morning-peak-performance pre-competitive microcycle, alternating maximum-strength/technique
    45	mornings with sport-specific-simulation/alactic afternoons, moving toward heavier
    46	sport-specific-simulation and explosive-strength content later in the week. On strength work
    47	specifically, Issurin notes a real disagreement in the field: many athletes (particularly
    48	swimmers, per Platonov & Fesenko, 1990, and also reported among volleyball and tennis players)
    49	report high-resistance work pre-competition degrades fine technical skill, arguing for
    50	reducing or excluding it; but sport-specific maximal/explosive strength work is also argued to
    51	maintain the technical force component (Bompa & Carrera, 2003) and to prevent uncontrolled,
    52	stress-hormone-driven muscle-mass loss pre-competition (PDF p. 108, printed p. 97).
    53	
    54	**Why it works** — Reduced total load is stated as the "principal condition" for reaching
    55	supercompensation before competition (PDF p. 106, printed p. 95); the two things this
    56	recovered state is then spent on, maximal-speed drills and sport-specific simulation, are
    57	each chosen because their quality specifically depends on a well-rested nervous system and
    58	energy-resource availability (maximal speed) or on the athlete's capacity to fully attend to
    59	technical/tactical detail under simulated competitive stress (sport-specific tasks). Keeping
    60	frequency similar while cutting volume is explicitly a quality lever, not a rest lever: more,
    61	shorter, higher-quality sessions rather than fewer long ones.
    62	
    63	**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college football calendar** — **Bowl prep:** the
    64	week(s) immediately before a bowl game map most directly onto this framework: cut total
    65	practice/lift volume hard, hold practice frequency roughly where it has been, shift the
    66	practice mix toward maximal-speed/explosive work and heavy game-plan (sport-specific)
    67	simulation, and weight walkthroughs/individual work more than a normal week. **In-season
    68	tuning week:** the standard in-season game week already functions as a compressed version of
    69	this pattern (practice volume tapers toward the game, Thursday/Friday sessions shift toward
    70	walkthrough-intensity techno-tactical work); see
    71	[[framework--in-season-team-sport-mini-block-microcycle]] for the specific team-sport
    72	adaptation Issurin gives later in this chapter. **Season opener after fall camp:** the final
    73	camp week before the opener is a pre-competitive microcycle relative to the whole camp block:
    74	volume should come down from camp peak even though frequency (practice count) may stay similar.
    75	The strength-during-taper disagreement Issurin reports (reduce high-resistance work vs. keep
    76	sport-specific strength to protect force and prevent hormone-driven muscle loss) is directly
    77	relevant to the recurring question of whether to lift heavy in bowl-prep week; this text does
    78	not resolve it and presents both positions as live in the field. **Eric's baseline** gives no
    79	session-frequency rule for a taper anywhere in `method-baseline.md`; the closest material is
    80	the general in-season rule "maintain intensity, reduce volume" (`method-baseline.md`, "Strength
    81	and power," citing `01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md` pp.20-21), which agrees directionally
    82	(reduce volume, don't reduce intensity/speed work) but does not specify a frequency-holding
    83	rule, an eight-to-ten-sessions-per-week figure, or an explicit sport-specific-task ramp; this
    84	entry supplies that missing numeric structure, not something already adopted.
    85	
    86	**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — Every numeric particular in Table 3.5 (frequency held at
    87	8-10/week, "substantially reduced/increased" volume and speed contributions) is Issurin's own
    88	applied synthesis with no cited dose-response study behind the specific numbers; he
    89	acknowledges directly that "many contradictions exist regarding workout duration and frequency
    90	of high intensity exercises" in the pre-competitive/taper literature generally (PDF p. 105,
    91	printed p. 94, citing Kubukeli et al., 2002). **Population caveat:** the worked example
    92	(Figure 3.7) and both named case examples (Olympic canoe-kayak/rowing morning scheduling;
    93	swimmer/volleyball/tennis strength-reduction reports) are individual, dual, or non-contact
    94	sports; none address a 100+ player roster with heterogeneous individual readiness, a fixed
    95	practice-week structure set partly by NCAA rules, or the additional load of team walkthroughs
    96	and special-teams reps that a football pre-competitive week must still include. The
    97	"8-10 workouts per week" frequency figure assumes an individual/dual-sport training camp
    98	structure and should not be read as a literal session-count target for a football bowl-prep
    99	week. Issurin does not address game-week travel, opponent scouting load, or academic-calendar
   100	constraints anywhere in this framework.
   101	
   102	**Example** — A bowl-prep week could hold practice count similar to a normal in-season week
   103	while cutting total practice/lift volume substantially, shifting a larger share of practice
   104	time to explosive/speed work and full-speed game-plan simulation than a mid-season week would
   105	carry, and deliberately expanding individual position-group time (film, technique correction)
   106	relative to full-team periods, while treating the "how much to lift" question as an open
   107	disagreement the source itself does not resolve, not a settled rule.

    14	# A Two-Mini-Block Weekly Structure for In-Season Team Sports, Naming American Football Directly
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Issurin addresses the specific constraint that team and dual sports (he
    17	names **soccer, American football, rugby, volleyball** explicitly) run a 20-35 week season
    18	where "the possibility of inserting highly concentrated training blocks is very limited"
    19	(PDF pp. 108-109, printed pp. 97-98). He cites Fleck and Kraemer (1997): players typically
    20	maintain strength near pre-season levels for 14-16 weeks, after which strength variables
    21	decrease dramatically. Because the usual one-week gap between games is too short to work
    22	effectively on every training target (metabolic conditioning, maximal/explosive strength,
    23	injury prevention, techno-tactical skill, game-specific endurance, active restoration) at once,
    24	Issurin proposes adapting three BPC approaches at the *week* level: high concentration of
    25	specialized workloads within **mini-blocks**, separating mini-blocks by training modality, and
    26	determining **key-workouts** within each mini-block. The worked example (Figure 3.8, PDF p.
    27	109, printed p. 98, "based on Baker, 2001," a real cited source, not an uncited synthesis)
    28	splits a standard Monday-Sunday week into two mini-blocks. **Mini-block 1 (Monday-Wednesday)**
    29	targets sport-specific maximal and explosive strength as the dominant training aim: it opens
    30	Monday with an active-recovery session (medium/low load, aerobic and general conditioning),
    31	then delivers the strength emphasis across **two key-workouts**, combined with a medium-load
    32	team practice (Tuesday afternoon) and a conditioning-restorative workout (Wednesday afternoon).
    33	This sequencing leaves **28-30 hours of recovery** after the strength key-workouts before
    34	mini-block 2 begins, to let the anabolic/restorative process run before the next demand.
    35	**Mini-block 2 (Thursday-Saturday)** targets sport-specific team practice and immediate game
    36	preparation: it includes two obligatory team practices, one before the day off (intended to
    37	deliver a pronounced game-specific stimulus) and a second, load-restricted session immediately
    38	before the game itself (mostly techno-tactical drills tied to the specific forthcoming
    39	opponent). Issurin notes some highly motivated teams skip the day off and add an optional
    40	Friday-morning conditioning or techno-tactical session instead, trading recovery time for
    41	extra preparation; he states the tradeoff explicitly without endorsing either choice. The
    42	chart gives priority to maximal-strength development over explosive-type work within the week,
    43	which Issurin explains as protecting "somatic, functional and anabolic status," while noting a
    44	cited counter-example (Newton et al., 2006) of sequencing classic high-resistance training with
    45	ballistic explosive-type mesocycles to enhance jump performance instead.
    46	
    47	**Why it works** — The mini-block structure is this chapter's direct answer to the constraint
    48	stated in [[principle--concentration-of-training-workloads]] (BPC needs concentrated,
    49	sequenced blocks) meeting the constraint that team sports cannot suspend competition long
    50	enough to run full-length accumulation/transmutation/realization mesocycles
    51	(see [[framework--accumulation-mesocycle-duration-and-monitoring]] and
    52	[[framework--transmutation-mesocycle-duration-and-overtraining-monitoring]] for what those
    53	normally require in duration). Splitting the week itself into two purpose-specific mini-blocks,
    54	each with its own key-workouts, is Issurin's way of keeping some of BPC's concentration
    55	benefit even though the underlying block can only be one week long, not several.
    56	
    57	**When to apply — this is the entry most directly answering the in-season half of the
    58	strength-and-conditioning miss-log's "peaking phase structure for collegiate football" gap
    59	(logged 2026-09-03)** — Issurin explicitly names American football as a sport this structure
    60	is meant for. **Eric's baseline's** in-season rule, "maintain intensity, reduce volume"
    61	(`method-baseline.md`, "Strength and power," citing `01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md`
    62	pp.20-21), is compatible with and gets a concrete weekly shape from this framework: the
    63	early-week mini-block is where intensity (maximal/explosive strength) gets its two key-workouts,
    64	while the late-week mini-block is deliberately techno-tactical and load-restricted heading into
    65	the game, which *is* the volume reduction the baseline rule calls for. The 28-30 hour recovery
    66	window between the strength mini-block and the practice-heavy mini-block gives a specific,
    67	citable number for how much gap a college program might target between its heaviest in-season
    68	lift and the next high-intensity team session.
    69	
    70	**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — The specific day-by-day chart (Figure 3.8) is built from
    71	one cited source, Baker (2001), a study of professional and college-aged rugby league players'
    72	in-season concurrent training: a real, checkable citation, but a single study in rugby league,
    73	a sport with a different weekly game/practice rhythm than college football, which layers
    74	additional constraints (NCAA-mandated practice-time limits, travel, and a Saturday-only game
    75	schedule rather than rugby league's varying weekly structure). **Population caveat:** none of
    76	this section's evidence comes from American football itself; Issurin lists
    77	football alongside soccer, rugby, and volleyball as sports the *problem* applies to, but the
    78	worked example and its supporting citation are rugby-specific. **Inference:** the specific
    79	"two key-workouts, 28-30 hours recovery" prescription should be treated as a structural
    80	template to adapt to football's own practice-count and padded/unpadded-practice rules, not a
    81	ready-made in-season lift schedule. Issurin gives no guidance here for bye weeks, short
    82	(Thursday-game) weeks, or the postseason gap before a bowl game; those fall outside this
    83	weekly-cycle framework and are closer to the pre-competitive/realization material in
    84	[[framework--pre-competitive-microcycle-tuning]] and
    85	[[framework--prolonging-residual-effects-with-mini-blocks]].
    86	
    87	**Example** — A Baylor in-season week could place the two heaviest strength key-workouts on
    88	Monday and Tuesday (following a Monday active-recovery/conditioning opener), hold Wednesday as
    89	a conditioning-restorative bridge, then shift Thursday-Saturday entirely to team practice and
    90	game-specific techno-tactical work, with the Friday or Saturday walkthrough load-restricted and
    91	opponent-specific, and checking that the strength work and the first hard team practice of
    92	mini-block 2 are separated by at least a full day, per the 28-30 hour figure.

    14	# Build a Microcycle Key-Workouts First: A Six-Step Sequence and Five Design Rules
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Issurin gives a six-step operational sequence for compiling a training
    17	microcycle (Table 3.6, PDF p. 111, printed p. 100): (1) determine dominant and secondary
    18	training modalities, based on the annual plan and the current mesocycle's specificity; (2)
    19	determine, place and compile **key-workouts** (see
    20	[[framework--key-workout-and-key-exercise-concept]]), which carry the main development
    21	impact; (3) determine restorative workouts and restorative "windows," to facilitate the
    22	key-workouts and prevent excess fatigue; (4) determine, place and compile other
    23	developmental/supportive workouts, paying special attention to how preceding workouts affect
    24	sensitivity to subsequent ones; (5) select monitoring means for the targeted abilities and
    25	functions; (6) plan special events (psychologist, physician, team meeting, etc.). Alongside
    26	this sequence, Issurin states five general design rules (PDF p. 112, printed p. 101):
    27	**first**, key-workout priority: once targeted abilities are defined, design should
    28	start from the key-workouts, not fill them in last; **second**, arrangement around
    29	key-workouts: the preceding session affects the athlete's sensitivity to the key-workout's
    30	stimulus, while the following session determines fatigue accumulation and restoration, so both
    31	neighbors must be chosen deliberately; **third**, sharing restorative means: restorative
    32	workouts, restorative exercises (low-intensity aerobic work, stretching, relaxation, shaking,
    33	breathing) and restorative procedures (massage, sauna, hydro/physiotherapy, mental training)
    34	are "indispensable" and must be expertly planned into every microcycle, not treated as
    35	optional filler; **fourth**, workload initiation and peaks: a day off lowers readiness for
    36	high workloads, so the first session after a day off should **not** be a key-workout; the
    37	number and placement of key-workouts is what determines whether the week is a one-, two-, or
    38	three-peak design (see [[framework--microcycle-types-and-load-wave-design]]); **fifth**,
    39	monitoring: key-workout execution data (achievement level, technical quality, HR, blood
    40	lactate, RPE) is the single best indicator of an athlete's current state.
    41	
    42	**Why it works** — This is a process heuristic, not a physiological mechanism: Issurin's
    43	stated rationale is that once the microcycle's actual development stimulus (the key-workouts)
    44	is fixed, every other design decision (what precedes it, what follows it, how restoration is
    45	distributed) exists to protect that stimulus's quality and manage the fatigue it produces.
    46	Designing "everything else first, then slotting in the hard part" risks the key-workout landing
    47	on a fatigued or under-prepared athlete.
    48	
    49	**When to apply** — As a checklist when building or auditing any microcycle: has the dominant
    50	modality been named before workouts were scheduled; are the key-workouts placed away from the
    51	first session after a day off; is there a planned restorative window around each key-workout;
    52	and is monitoring tied specifically to key-workout performance rather than spread evenly across
    53	every session. Useful as a coach-facing planning template independent of sport.
    54	
    55	**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — This is Issurin's own applied synthesis of BPC design
    56	logic; no citation or comparative study is given for the six-step sequence or the five rules
    57	themselves. Treat them as a structured practitioner heuristic, not an experimentally
    58	validated planning method. **Inference:** the sequence assumes a coach already has a clear
    59	single dominant-and-secondary modality assignment for the week (step 1); it gives no guidance
    60	for a team-sport week that must simultaneously serve several position groups with different
    61	dominant needs on the same day, a common real constraint in football that this chapter's
    62	narrower single-athlete/single-event examples elsewhere do not have to solve.
    63	
    64	**Example** — Before finalizing a week's training plan, a coach could walk the six steps in
    65	order: name this week's dominant ability, place its key-workouts (never as the first session
    66	after a day off), build restorative windows around them, fill in supporting sessions with an
    67	eye to how each affects the next, pick 2-3 monitoring indicators tied to the key-workouts, and
    68	only then schedule any non-training events.
    69

    14	# Accumulation Mesocycle: 3-6 Weeks Early, Down to 10-14 Days Late-Season, With Diminishing Weekly Returns
    15	
    16	**What it is** — This is the full prescriptive detail behind the accumulation block first
    17	introduced conceptually in [[framework--accumulation-transmutation-realization-mesocycles]]
    18	(Ch. 1). Issurin gives duration as a function of two competing factors (PDF pp. 113-115,
    19	printed pp. 102-104): the time needed for a real cumulative training effect in the targeted
    20	basic abilities, aerobic endurance and maximal strength, both requiring "profound morphological
    21	and even organic changes," versus the time limit the competition calendar allows. He cites a
    22	supporting case study: eight highly qualified female kayakers, monitored over a **20-week**
    23	program of high-resistance training (**4-5 hr/week**) aimed at maximal strength and aerobic
    24	endurance, tracked via maximal isometric force in kayak-specific positions (Figure 3.9, PDF p.
    25	114, printed p. 103, based on Sharobajko, 1984). Strength gain and weekly improvement rate fell
    26	sharply over time: the first 3 weeks produced a 5.9% average gain (1.93%/week improvement
    27	rate); the next 3 weeks added only 1.6% more (0.53%/week); the remaining 14 weeks added only
    28	0.25% and then 0.13% per week. Issurin's stated conclusion: the fitness program was "very
    29	effective at the beginning, reasonably effective through the first six weeks, and had only a
    30	minor effect for the next 14 weeks," directly supporting BPC's case for shorter, higher-turnover
    31	blocks over long, continuous ones (citing the same general adaptive-decline concept in
    32	Bouchard, 1986, as applying to aerobic-endurance programs too). Based on this, Issurin sets the
    33	following duration bands (PDF p. 115, printed p. 104): **up to six weeks** when deeper
    34	physiological change is the goal (typically early season, when athletes are less
    35	calendar-constrained); **three-four weeks** at mid-season, once important-competition timing
    36	starts to dictate mesocycle sequencing; **10-14 days** at season's end, when important
    37	competitions come at short intervals. Content is structured by sequencing microcycle types
    38	(Table 3.7, PDF p. 116, printed p. 105): the **initial** part uses a restoration microcycle
    39	(if starting a new training stage, not needed after a transition period) followed by an
    40	adjustment microcycle (can be shorter than a week); the **mid-and-end** part is built from
    41	loading microcycles, whose count sets the mesocycle's total length, with optional impact and/or
    42	restoration microcycles inserted (a restorative microcycle, usually 3-6 days, can go
    43	mid-mesocycle, or immediately after an impact microcycle before the next loading microcycle).
    44	General load-shape rule: increase load gradually at the start, hold maximal load in the middle,
    45	and reduce load at the end so the next mesocycle (transmutation) begins without excess fatigue;
    46	a 3-4 day restorative microcycle can be inserted near the end of accumulation for this purpose.
    47	Monitoring (Table 3.8, PDF p. 117, printed p. 106; verified against page image) tracks three
    48	categories: **workload performance** (total weekly mileage, total sport-specific reps/week,
    49	key-workout outcomes, each compared actual-vs-planned, week-to-week, and across the season);
    50	**targeted (basic) abilities** (time-trial results, free-weight-trial results, average
    51	key-workout results, using "appropriate valid tests"); and **training responses** (resting
    52	heart rate, blood urea and CPK, body mass/muscle mass/fat component, blood lactate after
    53	trials). Anthropometric change is flagged as a specific, useful side-outcome to track (strength
    54	training raises muscle mass; aerobic training reduces fat component), especially relevant in
    55	weight-category or leanness-sensitive sports.
    56	
    57	**Why it works** — The kayaker case study is offered as direct evidence for the general
    58	biological principle that "the adaptive response induced by long-term training declines with
    59	time" (Bouchard, 1986): diminishing returns set in within weeks, not months, for a fixed
    60	training emphasis, which is the physiological argument for keeping each mesocycle-block short
    61	and switching emphasis rather than running one long undifferentiated program.
    62	
    63	**When to apply — Inference, mapped to a college football calendar** — **Fall camp** functions
    64	as a short accumulation mesocycle at the low end of Issurin's range (closer to the 10-14 day
    65	figure, or the 3-4 week figure if camp's aerobic/strength-base emphasis is counted from
    66	voluntary summer conditioning): given the diminishing-returns pattern in the kayaker case
    67	(most of a strength/aerobic gain arrives in the first 3-6 weeks), a multi-month, undifferentiated
    68	summer conditioning block is exactly the pattern this framework argues against; a shorter,
    69	concentrated base-building window, followed by a shift to more specific work, is the BPC
    70	alternative. **The gap between the last regular-season/conference-championship game and a bowl
    71	game** (commonly roughly 3-5 weeks for many programs) is long enough to support a short
    72	accumulation mesocycle (10-14 to 20-25 days, matching both this section's end-of-season figure
    73	and the mini-block interval used later in [[framework--prolonging-residual-effects-with-mini-blocks]])
    74	if the goal is to rebuild any basic-ability deterioration accumulated over a long season before
    75	shifting into a bowl-specific transmutation/realization sequence.
    76	
    77	**When NOT to apply — mandatory** — The kayaker case study is a single unpublished thesis
    78	(Sharobajko, 1984, a Ph.D. dissertation, Moscow All-Union Research Sport Institute), n = 8,
    79	female kayakers, with no control group not receiving the same 20-week program; it demonstrates
    80	a within-group improvement-rate curve, not a controlled comparison against an alternative
    81	mesocycle length. **Population caveat:** this is Issurin's own book-wide pattern: the
    82	accumulation-duration guidance and its supporting evidence are drawn from individual/endurance
    83	Olympic sports (kayaking) tracking isometric strength and presumably aerobic markers, not from
    84	any team-contact-sport population; football's basic-ability profile (short-duration high-force
    85	efforts, position-specific demands, large roster with heterogeneous training age) is not
    86	represented in this evidence base at all. The specific duration bands (six weeks / 3-4 weeks /
    87	10-14 days) are Issurin's own applied judgment, not derived from a stated formula connecting
    88	them to the kayaker data's exact decay curve.
    89	
    90	**Example** — A 3-4 week early-summer strength-aerobic emphasis block, monitored against
    91	Table 3.8's indicators (weekly key-lift totals, a repeated time-trial or aerobic test, resting
    92	HR trend, body composition), gives a coach a citable structure for when to expect most of a
    93	block's gain to already have happened (roughly the first half of the block, per the kayaker
    94	curve) and when continuing the same emphasis is likely yielding only marginal further return.
