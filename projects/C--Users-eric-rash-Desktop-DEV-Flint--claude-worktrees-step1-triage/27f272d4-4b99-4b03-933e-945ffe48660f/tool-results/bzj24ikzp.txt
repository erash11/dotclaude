# The Book's Four Exercise Classes, from Its Own Glossary: General Preparatory, Specialized-Preparatory, Specialized-Developmental, Competitive

**What it is** — This is the book's own definition of the classification Chapter 2's
correlational data depends on, drawn from its front-matter Glossary (PDF pp. 10-14,
printed pp. ix-xiii; Chapter 2's own prose, PDF p. 75, printed p. 58, assumes the reader
already knows these terms and does not redefine them). **Voice note:** the book credits
"About the Author" (Bondarchuk) and "About the Translator" (Michael Yessis) separately,
and the Glossary itself carries no byline. Definitions inside it cite the book's own
numbered bibliography (e.g. the "Block method" entry cites "Y.V. Verkhoshansky [49, 50]"
using the same reference numbers Bondarchuk's own chapters use), which is evidence the
Glossary is Bondarchuk's own material carried through translation rather than a
translator's separate addition — but the book gives no explicit statement either way, so
this entry treats the Glossary as **the book's** definitions rather than asserting they are
Bondarchuk's personal wording. Do not cite this entry as "Bondarchuk says" in a future
entry without that caveat:
- **General (Preparatory) Exercises / GPP**: "do not repeat the competitive actions as a
  whole or their separate parts. Other muscle groups take part in the work being done. The
  functional systems of the body are activated but do not ensure an increase in results in
  the competitive exercise." Function: all-round development, work capacity, and active
  rest/restoration. (PDF p. 12, printed p. xi.)
- **Specialized-Preparatory Exercises / SPP**: like general exercises, "do not repeat the
  competitive actions as a whole or in their separate parts. However, they use similar
  muscle groups in their execution," activating the functions and body systems the main
  movement depends on, with "identical or close to identical regimes of muscle work."
  (PDF p. 14, printed p. xiii.)
- **Specialized-Developmental Exercises / SDE**: "repeat the competitive exercise in its
  separate parts. In executing them, one and the same muscle groups participate, together
  with activation of similar systems and organs. They not only repeat the muscle work
  regime and other systems of the body... but also supersede them," and "serve for entry
  into the state of sports form." (PDF p. 14, printed p. xiii.)
- **Competitive Exercises / CE**: "the acting definition... is 'sport event,' in which the
  athlete participates in competition," executed either in actual competition or in
  training as a model/repeat of competitive conditions, easier or harder (this definition
  spans a page break). (PDF pp. 11-12, printed pp. x-xi.)
Chapter 2's own intro adds a cross-cutting distinction independent of the four classes: a
**local** exercise (uses separate parts/body links — squat, bench press, standing long
jump, vertical jump) versus a **global** exercise (uses all body links together — the
barbell clean). (PDF p. 75, printed p. 58.)

**Why it works** — The Glossary gives no stated mechanism for why these four classes
should differ in transfer value; it only defines them by how much of the competitive
movement each one reproduces. **Inference:** reading the four classes as an ordered
continuum, and connecting that continuum to
[[framework--transfer-chain-general-to-competitive]] and the specifist "identical
elements" position in [[framework--three-viewpoints-on-training-transfer]] (more shared
elements with the competitive movement predicting more reliable transfer), is this entry's
own synthesis across sections of the book, not a claim the Glossary or Chapter 2's intro
states in those words. Chapter 2's correlational data (see the claim entries below) can be
read as an empirical test of that inferred prediction, event by event, but the book itself
does not frame its own tables that way.

**When to apply** — Use as the primary-source definition when auditing any exercise
selection against this taxonomy, in place of or alongside secondary paraphrases of it.
**Eric's baseline:** exercise selection in HHPR 5355 is taught "on a spectrum from general
preparation through specific preparation, specific development, to competitive exercises
(Bondarchuk), replacing the loose and often-misapplied idea of 'sport specific'"
(`method-baseline.md`, "Strength and power," citing
`01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md pp.13-16`; `week1-lecture.md pp.32-36`) — this entry
gives that course material its primary-source citation and exact definitions. This
library already has a secondary-source treatment of the same taxonomy in
`fundamentals-of-sprinting--moyer/framework--sprint-exercise-classes-bondarchuk-four-categories.md`
(vault), built from Moyer's course paraphrase rather than Bondarchuk's own text; that
entry's table ("GPE/SPE/SDE/CE") matches this one closely but labels Moyer's own sprint
examples (bounding, banded pawback, etc.), not Bondarchuk's.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is supported**, not proven: this is the book's own
classification scheme (see the voice note above on Glossary authorship). Two other sources
in this library already use it independently
(the `fundamentals-of-sprinting--moyer` vault entry linked above, and Eric's HHPR 5355
course per `method-baseline.md`), which is evidence the taxonomy circulated beyond this
one book, but this ingest did not check how far it spread through the wider Soviet/Russian
sport-science literature, so that broader claim is not made here. It is a practitioner
taxonomy, not an independently tested causal model, and the boundary between
classes is fuzzy in application (the Moyer-derived entry below makes the same point about
sprint exercises specifically). The four-class definitions here are written for individual
track and field events; a football skill (e.g., a pass rush) is a much less discrete,
single "competitive exercise" than a 100m sprint or a hammer throw, so classifying a given
drill as SPP vs. SDE for football is Inference, not something Bondarchuk's glossary
addresses.

**Example** — For a shot putter: general preparatory = general conditioning running;
specialized-preparatory = barbell snatch, half squat (same leg-extensor muscle groups,
different movement); specialized-developmental = throwing a lighter or heavier shot
(repeats the throw's parts under altered load); competitive = throwing the 7.26kg
competition shot in a meet.

# "Current" vs. "Distant" Transfer: When in a Training Block the Earlier Work Actually Pays Off

**What it is** — Bondarchuk proposes that when a training cycle is built in successive
stages/blocks, the achieved level from a previous stage can transfer to the following
stage in one of three ways (PDF pp. 35-38, printed pp. 18-21):
1. **Transfer at the start** of the next stage/block — the previous stage's result can
   then be allowed to decrease, increase, or hold, because it already did its job of
   creating the "base."
2. **Transfer in the middle** of the next stage/block — the previous level must be
   actively maintained (not allowed to drop) while the next stage's exercises are
   developed, using reduced-volume maintenance work.
3. **Transfer at the end** of the next stage/block — the previous stage's level must be
   held all the way through.

He then names two broader modes across an entire multi-stage cycle: **"distant" transfer**
(entering sports form in one exercise class first, other classes only later — harder to
manage because you must simultaneously maintain the earlier gains and build the later
ones) versus **"current" (flowing) transfer** (all exercise classes reach sports form
together, because a consistent, unbroken mix of general/specialized-preparatory/
specialized-developmental/competitive exercises runs throughout every stage rather than
being introduced and dropped stage by stage). Current transfer is associated with
variational, combinational, and complex methods of cycle construction; distant transfer
with the stricter stage/block methods. (PDF pp. 41-42, 66-67, printed pp. 24-25, 49-50.)
He also states that excluding specialized-developmental and competitive exercises for more
than 3-4 weeks under the variational method causes a real loss of the previously achieved
level. (PDF p. 38, printed p. 21.)

**Why it works** — No physiological mechanism is offered; this is a purely descriptive,
practice-derived model the author says he arrived at by observing which cycle-construction
methods succeeded ("we came to the conclusion that..."), with no cited independent
verification or controlled comparison in the excerpted text. **Inference:** the "current
transfer" mode maps loosely onto what Western periodization literature calls concurrent or
conjugate training (train all qualities across the whole cycle, in varying proportions)
versus "distant transfer" mapping onto strict block/linear periodization (sequential
single-quality phases) — a connection this passage does not make itself.

**When to apply** — Use as a lens for diagnosing a specific periodization complaint: if
an athlete's speed or strength level noticeably drops during a technical or in-season
phase, ask whether the program is using a "distant transfer" model (which requires
active, planned maintenance work for the earlier-built quality) without actually
including that maintenance work. **Eric's baseline:** in-season programming at Baylor
follows "maintain intensity, reduce volume" rather than continuing to build strength
(`method-baseline.md`, "Strength and power," citing
`01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md pp.20-21`) — that is functionally the "transfer in
the middle" case above (actively holding a previously-built level while a new emphasis
develops), though the course material does not use Bondarchuk's terminology.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is untested.** This is presented in the text as the
author's own unverified conclusion from practical observation, not tested against a
control condition or independently replicated, and the book gives no citation trail for
it at all (unusual for this chapter, which otherwise cites bracketed references
throughout). Treat this as a plausible practitioner heuristic, not an established finding.
Population is elite Soviet track and field athletes on multi-decade national training
cycles; applying the three-situations timing model to a much shorter college football
in-season/off-season calendar is Inference.

**Example** — A discus thrower doing a strength-emphasis GPP block followed by a
technical/competition block: under "current transfer," strength work continues at reduced
volume throughout the technical block so the strength level is still rising (or at least
held) when the athlete peaks technically; under "distant transfer" with poor execution,
strength work is dropped entirely once the technical block starts and the athlete arrives
at competition weaker than at the end of GPP.

the companion finding on how stage duration interacts with whether transfer can happen at
all.

# Two Test Families: "General" Tests Shared Across Events, "Specialized" Tests Unique to Each

**What it is** — Bondarchuk splits the tests used to study training transfer into two
families (PDF pp. 68-69, printed pp. 51-52):
- **General tests**: not tests of general-preparatory exercises specifically, but tests
  "used in mixed groups or between groups" — i.e., shared across many events. For
  heart-circulatory/respiratory function, nearly identical tests are used across all of
  track and field (max oxygen uptake on a veloergometer, time-to-exhaustion at max VO2,
  anaerobic threshold). For speed-strength sports, common general tests are the 60m run
  from blocks, barbell snatch, half squat with barbell, standing long jump, vertical jump
  (Abalakov method), triple jump from place, and forward/backward shot throws.
- **Specialized tests**: tests unique to each event's specific demands, most reliably
  results in **specialized-developmental exercises** — i.e., the competitive movement
  itself executed under lighter or more difficult conditions (lighter/heavier implements
  in throws, shorter approach runs in jumps, varied segment/rest combinations in cyclical
  events). He states specialized-developmental test reliability is the best-established
  of the test types; general-preparatory and specialized-preparatory test reliability is
  explicitly flagged as "not fully confirmed" and something to be "tightened up in the
  near future." (PDF p. 69, printed p. 52.)

**Why it works** — The text itself gives no mechanism connecting these two ideas; it simply
asserts that specialized-developmental test reliability "has already been shown" without
citing why. **Inference:** connecting this to
[[framework--transfer-chain-general-to-competitive]] — specialized-developmental exercises
sit closest to the competitive event in that transfer chain, so tests built from those same
exercises should track competitive readiness more reliably than tests built
from more general exercises further from the competitive movement.

**When to apply** — When designing or auditing a testing battery, prioritize tests that
are themselves specialized-developmental-class movements (the competitive skill under
altered load/distance conditions) over generic strength/power tests when the goal is
tracking competitive readiness specifically; reserve the generic tests (snatch, squat,
vertical jump, etc.) for tracking general physical qualities, which the author himself
says have less-confirmed reliability as *transfer* indicators even though they are
common practice.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is supported.** This is the author's own
methodological framing for the correlational study that follows in Chapter 2, presented
without a formal reliability study of his own two test families in the excerpted pages
(he cites "already been shown" reliability for specialized-developmental tests, but gives
no reference number here). Population is Soviet track and field testing practice of the
1970s-80s; the specific listed general tests (barbell snatch, Abalakov jump, etc.) reflect
that era's tools, not necessarily current force-plate/GPS-based options. Applying the
general/specialized test distinction to Baylor's own battery design (`method-baseline.md`,
"Testing and assessment") is Inference in terms of which specific tests to pick, though
the underlying logic (prefer tests closest to the competitive movement for readiness
tracking) is directly portable.

**Example** — For a football wide receiver, a "specialized" test in this framework would
be a competitive-adjacent movement under altered conditions (e.g., a route run against a
lighter or heavier resistance band, or a shortened-approach vertical jump), while a
"general" test would be a standard vertical jump or broad jump shared across every
position group.

this test-selection framework is built on.

# Three Competing Theories of Training Transfer: Specifist, Generalist, Holistic

**What it is** — Bondarchuk frames the entire theory of training transfer as a debate
between three schools (PDF pp. 20-28, printed pp. 3-11):
- **Specifists** (traced to E.L. Thorndike's "theory of identical elements," cited as
  ref [223]): transfer between two activities happens only to the degree the activities
  share identical elements (muscle groups, coordination structure, biomechanics). No
  shared elements, no transfer. (PDF p. 20, printed p. 3.)
- **Generalists** (dominant 1940-1960, per the text): argue for the "unity of general and
  specialized preparation" — build a broad general base first (a multi-year "base stage"
  of 4-6 years), then specialize. Bondarchuk reports this view was later revised once
  research showed general-preparation means only build a base for **low-level** athletes;
  for high-level athletes, transfer to the competitive event comes only through
  specialized-preparation means (citing refs [34, 57, 182]). (PDF pp. 22-24, printed pp.
  5-7.)
- **Holistics**: the body acts as one system, so any exercise affects the whole body's
  systems to some degree (non-specific reactions of the heart-circulatory/respiratory
  systems from any cyclical exercise, e.g. rowing or cycling helping a middle-distance
  runner's aerobic base). Bondarchuk explicitly rejects the holistics' full conclusion
  ("it is not possible to agree with the conclusions of the holistics") but keeps their
  narrower point about non-specific systemic reactions, and credits the "block method"
  (physical-preparation block before a technical-preparation block) as the holistics'
  practical answer to sequencing. (PDF pp. 26-28, printed pp. 9-11.)

**Why it works** — The author does not resolve these into a single mechanism in this
section; he uses the three-way split to set up why transfer is contested territory before
he presents his own correlational data in Chapter 2. **Inference:** the specifist view is
the closest ancestor of what this library already holds as
[[principle--specificity-of-training-ten-dimensions]] (Supertraining, Verkhoshansky/Siff)
and of Verkhoshansky's Principle of Dynamic Correspondence taught in HHPR 5355
(`method-baseline.md`, "Strength and power"); the generalist/holistic debate maps onto
the general-preparation-vs-specialization sequencing question this library already tracks
via Bompa's multilateral development model (vault, not yet cross-checked in this batch).

**When to apply** — Use this three-way split as a diagnostic when reading any other
S&C source's claim about "general" vs "specific" training: ask which of the three
positions it is implicitly taking. **Eric's baseline:** HHPR 5355 teaches exercise
selection on a spectrum from general preparation through specific preparation to
competitive exercises (`method-baseline.md`, "Strength and power," citing
`01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md pp.13-16`) — that is functionally a generalist-then-
specifist sequencing, not a pure specifist position, and this entry gives Eric's course
material a named theoretical lineage (Thorndike/specifist vs. the generalist sequencing
debate) that the transcript itself does not cite.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is supported, not proven.** This is Bondarchuk's own
literature synthesis of a decades-old Soviet-era academic debate among track and field
scientists, not an independently replicated finding, and the book gives no citation for
exactly how "generalist" views were falsified beyond two bracketed reference numbers
([34, 57, 182]) this ingest did not chase down. The population behind all three positions
is elite Soviet track and field athletes; extending any of the three views to football
programming (e.g., "GPP only helps low-level players") is Inference, not something this
passage states.

**Example** — A coach who says "just build a big general base before specializing" is
taking the classical generalist position Bondarchuk says was already revised by the
1970s-80s once high-level athlete data showed general work stops producing transfer past
a certain qualification level; see
[[claim--sprint-strength-exercise-transfer-ceiling]] for the Chapter 2 data behind that
revision.

staging) — the specifist position's closest modern-book relative in this library.

# Transfer Runs in a Chain: General to Specialized-Preparatory to Specialized-Developmental to Competitive

**What it is** — Bondarchuk describes training transfer as flowing through an ordered
chain of exercise classes rather than any exercise being able to transfer to any other:
"first is the influence of general developmental exercises on specialized-preparatory,
specialized-developmental, or competitive exercises. Second, is the effect of
specialized-preparatory exercises on specialized-developmental and competitive exercises.
Third are training questions on the effect of specialized-developmental exercises on the
competitive event." (PDF p. 33, printed p. 16.) He also notes transfer holds "within the
limits of one exercise" across joint angles or conditions (training leg extensors at one
joint angle transfers to other angles; throwing the full implement transfers to throwing
from a standing position; jumps with a full run-up transfer to short-run-up speed), and
"within the confines of whole motor actions" across related competitive events (an 800m
runner's training affects 400m and 1500m results). (PDF pp. 33-34, printed pp. 16-17.)

**Why it works** — No mechanism is given beyond the chain's own logic: exercises closer
to the competitive movement (in the classification given in
[[framework--bondarchuk-four-exercise-classes]]) share more of the competitive event's
"identical elements" (see the specifist position in
[[framework--three-viewpoints-on-training-transfer]]), so influence should flow
"downstream" toward more specific exercises more reliably than "upstream." **Inference:**
this is the theoretical basis for a common practical rule — sequence a training block from
general to specific — but the author does not explicitly rule out upstream transfer here,
only describes the direction he studied.

**When to apply** — Use this as the reasoning behind block sequencing (general
preparatory before specialized-preparatory before specialized-developmental before
competitive) rather than treating "sequence general to specific" as an arbitrary
convention. When auditing a program, check whether specialized-developmental and
competitive exercises are present at all — per this chain, they are the only exercise
classes with a direct claim on transferring to the competitive result.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is supported.** This is Bondarchuk's own descriptive
model built from the same single Soviet track and field dataset referenced throughout the
book, not an independently tested causal chain. It describes track and field individual
events; a football position group's skill (e.g., pass protection) is a much more complex,
multi-factor competitive action than a single-event track result, so directly re-applying
"general → specialized-preparatory → specialized-developmental → competitive" sequencing
to a football skill is Inference, reasonable by analogy but untested here.

**Example** — In sprinting, this chain implies: general conditioning (GPE) affects hill
sprints or resisted sprints (SPE) affects flying-start segments or overspeed towing
(SDE) affects the competitive 100m/40-yard result (CE) — each link transferring more
reliably than trying to jump straight from GPE to CE.

**Related** — [[framework--bondarchuk-four-exercise-classes]] — the classification this
chain is built on. [[claim--sprint-jump-exercises-most-transferable-assistance]] — Chapter
2's empirical test of which specific exercises actually sit closest to the competitive
end of this chain for sprinters.

# The Principle of Conjugated Training Effects: Physical Preparation and Technique Must Coincide

**What it is** — Physical-ability development and technical-mastery development should
be trained so they "coincide and correspond to the competitive exercise according to
their basic characteristics" rather than as separate tracks. Bondarchuk attributes the
principle to Soviet head coach/scientist V.M. Dyachkov in the 1960s (ref [81]), and notes
it was later "modified by other specialists who called it dynamic correspondence and the
principle of resolving motor actions," citing U.V. Menkhin (ref [157]) specifically —
**not Verkhoshansky** — for that renaming. (PDF p. 29, printed p. 12.) Using gymnastics as
Menkhin's example, the best conjugation requires five things at once: (1) motor qualities
localized to the right muscle groups, (2) motor qualities matching the biodynamic demands
of the skill, (3) transfer into the most common forms of motor activity used for learning
technique, (4) enough work capacity to repeat the skill reliably across a long
competition, and (5) transfer into the competitive combination itself. (PDF p. 29,
printed p. 12.) Bondarchuk says he used this same conjugate principle to build his own
"principle of all-round specialized preparation" (ref [265]): mostly specialized-
preparatory and specialized-developmental exercises, general preparation only for warm-up
and restoration, with each training cycle needing "new" and progressively stronger
special exercises to keep producing adaptation. (PDF p. 30, printed p. 13.)

**Why it works** — The author gives no explicit physiological mechanism here beyond the
form-and-content unity idea inherited from the holistics critique in the prior section
(see [[framework--three-viewpoints-on-training-transfer]]): training physical qualities
and technique as artificially separate tracks is, in his view, a "theoretical and
practical error" because they are "united in all of their displays." **Inference:** this
reads as an early, narrower cousin of the ten-dimension specificity logic in
[[principle--specificity-of-training-ten-dimensions]] (Supertraining) — both argue that an
exercise's value depends on matching several specific parameters to the target skill, not
just training "strength" or "technique" in isolation.

**When to apply** — Use as a check when a program trains strength/power work and skill
work in completely separate blocks with no attempt to match them: ask whether the
strength work is being selected (per Menkhin's five criteria) to actually support the
target skill's muscle groups, biodynamics, and movement pattern, not just "get stronger in
general." **Eric's baseline:** the Principle of Dynamic Correspondence taught in HHPR 5355
is credited there to Verkhoshansky, not Dyachkov/Menkhin (`method-baseline.md`, "Strength
and power," citing `01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md p.14`; `week1-lecture.md p.34`).
**This is a real lineage discrepancy worth flagging, not resolving here**: Bondarchuk's
own text credits the "dynamic correspondence" *name* to Menkhin's modification of
Dyachkov's conjugate principle, while Eric's course credits the *concept* to
Verkhoshansky. These may be the same idea moving through multiple Soviet-era authors who
each get cited differently in different secondary sources, or they may be distinct
formulations that later got merged under one name in Western coaching literature. Check
Supertraining's own Chapter 4.3 (not yet ingested; see `_book.md` in the Supertraining
staging folder) before treating "dynamic correspondence" as a single-author concept in any
future entry.

**When NOT to apply** — **Confidence is supported, not proven.** This is Bondarchuk's own
reading of Dyachkov's and Menkhin's Soviet-era proposals, cited only by bracketed
reference number, not independently verified in this ingest. The five-component
gymnastics example is Menkhin's, imported by Bondarchuk into track and field without
restating it for that context; applying it to a specific football exercise-selection
decision is Inference. Population throughout is elite Soviet gymnasts (Menkhin's own
example) and track and field athletes (Bondarchuk's extension), not football or other
team-sport athletes.

**Example** — **Inference:** a football strength coach picking a trap-bar deadlift
variation for a linebacker should, per this principle, be asking not just "does this build
leg strength" but whether the pull's force direction, joint angles, and muscle-work regime
actually match the demands of a linebacker's competitive movement — the same "coincide and
correspond" test Bondarchuk describes for track and field exercise selection.

staging) — cites Verkhoshansky's Dynamic Correspondence by name; flag the Dyachkov/Menkhin
vs. Verkhoshansky attribution question there too if that entry is revisited.
[[framework--three-viewpoints-on-training-transfer]] — the holistics' form-and-content
