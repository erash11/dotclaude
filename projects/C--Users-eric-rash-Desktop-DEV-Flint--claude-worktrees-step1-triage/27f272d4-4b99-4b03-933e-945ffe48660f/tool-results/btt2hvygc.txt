# HSR/VHSR Delivery Shifts: Structured Repeats, Then Short Intervals, Then Small-Sided Games

**What it is** — Across the case studies, high-speed running (HSR/VHSR) volume is not just dosed up session to session, it is delivered through three successive formats as rehab advances. Early sessions accumulate HSR through structured, individually-prescribed repeats over a fixed distance (e.g. 100 m and 60 m repeats). From around mid-rehab (session 5 in Case 1, session 3 in Case 2), HSR shifts to short interval formats: "10 seconds on, 10 seconds off, or 15 seconds on and off" (Case 1, ~10:17-10:35), and "short interval, high intensity interval training formats, like 10 seconds on, 10 seconds off, 20 seconds on, 20 seconds off" (Case 2, ~23:14-23:22). From around session 7-8, small-sided games and technical/tactical drills start to contribute HSR/VHSR "a little bit more meaningfully" or "more organically," alongside whatever prescribed running work continues (Case 1, ~10:35-10:46; Case 2, ~23:25-23:30). A similar shift to small-sided games shows up in Case 4, a knee-joint case kept straight-line-only through session 6 for bracing reasons: "small-sided games, so modified rehab specific drills and also partial participation with the team" began at session 8 (~41:34-41:40). Harries reports a separate, linear VHSR/sprint increase from sessions 4-8 in the same passage (~41:43-41:46) without explicitly linking the two statements, so this case supports the format shift itself but not a stated causal link to VHSR volume.

**Why it works** — Harries does not give an explicit mechanism for why the delivery format itself should shift (his only nearby general statement is that session structure "could be employed slightly earlier or held slightly later, depending on how the athlete's responding," said of the mid-rehab checkpoint, not this format shift specifically, ~20:08-20:14). Inference (entry writer's): moving from individually-prescribed, fixed-distance repeats to short intervals to game-based drills lets the athlete accumulate the same category of high-speed exposure in progressively less predictable, more sport-representative contexts, without needing to represcribe every rep once basic mechanics and pacing are established.

**When to apply** — Use as the default organizing structure for programming HSR/VHSR volume across an on-field rehab block, once basic linear mechanics and pacing are established (roughly Level 1-2, see [[framework--on-field-rehab-levels-with-mv-bands]]). Eric's baseline: `method-baseline.md` records that Baylor has no documented phased RTP protocol of its own yet; this three-stage shift is a directly transferable structure to pair with Baylor's own GPS velocity-band thresholds.

**When NOT to apply** — Harries never states this as a fixed session-count rule; the session numbers cited here (5, 3, 7, 8) are case-specific, not a stated formula, and the source's own repeated caveat is that the framework is a guide adjusted to context, not a step-by-step protocol (see [[principle--framework-is-a-guide-not-a-protocol]]). The Case 4 example also shows the shift can run alongside other, unrelated restrictions (change-of-direction was still withheld through session 6 there for bracing reasons); do not treat "small-sided games by session 7-8" as evidence that all restrictions lift on that same timeline. Do not import a specific case's session numbers as a prescription.

**Example** — In [[case-study--semimembranosus-adductor-strain-case-2-afl]], HSR moved from 100 m repeats (session 2, which overshot the low 17 km/h threshold, see [[heuristic--low-speed-threshold-inflates-hsr-totals]]) to short intervals (session 3) to small-sided games contributing HSR organically (session 8).

# Fitting Rehab Sessions Into the Team's Own Training Week

**What it is** — As a returning athlete moves from an isolated running-only schedule into overlapping with team training, Harries shapes each week of rehab around the team's own weekly training rhythm, not a fixed rehab-only calendar. Case 2: "by week three, we started to fall into the team's training structure a bit more" (~17:40-17:44): a small on-field rehab session early in the week, a return to partial training midweek, then "a larger combined rehab session with modified team training" at week's end (~17:45-17:56). Case 2, week 4: two non-consecutive training days then two consecutive days, the second-to-last being "a captain's run walkthrough style session, though very low intensity," followed by return to play in a match (~17:56-18:10). Case 1, week 3: "a high moderate high structure, including partial training exposure in the midweek, and a heavier modified training session on the weekend" (~05:18-05:27). Case 1, week 4: "one light partial session, a full training midweek, and a walkthrough style session... And then they played their game at the end of the week" (~05:27-05:37). Case 4 (knee), week 5: running frequency was deliberately reduced to two larger-load sessions "in order to make a more significant volume increase in those individual sessions," explicitly to bridge into the larger session planned at the end of week 6 (~37:24-37:46). Case 4, week 7 (the RTP week): "a small session early in the week, a moderate session mid-week, which was aligning to the team's normal training schedule in the in-season," then return to play at week's end (~38:13-38:26).

**Why it works** — For the Case 4 frequency cut, Harries states the reason directly: reducing running frequency let him "make a more significant volume increase in those individual sessions" so there was "a nice little progression into" the larger, match-comparable session planned for the following week (~37:26-37:55). For the broader practice of shaping rehab-week structure around the team's rhythm, his only stated reason is the week-7 mid-week session "aligning to the team's normal training schedule in the in-season" (~38:20-38:22); he gives no further explicit rationale for the general practice. Inference (entry writer's): tying rehab-session load to the team's own hard/light day pattern lets the returning athlete's tolerance build in step with what full training will actually demand in the week he returns, rather than on a schedule disconnected from it.

**When to apply** — When designing a returning athlete's week once he is off an isolated running-only schedule and beginning to overlap with team sessions (typically mid-to-late rehab, alongside modified/partial training participation). Eric's review: Applied Performance "already undulate[s] load across the team week," and this pattern is proposed as "the same logic applied to one returning player, anchored to the team's Tue/Wed high, Thu low pattern" (Review and Plan, line 75) - i.e. the existing team-week undulation is the natural anchor for this on-field-rehab pattern, not a separate structure to invent.

**When NOT to apply** — Harries never states this as a fixed template; no claim is made that a "high-moderate-high" week or a captain's-run walkthrough is required, and the specific weekly shapes differ across all three cases that show them, tracking each athlete's own timeline and injury type. None of it is validated against outcome data beyond what happened in each case being reported without a stated recurrence. Do not import a specific case's weekly pattern wholesale as a template; use the underlying idea, build the rehab week around the team's actual week, and rebuild the specific days for each returning athlete.

**Example** — See [[case-study--mcl-sprain-meniscal-case-4-afl]], weeks 5-7, for the clearest stated rationale: a frequency cut used as a deliberate bridge to a larger session, and an RTP week deliberately matched to the team's normal in-season training rhythm.

**What it is** — Harries gives a calendar anchor, matched to force-production, reactive-strength and healing-time criteria that line up with the general 75-85% MV gate (see [[heuristic--75-85-percent-mv-decision-gate]]), for hamstring-type strain injuries specifically. Describing session 5 (stated as "identical to session five in case one," ~19:47-19:56), he says it "probably represents a typical mid stage max velocity progression for this type of injury at about the 10 [to] 14 day post injury mark" (~19:56-20:04) and, discussing the same session a few lines later, that it "typifies the point in rehab where you push towards around about 85% max velocity at that day 14 type mark, plus or minus, but rarely would you go beyond that. # Broken-Stride Timing: Time Only the Middle Phase

**What it is** — Once an athlete progresses beyond ~70-75% of MV, Harries moves from full-distance timed repeats to a "broken stride": a run divided into an acceleration phase, a speed-maintenance phase and a deceleration phase, e.g. 60 m split into 20 m / 20 m / 20 m (adjustable, e.g. 15/10/15 to demand a more aggressive acceleration into the same target speed) (~17:01-18:20). Critically, he times only the middle (speed-maintenance) phase, not the whole distance, because that isolates the actual target speed from the acceleration and deceleration mechanics around it. Worked example: hitting 2.88-3.0 seconds across the middle phase equates to ~24-25 km/h, which is ~70-75% of that athlete's MV (~18:29-18:51); the next progression steps require 2.77-2.88 seconds through the same phase, pushing speed up roughly 1 km/h more. He prefers a stopwatch for immediate feedback, using GPS/video as a secondary confirmation of peak velocity, not the primary control method (~18:51-19:17).

**Why it works** — Inference (entry writer's rationale, not stated by Harries this way): timing the whole distance conflates three different physical demands (accelerating, holding speed, decelerating) into one number, so it can't precisely control what speed was actually reached in the middle. Harries' own stated reason is narrower: the middle-phase time is set "to equate to a given speed through that middle distance... that's going to correspond to the athlete's max velocity" (~18:18-18:29) - i.e. it gives a direct, controllable readout of the target velocity.

**When to apply** — Use from ~70-75% MV (Level 3) onward, once an athlete moves "beyond around about this 70 to 75% of max velocity" (~17:01-17:07), as the standard method for prescribing and verifying max-velocity exposures. At later levels, continue shortening the acceleration-phase distance (while keeping the target maintenance speed) to increase acceleration magnitude, and build in fatiguing conditions before high-%MV maintenance-phase reps.

**When NOT to apply** — Harries explicitly becomes "far less rigid" with these time targets in later rehab stages. He does not replace the target time with a bandwidth; he adds a percentage bandwidth in addition to the target completion time, "albeit with a ceiling on what is an acceptable progression for that session" (~20:30-20:48), because forcing a fixed time can push an athlete faster than they are comfortable with or unnecessarily hold them back; he pairs each rep with a conversation (how did that feel, are you comfortable) rather than treating the stopwatch number as absolute (~20:17-20:48). Do not apply this as a rigid pass/fail number in late-stage rehab without that check-in, and do not drop the target time itself once the bandwidth is added.
