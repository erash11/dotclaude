## Lessons from the Supertraining Chapter 1 review (2026-09-26: 30 FIX, 1 NO-GO of 41)

- **Keep the source's internal contradictions.** When a book says X on one page and not-X on another, record both with their pages and set `contested`. Never pick one side silently. The NO-GO entry reversed the book's SJ-CMJ strength-deficit rule because the book states it both ways.
- **Name the right voice.** In co-authored books, write "the authors" unless a passage clearly belongs to one author. A passage that names Verkhoshansky in the third person is Siff writing.
- **Keep `proven` rare and scoped.** Scope it to the replicated core (for example, "Hill's force-velocity curve"), never the whole entry, and mark any disputed sentence inside it. Four of eight `proven` ratings were lowered.
- **No mechanism the source does not give.** About a dozen "Why it works" sections supplied physiology the book never states. Label it "Inference:" or leave it out.
- **Do not add "well established" or "modern research shows" without a citation.** Name the paper, or delete the phrase.

## Lessons from the 2026-09-27 review wave (Supertraining Ch2-3, Issurin Ch3, Bondarchuk, Evans, Old Bull)

## Evidence

- Never invent a number. Keep ambiguity visible: mark unclear audio or OCR as unclear, and do not harmonize two conflicting numbers from the source; record both with their locators.
- OCR sources (`staging/ocr/*.md`): pages listed as low_confidence in the header must be checked against the page image before any number from them is cited. Use the Read tool on the PDF page, or render it.
- **OCR confidence scores miss the worst errors** (checked 2026-09-27). Zatsiorsky 2e: the OCR interleaves the two columns and the sidebars line by line, so its text is not readable prose; use it only to find content, and read and quote from the page image. *CFTS*: single-column prose that mostly reads in order, but upside-down lines come out as gibberish, some lines are dropped, and figures are scrambled, all at "0 low-confidence pages". For any OCR source, quote only after checking the page image, and read every figure and table from the image.

Soviet-era or single-lab empirical findings and training prescriptions: `supported` at most. `untested` for single cases and anecdotes. `contested` where another library source or modern evidence disagrees (say which).

## Missing ideas (covered pages, no entry)

1. **ES safety rules and contraindications** (idx 225 / p.214). "Under no circumstances should MACS be applied across the brain, as it can cause serious harm." No form of ES is advisable for epileptics, pregnant women, cardiac patients or pacemaker users. MICS is "generally entirely safe" anywhere. This belongs in the safety heuristic, attributed to the authors (see batch item E).
2. **ES as restoration more than stressor** (idx 225 / p.214). ES "is generally of greater importance [for restoration] than as a means of stressing the muscles", using very-low-current devices at 0.5-8 Hz locally, transcranially or at acupuncture points (Siff & Yessis 1992). The counterweight (idx 227 / p.216) is that routine accelerated restoration "can impair the natural ability of the body to recuperate and adapt". Contrast: vault `dupuy-2018` (ES did not reduce DOMS or fatigue).
3. **Resistance for speed is mainly for novices** (idx 231 / p.220). Increasing movement speed through resistance exercise "is primarily for novice athletes ... Initially a positive influence, it becomes negative". Exercises with weights are "not necessarily adequate for developing speed of movement ... especially for highly qualified athletes". Speed-strength against significant resistance is useful only "in certain types of muscular work, at a reasonable volume, at specific stages of training". Check for overlap with the vault `framework--strength-speed-continuum-five-zones` before writing.
4. **Loaded depth jumps and the independent training role of the amortisation phase** (idx 233-234 / p.222-223, Fig 4.9). Adding 10-40 kg to a depth jump lowers rebound height, force and RC and lengthens contact time. Reflexive tension during amortisation "enhances the ability of the muscles to switch quickly to the active state", and experience says it is not detrimental, "although further research is required". Plyometrics was "originally called the 'shock method'", with a corroborating citation list and a closing note that more work is needed (p.223). Check against the existing `framework--shock-method-and-plyometric-terminology`.
5. **Strength vs speed-strength regimes differ physiologically** (idx 227 / p.216). Strength exercises use "the largest resistance possible", and speed-strength exercises use a resistance "whose optimal magnitude is determined by the required speed of movement" (Farfel 1940). Explosive movements need high neural excitation, while slow movements need excitation that is sustained (Federov 1957). The section also opens with the DeLorme progressive-resistance lineage.
