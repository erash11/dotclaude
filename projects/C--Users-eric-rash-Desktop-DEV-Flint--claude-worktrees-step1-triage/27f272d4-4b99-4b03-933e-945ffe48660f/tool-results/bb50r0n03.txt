# Power Output Peaks and Declines With Load in a Barbell Jump, but Falls Steadily in a Leg-Press, Because of Elastic Energy

**What it is** — As barbell load increases, work increases, but power output is not
unidirectional: for a jump with a barbell on the shoulders, power output initially
increases, then, once barbell weight exceeds about 66% of maximum, it begins to decrease
(Chikvadze, 1961). Maximum power output for this jump is achieved with weights of 30-40% of
1RM, and maximal reactivity coefficient at 30-33% of 1RM. Verified against the page image
(Fig 4.2, printed p. 217): as load rises from 14% to 86% of max strength, maximal force
(Fmax) and movement time both rise, while power and the reactivity coefficient (R) show a
non-monotonic pattern across the load range rather than rising or falling steadily
throughout. The authors attribute the rise in power and reactivity coefficient with
increasing movement time to "additional elastic energy accumulated in the muscles by
absorbing the kinetic energy of the body and apparatus during the amortisation phase." A
contrasting experiment removes that elastic-energy source: seated leg extensions from a
110-degree initial knee angle, lifting 20/40/60/80% of maximum concentrically with no
countermovement or amortisation phase. Verified against the page image (Fig 4.3, printed p.
217): here, maximal force and movement time again increase with resistance, "analogous to
jumps with a barbell," but power output decreases progressively across the whole load range
instead of peaking mid-range, "because the extra elastic energy is lacking in this case."
(PDF pp. 216-217, printed pp. 216-217, "4.2.2 Resistance and Strength Training," Figs 4.2-4.3.)

**Why it works** — The authors' stated mechanism is the presence or absence of an
amortisation (countermovement/stretch-shortening) phase that stores and releases elastic
energy: the jump-with-barbell condition includes this phase, so at moderate loads the added
elastic-energy return can boost power and reactivity even as load rises, up to the point
(about 66% of max) where added weight overwhelms that benefit; the seated leg-extension
condition has no comparable amortisation phase, so power falls steadily as soon as
resistance climbs, since only added resistance (not stored elastic energy) is changing.

**When to apply** — Use this as a caution when generalising a load-power relationship from
one exercise type to another: a countermovement or stretch-shortening exercise (jump,
countermovement press) can show power peaking at a specific submaximal load band (here,
30-40% of 1RM for maximal power, 30-33% for maximal reactivity), while a purely concentric,
no-amortisation exercise (seated leg extension here) shows power declining steadily as load
rises, with no such peak. Inference: this is consistent with why "optimal power load"
testing protocols (e.g. jump squats across a load spectrum) find a load band that maximizes
power, while purely concentric single-joint machine exercises typically do not show the same
peak.

**When NOT to apply** — This is single-lab, era-specific data (Chikvadze, 1961; Siff &
Verkhoshansky, 1999 figure redraws) on unspecified subjects; the specific percentages
(66% threshold, 30-40% for max power, 30-33% for max reactivity) should be treated as
illustrative for this particular jump-with-barbell protocol, not as universal constants for
all jumping or pressing exercises. Do not assume every loaded jump variation shows peak
power at exactly 30-40% of 1RM; load-power curves are known to vary by exercise and by
population in the modern literature, which this passage does not address.

**Example** — Inference: a coach programming loaded jump squats for maximal power output
should expect the power curve to rise then fall across the load spectrum (consistent with
Fig 4.2's pattern), and should not extrapolate that same rise-then-fall shape to a
non-countermovement exercise like a seated leg press or leg extension machine, where this
entry's Fig 4.3 data show power declining steadily as load increases instead.

# Inertial (Flywheel) and Elastic Resistance: Different Force Profiles From Free Weights, Each With Its Own Limitation

**What it is** — The authors note that specialised equipment for training against a load's
inertia (rather than its weight) is rarely used, despite the concept warranting more serious
attention. Two solutions are given: applying force against a horizontal wheel or a
suspended, pendulum-like load, or spinning a heavy flywheel ergometer with the arms or legs
(Fig 4.4, elbow-flexion flywheel device, Verkhoshansky 1977). A flywheel device's moment of
inertia can be varied to change the speed-of-contraction demand, and it can be used several
ways: accelerating against high inertia, using the wheel's momentum to compel a certain
movement cadence, or opposing the wheel's angular momentum via eccentric muscle action to
decelerate it, with these methods combinable in short or mixed bursts. Elastic bands are
treated as a separate special case: resistance follows Hooke's Law (F = kx, where k is the
spring constant and x is extension), so elastic force starts at zero and rises with range of
movement; the authors state plainly that "elastic materials should not be used for
developing starting-strength in ballistic movements or for developing explosive-strength,"
because those qualities need force available from the very start of the movement, which
elastics cannot supply. For strength-endurance over a selected range, however, an elastic
band's length and elasticity can be chosen so its resistance changes minimally over the
working range, a method used by swimmers to develop pulling strength-endurance (Fig 4.5,
Verkhoshansky 1977). Resistance at "sticking points" can be reduced by moving toward the
band's attachment point, or increased by moving away from it; resistance can also be varied
across the full range by accelerating free weights through a decreasing-resistance portion
of the lift (so-called Compensatory Acceleration Training, see
[[framework--compensatory-acceleration-training]]) or by using dedicated variable-resistance
machines. (PDF pp. 219-220, printed pp. 219-220, "4.2.2 Resistance and Strength Training,"
Figs 4.4-4.5.)

**Why it works** — For flywheel/inertial devices, the mechanism is that resistance is
determined by the load's inertia (F = ma) rather than its weight (F = m(g+a)), so the
athlete can train speed of contraction against a resistance that does not exist at rest, only
once acceleration is applied, unlike a gravity-loaded free weight. For elastic bands, the
mechanism is Hooke's Law itself: resistance is proportional to extension, so it is
inherently near-zero at the start of the range and maximal at the end, the opposite loading
curve from what ballistic and explosive-strength work typically needs (high force available
immediately).

**When to apply** — Use flywheel/inertial devices when the training goal is speed of
muscular contraction against inertia specifically (per
[[framework--two-force-regimes-overcoming-weight-vs-inertia]]), varying moment of inertia to
adjust the demand, and consider eccentric-deceleration use of the same device for eccentric
overload. Use elastic bands specifically for strength-endurance work over a selected range
(the authors' cited example: swimmers' pulling strength-endurance), choosing band length and
elasticity to minimise resistance variation across that range, and use position along the
band (closer to or farther from the attachment) to manage sticking-point loading.

**When NOT to apply** — Do not use elastic-band resistance alone for developing
starting-strength or explosive-strength in ballistic movements; the authors state this
directly, since Hooke's Law resistance is lowest exactly when ballistic/explosive movements
most need force to be available. The flywheel and pendulum devices described are
purpose-built or improvised apparatus (a 1992 University of the Witwatersrand student
biomechanics project supplied the plyometric bench-press device shown nearby, Fig 4.6, a
related but separate kinetic-energy device); the authors give no standardised commercial
specification for the flywheel or pendulum devices described here.

**Example** — Inference: a swim coach building pulling strength-endurance with resistance
bands, per the authors' own cited example, should select a band whose resistance changes
little across the stroke's working range, rather than a stiffer band whose resistance would
spike sharply near full extension; a sprint or jump coach chasing starting-strength or
explosive-strength should not substitute elastic-band resistance for free weights or
inertial-device training for that specific quality.

[[framework--compensatory-acceleration-training]].

# Two Ways Resistance Exercises Develop Force: Against a Load's Weight, or Against Its Inertia

**What it is** — The authors distinguish resistance exercises by two different bases: first,
by whether the working force is developed after preliminary muscular tension already equal
to the apparatus's weight (e.g. the starting position of a squat or squat jump, where the
legs and torso have already developed tension equal to the barbell's weight before the
dynamic effort begins), or whether force is developed essentially from zero without
appreciable preliminary tension (e.g. the snatch or clean-and-jerk, where the working force
accelerating the bar starts from near zero). The first group does not appreciably influence
the excitation-tension processes in the muscle and, depending on the weight used, chiefly
develops either muscular strength or speed of contraction, but not the speed of switching
from rest to active state; the second group has "simultaneous potential for developing
dynamic strength, speed of movement and especially starting strength." Second, and
separately, the authors classify exercises by which physical quantity the force is directed
against: force against the weight of a load (F = m(g+a), determined by the load's mass and
its acceleration against gravity, e.g. lifting a barbell), or force against the inertia of a
load (F = ma, dependent only on the load's inertial force at a given acceleration, e.g.
throwing, sprinting out of blocks, punching, where force acts roughly perpendicular to
gravity). In the first (overcoming weight) case, muscular force first equals the load's
weight (developed under virtually isometric conditions), then exceeds it as motion begins,
producing "a large gradient of acceleration-strength." In the second (overcoming inertia)
case, if friction and air resistance are excluded, movement begins with insignificant
propulsive force, and further force changes depend entirely on the speed of muscular
contraction, i.e. the muscle's simultaneous production of maximal strength and maximal speed
of contraction. Consequently, resisting a load's weight primarily stimulates the strength
component, while resisting a load's inertia primarily stimulates the speed of muscular
contraction. (PDF pp. 218-219, printed pp. 218-219, "4.2.2 Resistance and Strength
Training.")

**Why it works** — The mechanism is straightforward Newtonian mechanics: F = m(g+a) versus F
= ma describe two different physical situations a muscle can be asked to produce force
against, and the authors' point is that a muscle's neuromuscular demand differs between
starting from a preloaded isometric-like state (weight-bearing) and starting from
essentially unloaded rest and having to generate speed against only inertia. The "large
gradient of acceleration-strength" in the weight-bearing case follows from force already
being non-zero (equal to the load's weight) at the instant motion begins, unlike the
inertia-only case, which starts from near-zero force.

**When to apply** — Use the preliminary-tension distinction (loaded-start vs
from-zero-start) to classify which exercises best target starting strength specifically:
per this framework, exercises that begin from essentially zero muscular tension (snatch,
clean-and-jerk, and by extension sprint starts, punches, throws) uniquely combine dynamic
strength, movement speed and starting-strength development, while loaded-start exercises
(squat, squat jump) do not train the speed of switching from rest to active state in the
same way. Use the weight-versus-inertia distinction to separate exercises meant to build
raw strength capacity (heavy squats, deadlifts) from those meant to build speed of
contraction against a light or unloaded resistance (medicine-ball throws, sprint
acceleration drills, punching).

**When NOT to apply** — Inference: this is a biomechanical classification of the demand an
exercise places on the neuromuscular system, not a training-volume or periodization
prescription; the authors do not state how much of each type of exercise to program, only
that the two types target different qualities. Do not assume every "explosive" exercise
falls cleanly into one category; many sport movements (e.g. a jump shot, a football block)
mix a loaded start with subsequent inertia-dominated acceleration, and this framework
addresses the two idealized cases, not every blend.

**Example** — Inference: a back squat (loaded start, force against weight) and a standing
broad jump with no external load (unloaded start, force against inertia) place different
demands on the same lower-body musculature per this framework: the squat's preliminary
tension already equals the bar's weight before the concentric drive begins, while the broad
jump must generate all of its propulsive force from a near-zero starting tension, making
starting strength and speed of contraction the dominant qualities rather than absolute force
capacity against a heavy external weight.

resistance already varies with acceleration per Newton's second law).

# The Optimal Depth-Jump Drop Height Is 0.75-1.15 Metres: Maximal Power at the Low End, Maximal Force at the High End

**What it is** — Verified against the page image (Fig 4.7, printed p. 221): comparing three
vertical-jump conditions by force (in bodyweights) against time, (1) jumping from rest from
a low squat produced the lowest and latest force peak and a jump height of 0.67 m; (2) the
usual jump with a dip/amortisation phase produced a higher, earlier force peak and 0.74 m;
(3) a jump after a depth-jump from 0.4 m produced the highest and earliest force peak and
0.80 m (Verkhoshansky, 1977). Extending this to depth jumps from a range of drop heights
(Fig 4.10, printed p. 223): maximal force (Fmax) increases with drop height up to 1.15
metres, then diminishes sharply; movement time changes little at first, then increases
sharply at greater drop heights; maximum power output and maximum reactivity are both
achieved at a drop height of approximately 0.75 metres. The authors state the optimal range
of depth jumping for stimulating muscular activity is therefore 0.75-1.15 metres: "at the
lower end of this range the muscles are working at maximal power and at the upper end they
are working at maximal dynamic force." This finding underlies the training recommendations
for advanced jumpers (Verkhoshansky 1963, 1964, 1966), though the authors recommend erring
toward considerably lower drop heights for anyone unaccustomed to this training and strictly
limiting volume of this "shock" training. Beyond 1.15 metres, ground-contact (support) time
increases quickly, primarily because the eccentric-to-concentric switching phase lengthens,
while maximum dynamic force and contraction speed at take-off stabilise and stop increasing.
The take-off is described as two distinct phases: amortisation (kinetic energy absorbed,
characterised by progressively deeper knee flexion) and thrust proper (characterised by a
fairly constant speed of muscle contraction). (PDF pp. 222-223, printed pp. 221-223,
"4.2.3 Kinetic Energy and Strength Processes," Figs 4.7, 4.9, 4.10.)

**Why it works** — The authors' stated mechanism is again elastic-energy storage during
amortisation and its release in the thrust phase (see
[[framework--kinetic-energy-stimulation-mechanical-basis-and-advantages]]); at greater drop
heights, force capacity is used mainly for braking (absorbing more kinetic energy),
explaining why force keeps rising up to 1.15 m while power and reactivity peak earlier (at
0.75 m), once the added braking demand from a higher drop starts to lengthen the
amortisation phase and slow the eccentric-to-concentric switch.

**When to apply** — Use 0.75 m as a drop height when the training goal is maximal power and
reactivity, and up to about 1.15 m when the goal is maximal dynamic force, per this specific
protocol; for athletes unaccustomed to depth-jump training, start considerably lower than
this range and strictly limit training volume, per the authors' own caution. This gives a
citable historical anchor for programming depth-jump height, distinct from (and using a
different, variable-height protocol than) the fixed 0.4 m depth-jump reactivity test already
in this library (see [[framework--reactivity-coefficient-and-depth-jump-test]]).

**When NOT to apply** — This is single-lab, era-specific data (Verkhoshansky, 1963-1977) on
an unspecified population (the authors do not state training age, sport, or sample size for
the drop-height range experiment); the specific numbers (0.75 m, 1.15 m) should not be
applied to every athlete without accounting for training age, technical competency and
bodymass, which the authors themselves flag by recommending lower heights for the
unaccustomed athlete. Do not conflate this protocol's variable drop-height range with the
separate, fixed-0.4-m depth-jump reactivity test already documented in this library (see
Related); they are different experiments answering different questions (optimal training
drop height here, versus a reactive-ability measurement protocol there).

**Example** — The book's own example is the Fig 4.7 and Fig 4.10 data described above; no
further invented example is added here.

# Heavier Falling Loads Reduce How High They Get Thrown, but Falling From Higher Reduces It Less: The Kinetic-Energy Throw Experiment

**What it is** — A laboratory stand measured the height a subject could throw a load
upward after it had first fallen from a set height (0.5 to 3.0 metres), with load
expressed as a fraction of the subject's maximum isometric strength (Po): 3.3%, 6.6%,
9.9% and 13.3% of Po (Verkhoshansky, 1977). Verified against the page image (Fig 4.8a,
printed p. 222): as fall height (h1) increases from 0 to 3 metres, throw height (h2)
rises for every load, but the lighter loads (3.3% Po) reach a substantially greater throw
height at every fall height than the heavier loads (13.3% Po), and the heaviest load's
curve flattens and slightly declines at the greatest fall heights rather than continuing to
rise. The authors summarise: "an increase in kinetic energy caused by an increase in the
mass of the load decreases the height to which a load is thrown, but an increase in the
distance through which a load falls increases the height to which it is thrown." A similar
pattern appears for reactive ability (Fig 4.8b, reactivity coefficient plotted against the
falling load's kinetic energy in joules): verified against the page image, each load curve
(3.3%, 6.6%, 10%, 13.3% Po) is an inverted-U shape rising to a peak reactivity coefficient
then declining as kinetic energy increases further, with the peak's position and height
differing by load (the lightest, 3.3% Po, load reaches the highest peak reactivity
coefficient, around 7, at a moderate kinetic energy). The authors conclude "it is not
beneficial to increase kinetic energy by use of heavier loads," and that a better means of
increasing thrust height is needed. (PDF pp. 221-223, printed pp. 221-222, "4.2.3 Kinetic
Energy and Strength Processes," Fig 4.8.)

**Why it works** — The authors give no separate mechanism beyond the shared elastic-energy
logic already stated for this section (see
[[framework--kinetic-energy-stimulation-mechanical-basis-and-advantages]]): a heavier load
carries more kinetic energy at a given fall height (KE = mgh scales with mass), but that
extra energy appears to exceed what the muscle can productively absorb and return within a
short amortisation phase, so throw height and reactivity fall off rather than continuing to
rise with load. Inference: this is consistent with an optimal-loading phenomenon where
elastic-energy return and voluntary concentric contribution both have a productive range,
beyond which added mass mainly adds braking demand rather than usable stored energy.

**When to apply** — Use this as evidence against assuming "more kinetic energy equals more
training stimulus" when programming falling-load or catch-and-thrust exercises (e.g. weighted
plyometric throws, loaded catch-and-press devices): per this experiment, increasing the drop
height is a more productive way to raise thrust/throw height than increasing the load,
within the tested range (0.5-3.0 m fall heights, 3.3-13.3% of isometric strength as load).

**When NOT to apply** — This is a single-lab (Verkhoshansky, 1977) experimental stand using a
narrow load range expressed relative to isometric strength (Po), not a standard external
load; the specific percentages and the shape of the reactivity-coefficient curves should not
be assumed to transfer directly to other exercises, populations, or load types (e.g. a
barbell drop-catch versus this stand's specific apparatus) without direct testing. The
authors do not report the subject population or sample size for this experiment.

**Example** — The book's own example is this experimental stand and its Fig 4.8 data; no
further invented example is added here.

# Stimulating Muscle by Absorbing a Fall's Kinetic Energy: The Mechanics and Five Claimed Advantages Over Weighted Resistance

**What it is** — When a training device such as a barbell falls from a height and the
athlete's task is to actively stop it, then quickly thrust it upward (Fig 4.6, a purpose-built
plyometric bench-press device from a 1992 University of the Witwatersrand student
biomechanics project), the total muscular working force developed by eccentric amortisation
followed by active concentric work can be calculated as F = m(V1^2/2S1 + g) + m(V2^2/2S2 +
g), where V1 and V2 are the barbell's velocity at displacements S1 and S2 (derived from V^2 =
u^2 + 2as). Much of the falling apparatus's kinetic energy converts, during the amortisation
phase, into elastic energy used in the subsequent concentric work; excluding losses such as
heat and viscous damping as a first approximation, this elastic energy equals the kinetic
energy at the end of the fall (KE = mgh, where h is the fall height). Muscular force at the
eccentric-to-concentric switch is greater for a shorter amortisation phase and shorter
braking time, provided the goal is to thrust the apparatus at maximum speed immediately
after stopping it. The authors state the external mechanical stimulus here is "not so much
the weight (and its force of inertia) as the energy accumulated after its free-fall,"
distinguishing this from ordinary weighted resistance (see
[[framework--two-force-regimes-overcoming-weight-vs-inertia]]). Drawing on the vertical-jump
data in Fig 4.7 (see
[[claim--optimal-depth-jump-drop-height-for-power-vs-force]]), the authors list five claimed
advantages of this stimulation method: (1) it develops maximal dynamic force very rapidly;
(2) the magnitude of that maximum is significantly larger than other methods produce; (3) a
large maximal force is achieved without additional resistance; (4) the eccentric-to-concentric
switch happens far more rapidly than with other methods; (5) the elastic energy accumulated
without additional resistance produces greater power and faster contraction in the thrust
phase, inferred from the greater jump height reached. (PDF pp. 220-222, printed pp. 220-221,
"4.2.3 Kinetic Energy and Strength Processes," Fig 4.6.)

**Why it works** — The authors' stated mechanism is elastic-energy storage and release during
the amortisation phase (the same mechanism underlying the shock method, see
[[framework--shock-method-and-plyometric-terminology]]), combined with the mechanical
stimulation of proprioceptors at the instant of an abrupt stretch when a falling body is
stopped (citing Ramsey 1944; Hill 1955; Tweit et al 1963), which the authors say "creates the
conditions for developing starting strength and explosiveness of muscular action" that "no
heavy resistance exercise can imitate," because stimulating muscular activity with
significant extra resistance slows the eccentric-to-concentric transition.

**When to apply** — Use this framework's five advantages as the stated rationale for
programming falling-body/kinetic-energy stimulation (depth jumps, plyometric catches) rather
than purely weighted resistance when the specific goal is starting strength and explosive
strength, per the authors' own claim that these qualities are acquired slowly through
ordinary resistance training because they require unusually stressful, high-intensity
conditions that most popular resistance exercises do not reliably provide even though they
also start force development from rest.

**When NOT to apply** — The force formula (F = m(V1^2/2S1 + g) + m(V2^2/2S2 + g)) and the KE
= mgh approximation explicitly exclude heat generation and viscous damping "as a first
approximation," so treat it as a simplified model, not an exact measurement of muscular
force in vivo. The five advantages are presented as the authors' own summary claims from the
Fig 4.7 jump data (see
[[claim--optimal-depth-jump-drop-height-for-power-vs-force]]), not as independently
replicated findings across other kinetic-energy-stimulation exercises; do not extend them
uncritically to every falling-load exercise without checking that exercise's own data.

**Example** — The book's own example is the depth-jump/plyometric-bench-press apparatus
described above (Fig 4.6); see
[[claim--optimal-depth-jump-drop-height-for-power-vs-force]] for the specific numeric
findings this framework's advantages are drawn from.

# Four Ways to Stimulate Working Muscular Tension, and How They Map to the Rest of the Chapter

**What it is** — External muscular force is a product of voluntary effort, but voluntary
effort alone has limits; the authors classify how "stimulation of working force" is
supplied from outside that voluntary effort into four basic types: (1) muscular tension
produced by voluntary effort against external resistance, where the moving load's
resistance "accelerates and regulates the effector impulses to the muscles"; (2)
stimulation by the kinetic energy of a falling object or the body, where "the effort is
primarily reflexive"; (3) stimulation caused primarily by voluntary effort under conditions
where additional external mechanical stimulation is absent or limited; and (4) stimulation
of muscle "elicited or intensified involuntarily by means of external electrical current."
The authors group the first two as dynamic work and the third as isometric work; for the
first, increased stimulation comes from increasing the velocity of the preliminary fall of
the body or load; for the third, from "mobilising the athlete's voluntary resources."
Voluntary effort plays an important role in the first case, but is "insignificant" in the
second (the falling-body case), where the braking/amortisation mechanism produces a
protective reflex response faster than a voluntary sequence of actions can. This forms the
basis of plyometric training (see [[framework--shock-method-and-plyometric-terminology]]).
This four-way classification sets up the rest of the chapter's structure: type (4) is
covered next as 4.2.1 Electrostimulation, type (1) as 4.2.2 Resistance and Strength
Training, type (2) as 4.2.3 Kinetic Energy and Strength Processes, and type (3) as 4.2.4
Isometric Training. (PDF p. 218, printed p. 207, "4.2 Different Means of Strength
Development.")

**Why it works** — The authors' stated reason: afferent impulses elicited by mechanical
stimulation inform the central nervous system about the strength of the external
influence evoking muscular tension, and "the greater the intensity of the external
stimulus, the greater the muscle effector discharge, and the larger the external work
produced." Inference: this is a general afferent-feedback framing rather than a
mechanism specific to any one of the four types; the chapter's later sections give more
detailed, type-specific mechanisms (e.g. elastic energy storage for the falling-body case,
gradual overload for the resistance case).

**When to apply** — Use this as an organizing checklist when auditing a program's strength
means: does a given exercise stimulate tension primarily through external resistance,
through absorbed kinetic energy (a fall or catch), through sustained voluntary effort with
little external aid (isometric), or through externally applied electrical current? Mixing
means from more than one category (e.g. resistance plus electrostimulation) is a distinct
programming decision from staying within one category, and the chapter's later sections
(4.2.1 Electrostimulation, "Sports Functional Electrostimulation") discuss combining
categories explicitly.

**When NOT to apply** — Inference: this is the book's own convenience classification for
organizing chapter 4.2, not a claim that these four mechanisms are mutually exclusive
within a single exercise (a depth jump followed by a jump, for example, combines kinetic-
energy stimulation with subsequent voluntary resistance work). Do not treat the four-way
split as a measurement protocol; it carries no associated testing method here.

**Example** — Inference: a coach comparing a paused back squat (voluntary effort against
external resistance, type 1), a depth jump (kinetic energy of a fall, type 2), a wall sit
(sustained voluntary isometric effort, type 3), and a quadriceps NMES session (external
electrical current, type 4) is comparing four different stimulation mechanisms for the same
target muscle group, each covered by a different subsection of this chapter. This example
is not in the source.
