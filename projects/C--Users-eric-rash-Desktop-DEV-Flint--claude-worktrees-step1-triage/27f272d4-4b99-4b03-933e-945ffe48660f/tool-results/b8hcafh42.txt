# Hettinger and Muller's Original Isometric-Training Finding: About 5% Strength Gain Per Week From One Daily 6-Second Effort at Two-Thirds Maximum

**What it is** — Isometric exercises became popular in the mid-1950s in the search for
economical, effective strength methods. Hettinger and Muller (1953, 1955) established that
one daily effort at two-thirds of maximum, held for six seconds, performed for 10 weeks,
increases strength by about 5% per week. Separately, Clark et al (1954) found that static
strength continues to increase even after a four-week isometric programme has concluded.
This early success prompted considerable research comparing isometric training's
effectiveness against dynamic training, which produced "rather contradictory data" (see
review by Verkhoshansky, 1970). The authors' general conclusion from that body of research:
isometric training can be more effective than dynamic exercise specifically where the target
exercise requires a large-magnitude muscle contraction at a certain stage of a movement, or
during early-stage injury rehabilitation; if the target exercise involves high-speed
movement, sustained isometric training is less effective. The authors also note that
because isometric training's usual purpose is developing absolute strength, tension should
be increased slowly and held for a relatively long time, since prolonged tension maintenance
demands the energy expenditure that stimulates adequate neuromuscular adaptation; some
researchers found no essential difference in strength gain from varying isometric tension
intensity, from which the authors infer that tension duration, not magnitude, is the
"fundamental conditioning factor." (PDF pp. 235, printed pp. 223-224, "4.2.4 Isometric
Training.")

**Why it works** — The authors' stated reasoning: prolonged maintenance of muscular tension
requires an energy expenditure that "stimulates adequate adaptation in the neuromuscular
system, thereby determining its strength potential," and this can exceed the stimulus from
brief transient dynamic tension. Inference: no cellular or neural mechanism is given for why
duration outweighs magnitude specifically; this is an inference the authors draw from
studies not finding a strength-gain difference across tension intensities, not a stated
physiological pathway.

**When to apply** — Use the 5%-per-week figure and the two-thirds-maximum, 6-second, daily,
10-week protocol as a citable historical anchor for isometric-training dosing, and use the
authors' scoping rule (isometrics favoured for large-magnitude contraction at a specific
movement stage or early rehab; less favoured when the target skill is high-speed) to decide
when isometric work is the better tool versus dynamic resistance training.

**When NOT to apply** — This is single-era data (1950s studies, corroborated only by a 1970
review) without modern replication in this text; treat "5% per week" as specific to
Hettinger and Muller's exact protocol (two-thirds max, 6 seconds, daily, 10 weeks), not a
generalizable isometric-training growth rate. The "contradictory data" comparing isometric
to dynamic training is acknowledged by the authors themselves, so this entry's headline
finding should not be read as settling the isometric-versus-dynamic debate; it explains only
the original claim's origin and the authors' scoping conditions for it.

**Example** — The book's own example is the Hettinger and Muller protocol itself (daily,
6-second, two-thirds-maximum isometric effort for 10 weeks, yielding about 5% weekly
strength gain); no further invented example is added here.

# Isometric Endurance Collapses Above 10-15% of Maximal Tension, and Recovers in Three Distinct Phases

**What it is** — Isometric endurance at resistance exceeding 10-15% of a muscle's maximal
strength is very short compared with dynamic exercise, because of metabolic demand and
impaired blood flow to the exercising muscle under sustained contraction (Petrofsky &
Phillips, 1986); below that threshold, contractions are considered non-fatiguing, since
intramuscular pressure stays low and is readily exceeded by rising mean arterial pressure in
the capillaries. Verified against the page image (Fig 4.13, printed p. 227, cat soleus and
gastrocnemius, electrically stimulated, based on Petrofsky & Hendershot, 1984): intramuscular
pressure rises roughly linearly with muscle tension, from near 0 kPa at rest to over 20 kPa
at 100% of maximal strength. As tension rises, intramuscular pressure rises with it and
metabolism cannot keep pace, producing a hyperbolic drop in performance and marked fatigue
increase (the Rohmert curve, first derived by Monod and Sherrer 1967, later by Rohmert
1968). Verified against the page image (Fig 4.14, printed p. 227, finger flexors, based on
Petrofsky 1980): endurance time falls hyperbolically as %MVC (maximum voluntary contraction)
rises, and females sustain the same tension level for longer than males at every tension
level tested, a difference the authors say "has not been adequately explained yet." Muscle
temperature also affects isometric endurance: hot packs reduce it and pre-exercise icing
increases it, with an optimum core muscle temperature (later work places it near 27-28
Celsius, Petrofsky & Phillips, 1986) above and below which endurance drops; higher bodyfat
percentage, associated with higher resting muscle temperature, is linked to lower isometric
endurance in most situations. Unlike dynamic exercise (which is more efficient at higher
temperatures, likely due to reduced tissue viscosity), isometric contraction involves no
fibre movement, so viscosity plays an "insignificant role compared with that of muscle
metabolism." Recovery from isometric contraction proceeds in three phases (Petrofsky &
Phillips, 1986): a rapid recovery of about 50% of endurance in the first 3 minutes; a slower
recovery lasting about 20 minutes, reaching about 90% of initial endurance; and complete
recovery within at least 24 hours; recovery from low-tension isometric contraction is
"interestingly" slower during the initial recovery phases. (PDF pp. 236-239, printed pp.
226-228, "4.2.4 Isometric Training," "Isometric Endurance," "Recovery after Isometric
Activity," Figs 4.13-4.14.)

**Why it works** — The authors' stated mechanism is a mechanical-metabolic one: rising muscle
tension raises intramuscular pressure, which above roughly 10-15% of maximal strength begins
to exceed the arterial pressure available to perfuse the muscle, choking off the blood flow
that metabolism needs to sustain the contraction, producing the hyperbolic Rohmert-curve
drop in endurance. No mechanism is given for the sex difference in endurance or for the
27-28 Celsius optimum temperature beyond citing the studies that measured them.

**When to apply** — Use the 10-15%-of-maximal-strength threshold as a practical dividing line
between "non-fatiguing" and rapidly fatiguing isometric holds when designing isometric
protocols (e.g. low-level postural holds versus near-maximal strength holds). Use the
three-phase recovery timeline (about 50% back in 3 minutes, about 90% back in 20 minutes,
full recovery by 24 hours) when spacing repeated maximal isometric efforts within and between
sessions.

**When NOT to apply** — The intramuscular-pressure data (Fig 4.13) are from electrically
stimulated cat soleus and gastrocnemius (Petrofsky & Hendershot, 1984), not human voluntary
contraction; treat the specific kPa values as animal-model data illustrating the
tension-pressure relationship, not human norms. The Rohmert-curve endurance data (Fig 4.14)
are specifically finger-flexor endurance (Petrofsky, 1980), a small, distal muscle group;
the shape of the curve (hyperbolic decline, female advantage) should not be assumed to hold
identically for large, proximal muscle groups without separate verification. The 27-28
Celsius optimum-temperature finding and the bodyfat-endurance link are each from single
cited sources without reported effect sizes.

**Example** — Inference: a coach prescribing a sustained postural isometric hold (e.g. a
plank or wall-sit at low relative intensity) can expect it to be comparatively non-fatiguing
per the 10-15%-of-maximum threshold, while a near-maximal isometric mid-thigh pull hold will
fatigue rapidly and require the multi-minute recovery windows this entry describes before a
comparable maximal effort can be repeated.

# Isometric Strength Gains Spread Further From the Training Angle When the Muscle Trains in a Lengthened Position

**What it is** — Many studies report isometric training producing maximum strength gains at
or very close to the trained joint angle, leading some athletes to avoid isometric training
altogether; the authors argue this "angular specificity" framing needs critical revision,
citing other studies showing isometric training also produces significant strength increase
up to about 15 degrees either side of the training angle. Because every type of muscle
contraction has its own force/torque-versus-joint-angle curve (see 2.1.2), the authors argue
it is "highly unlikely" that strength gain would be confined to one precise angle and
nowhere else, and prefer the term "regional specificity" over "angular specificity" (or
muscle-length specificity). Thepault-Mathieu et al (1988) found this regional specificity is
strongest when the muscle trains in its most shortened position and weakest when it trains
most lengthened: males training isometrically for 5 weeks (5 sets of 5 reps, 5 seconds each,
3 times weekly) at 25 degrees of elbow flexion (full extension = 0 degrees) gained 54%
strength at the training angle but only about 25% averaged over the rest of the range; a
group training at 80 degrees gained 30% at the training angle and about 25% over the rest of
the range; a group training at 120 degrees gained 25% at the training angle but only about
7% over the rest of the range. The authors' summary: isometric training of a relatively
lengthened muscle can produce substantial strength increase "not only near the region of
training, but also throughout the range of movement." They caution this finding should not
be read as meaning isometric training can replace other strength-training forms, since a
specific type of static or dynamic strength depends on the neuromuscular factors governing
how force is exerted in a given situation. (PDF pp. 237-238, printed pp. 226-227, "4.2.4
Isometric Training," "Isometric Training and Angular Specificity.")

**Why it works** — The authors give the biomechanical basis (a distinct force/torque-vs-angle
curve exists for every contraction type) for why strict single-angle specificity is
implausible, but they do not give a mechanism for why the Thepault-Mathieu et al (1988)
lengthened-position condition specifically produced the broadest transfer across the range
(54%/25 degrees training angle giving about 25% elsewhere, versus 120 degrees training angle
giving only about 7% elsewhere). Inference: a plausible explanation, not stated in the text,
is that training at longer muscle lengths recruits a different or broader distribution of
sarcomere lengths and joint-angle-dependent moment arms than training at a shortened length,
though this is not confirmed by the source.

**When to apply** — Use this as a rationale for choosing a relatively lengthened joint
position when isometric training is intended to transfer strength gains across a wider
portion of the range of motion, rather than assuming isometric work only strengthens the
exact trained angle. Use the "regional specificity" framing, not "angular specificity," when
discussing isometric training's carryover with coaches or athletes.

**When NOT to apply** — This is a single study (Thepault-Mathieu et al, 1988) with a specific
protocol (5 weeks, 5x5 at 5 seconds, 3x weekly, elbow flexion only) and three training
angles; the specific percentages (54%, 30%, 25% at-angle gains; roughly 25%, 25%, 7%
remainder-of-range gains) should not be generalised to other joints, muscle groups, or
protocols without direct testing. The authors' own caution stands: this finding does not
mean isometric training can substitute for dynamic strength training generally.

**Example** — The book's own example is the Thepault-Mathieu et al (1988) three-group study
described above; no further invented example is added here.

# Isometric Contraction Is Not One Thing: Voluntary vs Reflexive, Slow vs Explosive

**What it is** — The authors argue isometric training is not simply holding a static
contraction for a set time: a true isometric contraction requires the muscle to raise
tension from rest to a maximal or sub-maximal value over a rise ("attack") time, sustain
that tension for a resistance phase, then decrease it to rest or a lower value over a decay
time. Verified against the page image (Fig 4.11, printed p. 225): the authors categorise
isometric contraction into voluntary isometrics (splitting further into slow isometrics and
voluntary explosive isometrics) and reflexive isometrics (splitting further into reflexive
explosive isometrics and oscillatory isometrics). A voluntary isometric contraction can be
produced deliberately; a reflexive one occurs involuntarily via the muscle's reflex response
between the eccentric and concentric phases of plyometric activities such as a depth jump or
the weightlifting clean-and-jerk. Verified against the page image (Fig 4.12, printed p.
225): slow isometrics show a gradual force rise, a longer resistance-phase plateau, and a
gradual decay; explosive (reflexive) isometrics show a much steeper rise phase and a
substantially shorter resistance phase before decay. The authors argue that because the
steepness of the isometric force-time curve, and the higher magnitude of maximum isometric
versus dynamic maximum force at equivalent joint angles, both support developing explosive
strength isometrically when the training accent is on speed of force development, "it is
doubtful whether it is always productive to maintain a rigid distinction between dynamic and
isometric exercise": as resistance grows very large, dynamic work becomes progressively more
isometric, so isometric work is "really the limiting case of dynamic work as the velocity of
movement tends to zero." Because reflexive isometric contraction bypasses the inhibitory
effects normally associated with voluntary muscle action, "even greater explosive force can
be displayed isometrically than dynamically." A related but separate finding on impulsive
loading: short bouts of low-frequency mechanical vibration (10-35 Hz) applied to the body
have been shown to speed recovery, modulate muscle activity, raise strength/power stable
state, lower arterial pressure and enhance oxidative processes (Kopysov, 1978; Lebedev &
Peliakov, 1991), and whole-body vibration at 26 Hz through the lower extremities has been
shown to produce marked increases in jumping power (Bosco et al, 1998); the authors note
this fuels an open debate about whether plyometric training effects are driven mainly by
elastic-energy storage/release, neural facilitation, or intrinsic muscle changes (Ingen
Schenau et al, 1997). (PDF pp. 235-236, printed pp. 224-225, "4.2.4 Isometric Training,"
Figs 4.11-4.12.)

**Why it works** — The authors' mechanism for the voluntary/reflexive distinction is neural:
reflexive isometric contraction (occurring automatically between eccentric and concentric
phases) escapes voluntary inhibitory control that would otherwise limit force, permitting
higher force display than a deliberately initiated voluntary contraction can achieve.
Inference: the slow-vs-explosive distinction within each category follows from how quickly
neural drive ramps up to the target tension level, which the force-time curve shape (Fig
4.12) captures directly.

**When to apply** — Use this taxonomy when specifying what kind of isometric work a given
drill is training: a sustained maximal hold at a joint angle is a slow voluntary isometric;
a deliberately fast ramp to near-maximal tension is a voluntary explosive isometric; the
brief isometric moment at the bottom of a depth jump or clean-and-jerk catch is a reflexive
(explosive or oscillatory) isometric that the athlete does not consciously initiate. Per the
authors, distinguish "isometric training for developing absolute strength" from "isometric
training for developing explosive strength" and select the appropriate type for the goal.

**When NOT to apply** — The authors themselves flag this distinction as still needing
"detailed experimental corroboration." The vibration/whole-body-vibration findings are a
separate line of evidence (Bosco et al, 1998, and others) that the authors connect to this
discussion only by analogy (impulsive loading), not by stating vibration produces isometric
contraction in the sense defined here; treat the two topics as related but distinct.

**Example** — The book's own example is the depth-jump and clean-and-jerk catch positions
cited as sources of reflexive isometric contraction; no further invented example is added
here.

# External vs Internal Isometrics, and Using a Held Supramaximal Load to Prime the Nervous System

**What it is** — The authors distinguish "external isometrics" (contraction exerted against
an external load) from "internal isometrics" (contraction exerted against opposing muscles
within the body, as in bodybuilders' posing actions; see
[[heuristic--loadless-training-broomstick-protocol]]). Isometric contractions can be
submaximal or maximal, short or long duration, continuous or intermittent, sequenced across
different joint angles, alternated between agonist and antagonist (or adductor/abductor,
medial/lateral rotators), and alternated between intensities, including voluntary
oscillation between high and low intensity to prolong the total time under tension. A
separate practical technique: isometrically holding a heavy load at a "sticking point" can
reduce deficiencies in that region or prime the nervous system to cope with greater loading
during gradual progressive overload; the authors' specific example is supporting a
supramaximal load (120-150% of 1RM) on the shoulders, or performing quasi-isometric movement
over the first few degrees of knee flexion in a squat, to "accustom the body neuromuscularly
and favourably enhance one's mental perception" for handling future loads only slightly
heavier than the current 1RM. The authors also state that isometric contraction released
explosively "can decrease the reaction response time by as much as 7%, particularly if
associated with a strong pre-stretch," against the traditional coaching belief that
movement is most efficient starting from complete relaxation; Verkhoshansky is cited as
stressing that a movement produced from complete relaxation is usually slower and less
forceful than one that begins from some prior tension. Historically, isometric effort was
emphasised heavily by DeLorme, Hettinger and Muller, but with the rise of gymnasium machines
and the decline of Charles Atlas's mail-order "dynamic tension" programmes, isometrics
largely fell out of popular use, though physical therapists still use it in early
injury-rehabilitation stages, and Russian coaches often prescribe a 75% concentric / 15%
eccentric / 10% isometric strength-training regime for the average athlete. The authors also
note that because one basic PNF principle holds that mobility (dynamic contraction) is more
primitive than stability (isometric contraction), stability sits at a "higher level of
muscular learning." (PDF pp. 239-240, printed pp. 228-229, "4.2.4 Isometric Training,"
"Other Aspects of Isometric Activity.")

**Why it works** — For the reaction-time claim, the mechanism given is that reflexive
isometric contraction bypasses voluntary inhibition (already stated for explosive isometrics
generally, see
[[framework--isometric-contraction-categorization-voluntary-reflexive]]), and a strong
pre-stretch potentiates this further. For supramaximal holding, no cellular mechanism is
given; the stated effect is psychological/neuromuscular familiarisation ("accustom the body
neuromuscularly," "favourably enhance one's mental perception") rather than a measured
strength-transfer pathway.

**When to apply** — Use the external/internal isometrics distinction as vocabulary when
specifying whether a drill uses an external load or purely internal agonist-antagonist
tensing. Use supramaximal-hold priming (120-150% of 1RM support holds, or partial-range
quasi-isometric squat work) as a technique to familiarise an athlete with loads slightly
above their current 1RM before attempting a true max attempt. Use the 75/15/10
concentric/eccentric/isometric regime as a citable, if dated, historical reference point when
discussing how much isometric work a general strength programme might include.

**When NOT to apply** — The 7% reaction-time figure and the supramaximal-holding
familiarisation effect are stated without reported sample sizes or controlled outcome data
in this passage; treat them as the authors' summarised claims, not independently verified
percentages. The 75/15/10 regime is attributed generally to "Russian coaches" without a
specific citation, so treat it as a reported convention rather than an experimentally
validated ratio. Supramaximal holding (120-150% of 1RM) carries obvious injury risk if
attempted without adequate rack/spotter safety and should not be treated as a routine
prescription without those safeguards, which this passage does not itself discuss.

**Example** — Inference: a lifter approaching a new back-squat 1RM attempt could support a
supramaximal load (say, 110-120% of the projected new max) in the rack for a brief hold
beforehand, per this heuristic's priming logic, to build familiarity with that load level
before the actual max attempt; the specific 110-120% figure here is an adapted illustration,
not the source's own 120-150% number.

# Isometric Training's Six Advantages, Six Drawbacks, and the Authors' Recommended Execution Protocol

**What it is** — The authors list six advantages proponents claim for isometric training:
(1) it requires very simple apparatus; (2) it can locally exercise any muscle group at a
specific joint angle, which dynamic work can only briefly pass through, since movement
inertia usually carries the load past the angle of greatest mechanical effect; (3) it is
time-productive, since each 6-second isometric contraction is "equivalent in its effect" to
many dynamic ballistic contractions in which maximal force lasts no more than 0.1 second,
meaning 10 minutes of targeted isometric tension can replace "a fatiguing hour" of dynamic
weight training; (4) it produces an insignificant increase in muscle mass and bodymass
compared with dynamic strength work, particularly pressing exercises; (5) it costs far less
time and energy than dynamic weight training, letting an athlete preserve a high
speed-strength level during a crucial competition period; (6) it offers more potential than
dynamic exercise for using visual and kinaesthetic cues to hold a given position, an
advantage for studying and correcting technical errors. Six drawbacks are also listed:
isometrics fatigue the nervous system; may harm the cardiovascular system in some
individuals via prolonged breath-holding and elevated blood pressure; disturb coordination;
enhance muscle performance only near the specific trained joint angle; decrease speed of
movement; and diminish soft-tissue elasticity and functional range of dynamic movement.
Proper breathing, adequate rest/relaxation between exercises, and limiting contraction length
to 6 seconds (some authors recommend up to 10) can minimise these harmful after-effects. The
authors give four methods of producing isometric tension: (1) pressing against an immovable
structure or a partner's resistance, with the drawback that strength change can only be
judged subjectively without a dynamometer; (2) holding a raised load for a set period, with a
quasi-isometric variant shifting the load slowly across the range with stops at different
joint angles; (3) pushing a weight from a lower support up to press against an upper stop
(a power rack), giving a brief dynamic phase before the isometric hold and letting the
athlete lift several times between stops; (4) using a dynamometer or other resistance
device to control and display tension magnitude. For non-explosive isometrics, the authors
give a specific protocol: apply force gradually, hold maximal tension no longer than 8
seconds, perform 6 repetitions, rest 5-10 seconds between reps, limit total isometric
training to 10 minutes per session, and finish with relaxation exercises; for developing
explosive strength instead, tension should be generated at maximum speed to 70-80% of
maximum strength, and reflexive explosive isometric action from plyometric movements "can be
extremely effective" for this. (PDF pp. 236-237, printed pp. 225-226, "4.2.4 Isometric
Training.")

**Why it works** — The authors give a specific mechanism only for advantage (2), regarding
joint-angle specificity of loading, and advantage (3), the time-efficiency claim, which rests
on comparing sustained near-maximal isometric tension duration against the brief (about 0.1
second) duration of maximal force during a ballistic dynamic contraction. Inference: the
remaining advantages and drawbacks are stated as observations rather than derived from a
single unifying mechanism.

**When to apply** — Use the 8-second hold, 6-repetition, 5-10-second-rest, 10-minute-session
protocol as the authors' recommended baseline for non-explosive isometric training aimed at
absolute strength, and the 70-80%-max, maximum-speed protocol for explosive-strength-focused
isometric work. Weigh the six advantages against the six drawbacks for a given athlete and
context: the cardiovascular caution (drawback 2) is particularly relevant for athletes with
existing cardiovascular risk factors, and the coordination-disturbance and range-of-movement
drawbacks argue for pairing isometric work with dynamic training rather than using it as a
sole training means.

**When NOT to apply** — This protocol and these advantages/drawbacks are the authors' own
synthesis from an era (1950s-1970s citations) predating modern force-plate or dynamometer-based
isometric dosing research; do not treat the "equivalent to a fatiguing hour of training"
comparison (advantage 3) as a rigorously measured equivalence, since no controlled outcome
comparison is cited for that specific claim. The drawbacks (cardiovascular risk, coordination
disturbance, reduced movement speed, reduced tissue elasticity) argue against using isometric
training as an athlete's sole or dominant training means, per the authors' own framing.

**Example** — Inference: a coach using isometric mid-thigh pulls to reinforce a specific
sticking-point joint angle is applying advantage (2) directly; the same coach should still
program dynamic work elsewhere in the week to offset the coordination and movement-speed
drawbacks this entry lists.

# Loadless Training: Tensing Muscles Maximally Against an Unloaded Broomstick to Train Without Equipment

**What it is** — "Loadless training" (the Russian term; bodybuilders call the related
practice "posing" training) has an athlete voluntarily tense agonists and antagonists
maximally at every joint without an external load, imitating a loaded lift's demand through
mental effort and tension alone. In the early 1900s Anokhin and Froshek proposed developing
strength through motivated tensing of agonists and antagonists at every joint, similar to
bodybuilders tensing before judges, but the technique was not studied scientifically until
after 1965. Kovalik (1978) ran a doctoral thesis on this "voluntary loadless muscle tensing,"
aiming to develop a supplementary training system imposing less joint stress than weight
training, particularly for younger lifters whose skeletal systems have not matured. His
subjects performed Olympic and supplementary lifts (e.g. snatch pull, front squat) with a
broomstick while trying to generate maximum muscle tension as if overcoming a maximal
weight; this produced definite muscle strengthening and technical-skill improvement, but
Kovalik concluded it must be used cautiously in Olympic weightlifting because it might
disrupt optimal extensor-flexor strength ratios. Loadless training is described as
especially useful without access to training facilities or during recovery from
musculoskeletal injury, and can be performed isometrically (joint held at a specific angle),
quasi-isometrically (joint moved powerfully through a full range), or dynamically
(imitating a weight-training movement with unloaded muscular tension against a broomstick).
Kovalik's recommended protocol: 5 sets per exercise, separated by 3-10 seconds of rest;
during quasi-isometric loadless training, avoid long periods of intense breath-holding and
instead exhale slowly in synchrony with the slow tensing of each movement; each movement
should involve strong simultaneous tension in agonists and antagonists, starting from full
extension and ending with stretching beyond full extension and back, over 10-15 seconds; the
holding phase at the end of each movement (e.g. the overhead jerk position) should be held
with maximal tension for 6-8 seconds; rotating joints in different planes and moving limbs
in diagonal/spiral patterns follows PNF principles and can enhance loadless training's
effectiveness as a supplementary strengthening method. (PDF pp. 240-241, printed pp.
229-230, "4.2.4 Isometric Training," "Loadless Training.")

**Why it works** — No cellular mechanism is given beyond the general isometric/quasi-isometric
tension logic already stated for the chapter; the authors' stated rationale for loadless
training's specific value is reduced joint stress compared with weight training (relevant
for immature skeletons or injury recovery) and portability (no equipment required). The
caution about disrupting extensor-flexor ratios implies that voluntary tensing without an
external load and its accompanying proprioceptive feedback may not reproduce the same
relative muscle-group demand as loaded lifting.

**When to apply** — Use loadless training as a supplementary strengthening tool when
equipment is unavailable (e.g. travel) or during early-stage musculoskeletal injury
recovery, and specifically for younger or skeletally immature lifters where reduced joint
loading is a priority. Follow Kovalik's protocol details (5 sets, 3-10s rest, slow
exhalation during quasi-isometric tensing, 10-15s per movement, 6-8s holding phase) as the
authors' cited baseline.

**When NOT to apply** — Kovalik's own caution applies directly: use loadless training
cautiously in competitive Olympic weightlifting programming specifically, since it may
disrupt the optimal extensor-flexor strength ratio the sport requires. This is a single
doctoral thesis (Kovalik, 1978) without reported sample size or a control condition compared
against standard weight training in this passage; treat it as a supplementary method, not a
substitute for loaded resistance training.

**Example** — The book's own example is Kovalik's protocol described above (broomstick
snatch pull and front squat, maximal tension, 5 sets); no further invented example is added
here.

# Eccentric Training Damages Tissue and Causes Soreness at First, but Adaptation Removes Both Without Losing the Strength Gain

**What it is** — Because the muscle complex lengthens under eccentric contraction, its series
elastic component (SEC) is exposed to greater strain than under concentric conditions,
raising injury potential; most muscle strains and ruptures occur during the eccentric phase
of movement (Garrett, 1986). A single session of intense eccentric exercise commonly causes
pronounced delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), peaking 24-72 hours after exercise and
resolving within several days (Friden et al, 1983a), accompanied by reduced dynamic strength
and damage to myofibrils and connective-tissue elements such as the Z bands (a SEC
component). Friden et al (1983b) also examined longer eccentric-training exposure (3
sessions weekly over 8 weeks total) and found post-exercise soreness stopped occurring after
2-3 weeks, while the ability to perform eccentric work increased by 375%; biopsies showed no
Z-band damage at that point, suggesting adaptation had occurred (the repeated-bout effect).
Schwane and Armstrong (1983) corroborated this with downhill running in rats, which produced
a superior training effect to level or uphill running and prevented injury more effectively
over time. Eccentric exercise is known to produce greater and more rapid strength and
hypertrophy increases than concentric exercise (Hortobagyi et al, 2000), and it is sometimes
suggested this stems from eccentric exercise's greater tissue damage; however, Komulainen et
al (2000) found the increase in myofibrillar cross-sectional area in unchanged (non-damaged)
muscle fibres was much the same after concentric exercise as after the more damaging
eccentric exercise, a result the authors say "do not support the hypothesis that fibre
hypertrophy depends on the extent of the fibre damage in exercised muscles." (PDF pp.
241-242, printed pp. 230-231, "4.2.5 Eccentric Training.")

**Why it works** — For the soreness/damage mechanism, the stated pathway is mechanical:
eccentric lengthening strains the SEC (including the Z bands) more than concentric
shortening does, producing microdamage that manifests as DOMS. For the repeated-bout
adaptation, no cellular mechanism is given beyond the biopsy finding that Z-band damage no
longer occurs after 2-3 weeks of repeated exposure; Inference: this is consistent with a
protective structural or neural adaptation (increased sarcomere number in series, improved
motor-unit recruitment patterns, or connective-tissue remodelling) that the text does not
itself specify. For the Hortobagyi-versus-Komulainen tension, the authors present Komulainen
et al's finding as evidence against a damage-drives-hypertrophy causal model, without
proposing an alternative mechanism for why eccentric training produces faster
strength/hypertrophy gains if not primarily through greater damage.

**When to apply** — Use the repeated-bout effect (soreness and Z-band damage both disappear
within 2-3 weeks of regular eccentric exposure, while eccentric work capacity rises sharply,
here 375% in 8 weeks) as the rationale for introducing eccentric-emphasis training
gradually and consistently rather than sporadically, to reach the adapted, lower-injury-risk
state faster. Use the downhill-running finding (Schwane & Armstrong, 1983) as a citable
rationale for progressive eccentric loading in return-to-running or tendon/muscle
rehabilitation programming, alongside the tendon-adaptation hypothesis already noted in
[[claim--eccentric-vs-concentric-training-adaptation-contradictory-findings]].

**When NOT to apply** — The rat downhill-running data (Schwane & Armstrong, 1983) is an
animal model; do not assume the same magnitude of protective adaptation transfers directly
to human eccentric training without separate verification. The Komulainen et al (2000)
finding that hypertrophy does not depend on damage extent contradicts the popular
"damage-drives-adaptation" narrative for eccentric training; do not cite eccentric training's
greater soreness or damage as itself the reason to prefer it for hypertrophy, since this
specific study found comparable myofibrillar growth in undamaged fibres regardless of
contraction type. The 375% eccentric-work-capacity increase is from one study (Friden et al,
1983b) with a specific 8-week, 3x-weekly protocol; it measures work capacity, not maximal
strength specifically.

**Example** — Inference: introducing a new eccentric-emphasis exercise (e.g. slow eccentric
Nordic curls) should start conservatively and progress gradually over roughly the first 2-3
weeks, per Friden et al's repeated-bout timeline, before expecting soreness to diminish and
before assuming the tissue has reached its adapted, more damage-resistant state.

# Eccentric-Training Studies Disagree on Whether It Beats Concentric Training for Strength Gains

**What it is** — Eccentric contraction is metabolically more efficient than concentric
contraction and capable of generating higher forces (Kaneko et al, 1984; Komi, 1973; Rodgers
& Berger, 1974), and the concentric-eccentric force gap widens as contraction velocity
increases: maximum eccentric force rises while maximum concentric force falls as velocity
increases, even though EMG for the muscle group stays reasonably constant (Komi, 1973; see
also [[claim--eccentric-strength-and-energetics-vs-concentric]] for the underlying
force/energy-cost data from Ch3). Training-adaptation findings, however, are described as
"often contradictory": Johnson et al (1976) found no significant difference between
concentric and eccentric training methods; Komi and Buskirk (1972), using an isokinetic
dynamometer, found eccentric training produced greater eccentric, concentric AND isometric
strength increases than concentric training, while concentric training increased only
concentric and eccentric strength; Friden et al (1983b) found eccentric training to failure
produced marked increases in eccentric strength-endurance but minimal change in concentric
strength-endurance. The high-speed, large-acceleration SMART (Seated Movement Aerobic and
Resistive Training, "Supersitting") system created by Lisa Ericson (1992) for work with
disabled populations tends to increase both concentric and eccentric strength-endurance
together. The authors note other researchers report adaptation differences tied to training
velocity, measurement velocity and the specific muscle group tested, while very few studies
compare functional strength and power changes across free weights, auxotonic (variable
resistance) activities, or natural sporting movements, concluding "the central role played
by the specificity principle clearly has a profound effect on the experimental analysis." A
separate mechanistic note: the superior eccentric strengthening seen at high isokinetic
velocities (over 180 degrees/second) may reflect selective tensile-strength increase in
tendon and other series elastic components rather than the contractile elements, with a
training/rehabilitation implication that high-velocity progressive-overload eccentric work
may build the musculotendinous junction's capacity to resist high-impact forces from
running, jumping and other impulsive actions. (PDF p. 241, printed p. 230, "4.2.5 Eccentric
Training.")

**Why it works** — The authors give a partial mechanism for the velocity-dependent
concentric-eccentric force gap (already established in Ch3's force-velocity treatment, see
[[claim--eccentric-strength-and-energetics-vs-concentric]] and
[[framework--force-time-curve-adaptation-pattern-by-movement-type]]), but give no mechanism
reconciling why different training studies (Johnson 1976 vs Komi & Buskirk 1972 vs Friden
1983b) reach different conclusions about which training mode transfers best; the authors'
own explanation is methodological (differing velocities, populations, and measurement
specificity across studies), not physiological.

**When to apply** — Inference: when deciding between eccentric-emphasis and concentric-emphasis
training for a given goal, treat "eccentric training is superior" as a contested claim
depending heavily on which outcome is measured (eccentric strength specifically tends to
respond best to eccentric training across these studies; broader transfer to concentric or
functional strength is less consistent). The high-velocity eccentric/tendon-adaptation
hypothesis is a plausible rationale for progressive high-velocity eccentric work in tendon
rehabilitation (e.g. patellar or Achilles tendinopathy), consistent with modern eccentric
tendon-loading protocols, though the authors do not cite such protocols directly.

**When NOT to apply** — This entry summarises training-adaptation studies from a narrow era
(1972-1992) with small, heterogeneous samples and differing protocols (isokinetic dynamometer
training in Komi & Buskirk; training-to-failure in Friden; a seated aerobic-resistive system
in Ericson) that are not directly comparable to one another. Do not treat "eccentric training
increases concentric, eccentric and isometric strength" (Komi & Buskirk's specific finding)
as a settled general principle; Johnson et al (1976) found no such advantage under a
different protocol. The tendon-adaptation hypothesis for high-velocity eccentric work is
explicitly framed as a possible explanation ("may be due to"), not a confirmed mechanism.

**Example** — Inference: a rehabilitation programme using slow, controlled eccentric squats
for general lower-body strength is applying a different training stimulus than one using
fast, high-velocity eccentric work (over 180 degrees/second) aimed at tendon/musculotendinous
junction adaptation; per this entry, the evidence for broad strength transfer is
stronger in some cited studies than others, while the tendon-specific rationale applies
mainly to the high-velocity case.

# The Isokinetic Training Method: How It Works, and Five Advantages Claimed by Its Early Proponents

**What it is** — The isokinetic method, popular in the USA in the late 1960s and early
1970s, uses a device (e.g. a centrifugal clutch or hydraulic system) that automatically
adjusts resistance to control movement speed and, in principle, keep a maximal load on the
muscles throughout the full working range; the limiting factor is movement speed, not the
magnitude of resistance as in weightlifting, and resistance rises as speed rises. Depending
on the apparatus, isokinetics may be concentric or eccentric, though most commercial
machines of the era offered only the concentric mode; the device's resistance
automatically accommodates each joint angle to the muscle's momentary capability, provided
the athlete sustains sufficient motivation. Counsilman (1971, 1972), an early populariser
for athletic training, claimed the isokinetic method's basic advantage over others is that
it forces maximal-effort work throughout, producing larger, faster strength increases for
particular actions even in very strong athletes; the authors state this specific claim "is
incorrect," since isokinetic force is usually lower than isometric force throughout the
range of joint movement, and maximal strength production is not possible under isokinetic
conditions. Even so, the authors report some isokinetic benefits have been corroborated in
specific sports (swimming, rowing) by considerable research (Chu & Smith, 1971;
Rosentsweig & Hinson, 1972; Hinson & Rosentsweig, 1972; Wilson, 1972), which promote five
advantages: (1) the device accommodates to the athlete's own capability throughout the
range rather than the athlete accommodating to a fixed resistance, automatically adjusting
for fatigue, pain or strength gain and thereby minimising injury risk; (2) prolonged warm-up
becomes largely unnecessary, and a demanding workout is possible in five minutes, with no
per-athlete adjustment needed on team equipment; (3) fewer repetitions can produce
significant strength gains, since each repetition loads the muscle intensively throughout
the full range; (4) real-time results display (computer monitor or graph) lets the athlete
compete against themselves or others; (5) eliminating most eccentric activity on most
isokinetic machines reduces post-exercise soreness. The authors caution that the studies
behind these five points were not conducted with top-level international athletes, and did
not specify training-phase context (general versus specific preparation). Several studies
comparing isometric, dynamic and isokinetic regimes (Thistle et al, 1967; Rosentsweig &
Hinson, 1972) found isokinetic exercise produced the greatest electrical (EMG) activity,
largest muscle hypertrophy, and most effective maintenance of strength for specific muscle
groups in the average person, with more significant strength gains in shorter periods and
shorter sessions than other regimes in laboratory studies; transfer of this effect to
multi-dimensional functional sporting activity, or across athletes of different
qualification, "has not been proved." Isokinetic training also gives limited speed
specificity over some ranges of sporting movement, since the trainer can control joint-action
speed, but cannot reproduce force-time curves matching specific sports movements. (PDF pp.
243-244, printed pp. 232-233, "4.2.6 Isokinetic and Other Training Means," "4.2.6.1 The
Isokinetic Training Method.")

**Why it works** — The authors state the accommodating-resistance mechanism directly
(resistance adjusts automatically to applied force at each joint angle), but immediately
qualify Counsilman's stronger claim (that this guarantees superior, faster strength gains)
as incorrect on physiological grounds, since isokinetic force output is lower than isometric
force at the same joint angle throughout the range.

**When to apply** — Use this framework's five advantages as the historical case FOR
isokinetic training, while treating Counsilman's "forces maximal effort throughout, superior
to other methods" claim as specifically refuted by the authors. Inference: the swimming and
rowing support cited here is consistent with isokinetics being most defensible for sports
whose own movements are already closer to constant-velocity through part of their range (see
[[heuristic--isokinetic-testing-limitations-for-functional-sport-specificity]], which notes
isokinetic testing/training is "non-functional, except possibly for swimmers").

**When NOT to apply** — The cited supporting studies used non-elite athlete samples and did
not report training-phase context, per the authors' own caution; do not extend these
findings to elite/international athletes or to specific-preparation training phases without
separate verification. This entry's advantages should be read alongside
[[claim--isokinetic-devices-cannot-be-truly-isokinetic-physics-limitation]] and
[[heuristic--isokinetic-testing-limitations-for-functional-sport-specificity]], both of which
the authors use to substantially qualify how much practical value these advantages carry for
competitive athletes.

**Example** — The book's own example is the swimming and rowing research cited above (Chu &
Smith 1971; Rosentsweig & Hinson 1972; Hinson & Rosentsweig 1972; Wilson 1972); no further
invented example is added here.

# There Is No Single "Optimal" Quadriceps:Hamstring Ratio: It Changes With Sport, Joint Angle and Test Velocity

**What it is** — It is often claimed the optimal quadriceps-to-hamstring strength ratio is
60:40, but Russian scientists found this ratio depends on the specific sport (Vorobyev,
1978): measured at the joint angle of greatest knee-extension torque, the ratio should be
nearer 80:20 for weightlifters and jumpers, while a treadmill-movement measurement gives
runners a ratio near 50:50. A related popular belief, that injuries become far more common
once left/right lower-extremity strength differs by more than 10%, is also questioned.
Recent research (as of the text) found neither recommendation supported by scientifically
controlled experiments that correct torque outputs for gravity's effect and avoid
stretching the hamstrings during measurement (Stephens & Reid, 1988). The
flexor/extensor-ratio recommendation is additionally vague because the ratio varies across
the joint's range of motion. Verified against the page image (Fig 4.16, printed p. 234,
Cybex dynamometer data at 36, 108 and 180 degrees/second): the ratio at the knee measured
at 80 degrees is about 75:25 at 36 degrees/second but 68:32 at 180 degrees/second; the ratio
equals the traditional 60:40 figure only at approximately 50 degrees of joint angle. Since
the ratio changes with both joint angle and measurement velocity, the authors conclude "it
is meaningless to prescribe an optimal ratio for any joint," and it would be more accurate
to refer to a full-range characteristic curve for a given angular velocity than to a single
number. (PDF pp. 244, 245-246, printed pp. 233-234, "4.2.6.1 The Isokinetic Training
Method," "Recommended Strength Ratios," Fig 4.16.)

**Why it works** — No physiological mechanism is given for why the ratio shifts with sport,
joint angle or velocity beyond the general fact that different muscles' torque-generating
capacity varies differently across the range of motion and across contraction speed, so any
single-number ratio necessarily averages over that variation in a way that hides it.

**When to apply** — Use this as a caution against citing a single flexor/extensor or
left/right strength-ratio target (e.g. "60:40 quad:hamstring," "no more than 10% L/R
difference") as universally optimal or diagnostic of injury risk. If a ratio must be used,
specify the joint angle and angular velocity it was measured at, per this claim, and prefer a
full-range curve over a single number when data allow. **Eric's baseline:** this bears on how
isokinetic or ForceFrame-style ratio data should be interpreted for return-to-play decisions;
the corpus does not document a specific flexor/extensor ratio target
(`method-baseline.md`).

**When NOT to apply** — This is single-source, era-specific data (Vorobyev, 1978; Stephens &
Reid, 1988) using a Cybex dynamometer at three specific velocities on an unspecified
population; the specific ratio-versus-angle values (75:25 at 36 degrees/second, 68:32 at 180
degrees/second) should not be treated as universal constants for every athlete or joint. Do
not use this entry to conclude ratio testing is worthless; the claim is that a single
"optimal ratio" number is misleading, not that ratio measurement itself carries no
information (see [[claim--natural-asymmetry-caution-against-isokinetic-symmetry-correction]]
for the related but distinct caution about using isokinetic ratios to drive "correction").

**Example** — Inference: a rehabilitation report citing "this athlete's quad:hamstring ratio
is below the healthy 60:40 standard" should specify the joint angle and test velocity the
ratio was measured at, per this claim, since the same athlete could show a ratio at or above
60:40 at one angle/velocity and below it at another, without any change in underlying
tissue health.

# No Machine Can Be Truly Isokinetic: The Physics of Acceleration Means Injuries and Elastic Energy Happen Exactly Where Isokinetic Testing Cannot See

**What it is** — The authors call the assumption that isokinetic dynamometers offer
accurately constant angular velocity from start to finish a "faulty basic assumption": the
laws of physics make it impossible, since a body at rest must accelerate to reach a terminal
velocity, sustain it, then decelerate back to rest, meaning every movement (isokinetic or
otherwise) has periods of positive or negative acceleration; producing truly isokinetic
conditions throughout would require infinite acceleration, which "contradicts the laws of
science." Manufacturers can only minimise, never eliminate, these non-isokinetic transition
phases. The authors argue this matters critically because most injuries occur during exactly
these transition phases, when a limb is changing velocity or musculotendinous tension is
changing, and isokinetic testing "can reveal no useful information about what is happening
to the muscles then." A further limitation: isokinetic testing gives no information on the
major contribution of elastic-energy storage and release in soft tissue during dynamic,
natural actions like walking, running, jumping or throwing. The authors' summary: "isokinetic
testing or training is non-functional, except possibly for swimmers whose movements are
approximately isokinetic over part of their range." Biomechanical analysis of force-time and
rate-of-force-development curves for resisted free movement confirms distinct qualities
(maximal strength, starting strength, acceleration strength, explosive strength, see Fig
2.13) that isokinetic curves, being "so radically different from their free movement
equivalents," capture poorly, limiting their value for functional analysis or specific
neuromuscular conditioning. (PDF pp. 244-245, printed pp. 233-234, "4.2.6.2 Limitations of
the Isokinetic Method," "Fundamental Biomechanics of Isokinetic Devices.")

**Why it works** — The mechanism is pure Newtonian mechanics: any object moving from rest,
sustaining a velocity, and returning to rest must accelerate and decelerate, and true
isokinetic motion throughout would demand infinite (physically impossible) acceleration at
the start and end. Because injuries and elastic-energy exchange concentrate in these
non-constant-velocity transition phases (and in general dynamic movement, which is rarely
constant-velocity at all), a device measuring only the constant-velocity middle portion
misses the biomechanically important parts of most natural movements.

**When to apply** — Use this as the physics-level foundation for treating isokinetic test or
training data as descriptive of a narrow, artificial condition (the device's controlled
constant-velocity phase) rather than as a general index of an athlete's dynamic capability.
Inference: this reinforces the terminology point already in this library (see
[[framework--precise-muscle-action-terminology]]) that commercial "isokinetic" devices are
more accurately "quasi-isokinetic."

**When NOT to apply** — The authors' own exception is swimming, where movement is
"approximately isokinetic over part of their range," making isokinetic testing/training
comparatively more relevant there than for running, jumping, throwing or lifting. Do not read
this entry as claiming isokinetic devices provide zero useful information; the claim is
narrower: they provide no useful information specifically about the transition
(acceleration/deceleration) phases where most injuries occur and where elastic energy
storage/release happens, which is where much of natural dynamic movement's biomechanical
significance lies.

**Example** — Inference: a hamstring isokinetic strength test at a fixed angular velocity
(e.g. 60 degrees/second) can characterise force output during that constant-velocity phase,
but per this claim it cannot characterise what happens during the initial acceleration into
that velocity or the deceleration out of it, which is where a sprinting hamstring's peak
eccentric strain and injury risk concentrate.

misleading term for the same physical reason given here),

# Six Reasons Isokinetic Testing and Training Misrepresent Functional Sport Movement

**What it is** — Building on the physics limitation already covered (see
[[claim--isokinetic-devices-cannot-be-truly-isokinetic-physics-limitation]]), the authors
give a detailed, itemised critique of relying on isokinetic dynamometers (e.g. Cybex,
Orthotron) for testing and rehabilitation. (1) **Functional anatomy**: nearby-joint angle
changes the torque produced about the joint in question (e.g. hip angle changes knee
extension torque, important to weightlifters, jumpers and sprinters), but seated isokinetic
testing typically immobilises the hips with straps, creating "non-functional conditions" that
measure torque specific to the seated posture, not any actual sporting posture; testing is
also rarely combined with EMG or myotonometry to identify which specific muscles (e.g.
vastus medialis vs lateralis, affected by lateral/medial rotation) actually contribute to the
measured torque, and open-chain testing (foot not contacting the ground) alters how popliteus
and gastrocnemius contribute to knee flexion, both important in running, lifting and jumping;
seated testing also ignores medial rotation contributions (sartorius, gracilis,
semimembranosus, semitendinosus) and lateral rotation (biceps femoris). (2) **Muscle
physiology**: most sporting actions begin from intense isometric or explosive-isometric
contraction with stored tendon elastic energy, a state isokinetic testing/training involves
only insignificantly; the myotatic stretch reflex (stronger with faster stretch) is central
to explosive running, jumping, lifting and throwing but cannot be recruited under isokinetic
conditions; agonist-antagonist cocontraction and ballistic (preprogrammed,
feedback-independent) movement are both common in sport but isokinetic conditions permit
neither; torque under isokinetic conditions is usually much lower than isometric torque at
the same joint angle, contradicting the claim (Thistle et al, 1967) that isokinetics keeps
tension maximal throughout; reciprocal inhibition (antagonist pre-contraction raising
agonist force, used in PNF and common in plyometric rebounding) is not permitted by most
isokinetic devices; and bilateral versus unilateral force-production patterns differ, so
isokinetic limb-to-limb comparisons "can be very misleading," since a dominant limb's
superiority is often one of skill, not strength (e.g. it is common, but the authors imply
misguided, for therapists to isokinetically strengthen a footballer's weaker kicking limb
when the support leg's job is producing stabilising strength). (3) **Specificity**:
indiscriminate isokinetic use neglects the specificity principle across ten dimensions (type
of muscle contraction, movement pattern, region of movement, velocity of movement, force of
contraction, muscle fibre recruitment, metabolism, biochemical adaptation, flexibility,
fatigue), citing Sale and McDougall (1981) that strength gains are apparent only when
measured during the same movement type used in training; this specificity concern
"culminated" in Verkhoshansky's principle of dynamic correspondence (see 4.3, not yet
ingested in this pass). The authors' concluding analogy: relying on a single isokinetic model
to assess strength and endurance is "tantamount to permanently accepting" the early
planetary-electron atomic model just because it is easy to work with; a more versatile,
"kinesiological polygraph" combining high-speed video, EMG, myotensiometers, accelerometers
and force plates is what multi-faceted human movement actually demands, and sporting
performance itself remains the ultimate test of any training or rehabilitation regime. The
authors also name the APRE system (based on DeLorme's original work, see 5.2.2) as an
inexpensive, effective alternative to isokinetics, and point to Ostering (1986) for a
detailed review of isokinetic strengths and weaknesses. (PDF pp. 245-247, printed pp.
234-236, "4.2.6.2 Limitations of the Isokinetic Method," "Functional Anatomy," "Muscle
Physiology," "The Importance of Specificity," "Concluding Remarks.")

**Why it works** — Each of the six points above carries its own stated mechanism (listed
inline): joint-coupling effects on torque, missing EMG/myotonometry verification, absent
elastic-energy and stretch-reflex contribution, absent cocontraction/ballistic patterning,
absent reciprocal inhibition, and bilateral-versus-unilateral force-pattern differences. The
authors' unifying argument is that isokinetic devices remove or distort each of these
naturally-occurring features of dynamic sport movement, so a test built on removing them
cannot validly characterise performance that depends on them.

**When to apply** — Use this itemised list as a detailed checklist when deciding whether a
specific isokinetic finding (a strength deficit, an asymmetry, a rehabilitation
"clearance" threshold) should influence a functional/sport-specific training or return-to-play
decision: check whether the isokinetic condition tested (seated, seat-strapped, single-joint,
no elastic energy, no stretch reflex, no cocontraction, no reciprocal inhibition, bilateral
comparison) resembles the sporting movement in question closely enough for the finding to
transfer. This heuristic substantially extends, with a Ch4-specific itemised mechanism list,
the caution already recorded in
[[heuristic--isometric-dynamometry-misrepresents-dynamic-functional-strength]] (Ch2, citing
the book's own "futile" statement about isometric/isokinetic dynamometers); a reciprocal link
from that entry to this one is recommended.

**When NOT to apply** — Marked contested, inheriting the same internal tension already
recorded in [[heuristic--isometric-dynamometry-misrepresents-dynamic-functional-strength]]:
the book itself relies on isokinetic and isometric dynamometer data elsewhere (e.g. the
force-velocity curves in Ch3, the strength-ratio data in
[[heuristic--isokinetic-strength-ratios-vary-by-angle-and-velocity]]) to establish real
findings, without fully reconciling that reliance with this section's sweeping "non-functional"
critique. Do not read this entry as claiming isokinetic devices have zero diagnostic value
(the authors themselves call them "useful for measuring isokinetic actions... in two
dimensions"); the claim is that extrapolating isokinetic results to full sporting movement
"is scientifically unacceptable and misleading."

**Example** — Inference: a therapist isokinetically strengthening a footballer's weaker
kicking (non-support) leg, per the authors' own named example, may be targeting the wrong
quality if the support leg's job (stabilising strength under bilateral/unilateral,
cocontractive demand) is actually more functionally relevant to on-field performance than the
kicking leg's isolated, isokinetically-measured torque.

# The Static-Dynamic Method: Pairing a Brief Isometric Hold With Explosive Concentric Work in One Repetition

**What it is** — The static-dynamic method sequentially combines isometric and dynamic
(auxotonic) muscular activity within one exercise. Two variants are given as experimentally
effective: (1) a 2-3 second isometric contraction at 80% of maximum, immediately followed by
explosive concentric dynamic work with a 30%-of-maximum load; (2) a constant load of 75-80%
of maximum used for both the isometric and dynamic components, illustrated with a half-squat
example: the athlete sinks into a half-squat with a barbell on the shoulders, holds that
position for 2 seconds, then jumps upward with maximal acceleration before landing carefully
and repeating. Experimentally, variant (1) proved better than dynamic exercise alone for
developing speed-strength; variant (2) had an effect on speed-strength and absolute strength
improvement roughly equivalent to dynamic exercise alone (Dobrovolsky, 1972, 1973). (PDF p.
247, printed p. 236, "4.2.6.3 Static-Dynamic Methods.")

**Why it works** — No specific mechanism is given for why pairing a brief isometric hold with
subsequent explosive concentric work outperforms dynamic-only training for speed-strength in
variant (1); Inference: this is plausibly related to the same explosive-isometric-to-concentric
transition mechanism already described for depth jumps and the shock method (see
[[framework--kinetic-energy-stimulation-mechanical-basis-and-advantages]] and
[[framework--shock-method-and-plyometric-terminology]]), where the isometric phase primes the
neuromuscular system immediately before the explosive concentric effort, though the authors
do not state this connection explicitly for the static-dynamic method here.

**When to apply** — Use variant (1) (2-3s isometric hold at 80% max, then explosive
concentric at 30% max) when the specific goal is speed-strength development, per the cited
finding that it outperformed dynamic-only training for that quality. Use variant (2) (a
half-squat-and-jump at 75-80% max) as an option with roughly equivalent
speed-strength-and-absolute-strength effect to dynamic training alone, useful mainly for
variety or when the loaded-jump movement pattern itself is desired.

**When NOT to apply** — This is a single-source finding (Dobrovolsky, 1972, 1973) without
reported sample size, training-age context, or long-term outcome data beyond the immediate
comparison to dynamic-only training; treat the specific load percentages (80%/30%, 75-80%)
as the cited protocol's own parameters, not universally optimal figures. Variant (2)'s
half-squat-and-jump with load requires careful technique and landing control, which this
passage does not itself detail as a safety protocol.

**Example** — The book's own example is the half-squat-hold-then-jump description above
(variant 2); no further invented example is added here.

# No Single Training Regime Is "Most Effective": Combining Regimes Consistently Outperforms Any One Alone

**What it is** — Asked which of the chapter's several muscular-work regimes (concentric,
eccentric, isometric, isokinetic, static-dynamic, and combinations) is most effective, the
authors argue a categorical answer is not possible, for three reasons: (1) comprehensive
research comparing all regimes has not been conducted; one study comparing eccentric,
concentric, isometric and combined regimes found some advantage of concentric over eccentric
and isometric methods individually, but the combined-regime method showed "a clear
advantage" over all of them (Pletnev, 1975); (2) comparative research is inherently difficult
because training loads are hard to equate across different regimes; (3) it is
"inappropriate to raise the question of the absolute effectiveness of any specific regime,"
since each regime can be the most effective depending on the training situation, stage of
the annual cycle, athlete level, the primary regime of muscular work demanded by the specific
sport exercise, and the specific strength quality being trained. The authors' overall
conclusion, stated with "reasonable certainty": the most rational way to enhance special
strength preparation is to combine the various regimes of muscular work, a conclusion
corroborated by multiple studies (Vorobyev, 1966; Andrianov & Vorobyev, 1969; Kuznetsov,
1970; Verkhoshansky, 1970, 1972; Slobodyan, 1972; Tatyan, 1974; Pletnev, 1975; Khodykin,
1975; Savin, 1974). Such a combination should be selected by considering its cumulative
effect (see [[framework--acute-delayed-particular-cumulative-training-effect-taxonomy]]) and
its correspondence to the specific conditions of the sport. (PDF pp. 247-248, printed pp.
236-237, "4.2.6.4 Choice of Muscle Training Regimes.")

**Why it works** — No single mechanism is given for why combined regimes outperform any one
regime alone; Inference: a plausible explanation, not stated in the text, is that different
regimes stress different components of the strength system (contractile tissue,
elastic/connective tissue, neural drive, fibre-type-specific adaptation) somewhat
independently, so combining them addresses more of the system than repeatedly stressing only
one component.

**When to apply** — Use this heuristic to resist single-method dogma ("isokinetics is best,"
"only free weights work," "eccentric training is superior") when programming special
strength training; per this section, the authors' own multi-study conclusion favours
deliberately combining regimes (concentric, eccentric, isometric, static-dynamic,
resistance, kinetic-energy) rather than committing to one, selected for cumulative effect
and sport-specific correspondence.

**When NOT to apply** — The nine corroborating citations (Vorobyev 1966 through Savin 1974)
give no individual effect sizes or protocols in this passage; treat "combining regimes is
most rational" as the authors' summary conclusion across a body of era-specific Soviet sport
science, not a single quantified finding. This heuristic does not specify which combination,
in what proportion, or in what sequence to use for a given sport or athlete; it establishes
only the general principle that combination beats any single regime, leaving the specific
combination as a separate programming decision guided by cumulative-effect and
dynamic-correspondence principles (see 4.3, not yet ingested in this pass).

**Example** — Inference: a strength block that mixes free-weight concentric-emphasis lifts,
occasional isometric holds at sticking points, and periodic eccentric-emphasis work (rather
than committing the whole block to one regime) is applying this heuristic directly; the
specific mixture and sequencing would still need to be chosen using the cumulative-effect and
sport-specificity criteria the authors name.

# Two Classes of Training Machine: Functional Resistance (Sport-Specific Pattern) vs Non-Functional Resistance (General Pattern)

**What it is** — The authors distinguish two classes of strength machine: functional
resistance (FR) machines, whose purpose is offering resistance in sport-specific movement
patterns, and non-functional resistance (NFR) machines, whose sole purpose is offering
general resistance in non-sport-specific patterns. They call this distinction vital, because
Western "state-of-the-art" fitness centres are usually equipped almost entirely with NFR
machines, so much so that adequate free weights or FR machines can be hard to find in them;
they quote Zatsiorsky (1995) directly: "the important limitation of many strength training
machines is that they are designed to train muscles, not movement. Because of this, they are
not the most important training tool for athletes." FR devices (guided by Ratov, 1976) are
reported effective for aspects of special strength training across many athlete types
(Semenov, 1970; Petrova & Gorbunov, 1970; Verkhoshansky, 1970; Deniskin & Kuznetsov, 1972;
Dobrovolski, 1972, 1973; Kuznetsov & Aiunts, 1974; Savin, 1974), because they permit strict
regulation of load and the movement's spatial characteristics, letting a coach use the
appropriate regime of muscular work and programme force-over-time characteristics while
reproducing stereotypical training movements economically. The authors' example of a
simple, cheap, versatile FR device is the "hi-lo" pulley machine, whose rope-attached
handles (unlike fixed bars and pivots on most NFR machines) allow sport-specific movement
patterns of great complexity; they estimate a circuit of just four pulley machines can
provide training capacity that would otherwise need more than 40 "state of the art" NFR
machines, and that a circuit of 10 pulley machines plus a few benches, jumping boards, bars,
mats and wall-bars can serve more clients with a larger range of exercises than 100
specialised machines, at under 15% of the cost, though even this pulley-based facility would
give inferior multi-dimensional conditioning compared with an NFR-machine-only facility on
a per-machine basis. A major FR advantage is visual/computerised feedback on movement
quality and quantity for controlling training. The authors caution that simulating a sport
movement against heavy machine loads can still significantly alter the movement's
force-time characteristics and disrupt its neuromuscular programme; functionality is not
automatic from an exercise merely resembling a sport action, but depends on the functional
objective for which the exercise is used and how it is integrated into the long-term
conditioning programme. (PDF pp. 248-249, printed pp. 237-238, "4.2.7 The Use of Training
Machines," "4.2.7.1 Functional Resistance Machines.")

**Why it works** — The stated mechanism is specificity: FR machines let a coach control load
and the spatial/temporal pattern of the movement together, closer to the actual sport
action, while still gaining measurement feedback; NFR machines fix bars/pivots to a single
plane and pattern that need not resemble any sport movement, training "muscles, not
movement" per the quoted Zatsiorsky line.

**When to apply** — Use this framework to audit a training facility or programme for FR
versus NFR balance, per the authors' concern that Western commercial gyms over-provide NFR
equipment. Prefer FR devices (pulley systems, cable machines with free movement paths,
purpose-built sport-simulation devices) when the training goal requires reproducing a
sport-specific spatial/temporal pattern with measurable load control.

**When NOT to apply** — The authors' own caution applies: using any machine, FR or NFR, to
simulate a sporting movement against heavy load can still disrupt the movement's natural
force-time characteristics and neuromuscular programming; resemblance to a sport action does
not automatically make an exercise "functional." See
[[heuristic--nfr-machine-limitations-safety-and-appropriate-use]] for when NFR machines
remain appropriate despite this section's general preference for FR devices and free
weights.

**Example** — The book's own example is the "hi-lo" pulley machine and the four-pulley
versus forty-NFR-machine cost/capacity comparison described above; no further invented
example is added here.

# NFR Machines: Real Limitations on Proprioception and Joint Loading, but Not Actually Safer Than Free Weights

**What it is** — Non-functional resistance (NFR) machines can play a useful supplementary
role but generally cannot match free weights and FR machines for multi-dimensional,
full-range neuromuscular and musculoskeletal training. Many NFR machines force the user to
sit or lie, minimising training effect on major stabilising muscles and involving fewer
joints through fewer degrees of freedom; while potentially valuable early in rehabilitation,
this is an incomplete way of training the body for functional development, since machines
that stabilise major body regions and limit the number of moving joints diminish the
capacity to train balance, coordination and proprioception. The authors give a worked
example: a free-standing squat involves three joints (hip, knee, ankle), while a seated knee
extension machine constrains the body to one joint (knee) to exercise the quadriceps, so for
a comparable exercise intensity over the same range of motion, the machine increases
shearing force across the knee; the squat additionally exercises the thigh adductors, spinal
erectors and other stabilisers, and enhances balance and (via longitudinal compressive
loading) bone density. Seated exercise also always imposes a greater lumbar-disc load than
equivalent standing exercise: maintaining a neutral spinal curve while seated raises lumbar
disc pressure by about 40% (Chaffin & Andersson, 1984); allowing the back to flex forward can
raise this by as much as 90%; a well-designed lumbar-supporting backrest lowers the stress
but lumbar disc pressure can still run about 75% higher than standing; stress increases
further against resistance, especially with jerky movements, and seated positions cannot
absorb shock loading via knee/hip/ankle flexion the way standing can. On safety
specifically, the authors reject the common claim that NFR machines are much safer than free
weights: injuries to the back and knees (leg press, hack-squat, hip-sled), shoulders and
chest (bench press, pec-deck), elbow (bicep curl machines) and back (back-extension
machines) are "just as common with machines as with free weights," and many machines (pec
deck, bench press, leg press, calf raise, seated press) force the movement to start from the
biomechanically least efficient, most harmful position for the relevant joint; spotters are
just as necessary for machines as for free weights, and faulty technique is equally dangerous
with either. Some machines (leg extension, lat pulley, seated bicep curl) genuinely are safer
for someone with back weakness or injury, and can help in early rehabilitation, but
"continual avoidance of stress to the regions involved eventually leads to their progressive
weakening" if relied on long-term. NFR machines remain useful for isolating specific muscle
groups (bodybuilding-style supplementary training) or for the primary stages of injury
rehabilitation, but not as an all-round conditioning replacement for free weights and FR
devices; one exception noted is the lat-pulley machine, generally more suitable and safer
than the free-weight equivalent (bent-over rowing) for training latissimus dorsi. (PDF pp.
249-250, printed pp. 238-240, "4.2.7.2 Non-Functional Resistance Machines," "4.2.7.4 The
Training Safety of Machines.")

**Why it works** — The proprioception/stability mechanism is that fixing more joints and
supporting more of the body's weight through the machine itself removes the balance,
coordination and multi-joint stabilisation demand that free, standing exercise imposes. The
single-joint-versus-multi-joint mechanism is straightforward: constraining a comparable
training stimulus to fewer joints concentrates the same relative stress onto those joints'
structures (illustrated by the squat-versus-knee-extension shearing-force example). The
lumbar-disc mechanism is postural: seated positions load the discs differently than standing,
and cannot use lower-body joint flexion to absorb shock the way standing positions can.

**When to apply** — Use NFR machines for early-stage rehabilitation of a specific injured
joint or muscle group, for supplementary bodybuilding-style isolation work, or when back
weakness/injury makes a machine (leg extension, lat pulley, seated bicep curl) genuinely
safer than the free-weight equivalent. **Eric's baseline:** the department's testing
protocols already run standard CMJ family tests and mid-thigh pull on ForceDecks rather than
seated machine-based strength testing (`method-baseline.md`, "Force plate and neuromuscular
monitoring"), consistent with this section's general preference for standing, multi-joint,
free assessment over seated/constrained machine testing.

**When NOT to apply** — Do not rely on NFR machines long-term as a substitute for free
weights or FR devices in a healthy, competitive athlete's programme, since progressive
avoidance of stress to a region "eventually leads to... progressive weakening." Do not
assume NFR machines are inherently safer than free weights; per the authors' own comparison,
injury rates and biomechanically poor starting positions are comparable across both,
technique quality and spotting matter more than equipment choice.

**Example** — Inference: using a seated knee-extension machine during the first two weeks
after a knee injury (early rehabilitation, single-joint control) while progressing toward a
free-standing squat as tolerance improves applies this heuristic's appropriate-use window; continuing
to rely on the knee-extension machine as the primary quadriceps exercise once the athlete
returns to full training would not, per the "progressive weakening" caution above.

# Variable-Resistance Machines' Core Marketing Claim Rests on a Physics Error, and NFR Machines Need Many More Units to Match Free Weights

**What it is** — Many machine manufacturers design variable-resistance machines to match a
joint's natural torque curve, claiming superiority over free weights and other resistance
devices on this basis. The authors identify a flawed premise behind this claim: manufacturers
imply a free weight's resistance stays constant throughout a lift (e.g. a 50 kg biceps curl
is presented as exerting a constant 50 kg / about 490 Newton force from start to finish). A
simple application of Newton's Second Law (force proportional to acceleration) shows this is
false: the resistance a free weight offers changes with acceleration from rest and at any
point the trainee speeds up or slows down, "as can easily be demonstrated by force plate,
cable tensiometer or high speed video analysis"; a skilled trainee already uses proprioception
to perceive these changes and adjust resistance and direction more efficiently, the authors
argue, than current machines can. Separately, on training efficiency, the authors give
several specific machine-count comparisons: it takes at least 12 NFR machines to
approximately equal the training effect of the clean-and-push-press, at least 4 to rival the
free-standing squat, more than 3 to equal the standing dumbbell curl, and at least 5 to
rival the flexed-knee deadlift; in total, more than 24 NFR machines are required to match the
combined training effect of just four free-weight exercises (clean-and-push-press, squat,
deadlift, biceps curl), at roughly 40 times the combined cost of the equivalent free weights
and racks, with NFR machines also carrying higher maintenance costs and more frequent
breakdowns in a well-used gymnasium than "virtually indestructible" free weights. (PDF pp.
250-251, printed pp. 239-240, "4.2.7.3 Machines and the Variable Resistance Philosophy,"
"4.2.7.5 The Efficiency of Machine Training.")

**Why it works** — For the variable-resistance critique, the mechanism is Newtonian: force
equals mass times acceleration, so any free weight's true resistance already varies with the
trainee's own acceleration/deceleration throughout the lift, contradicting the marketing
premise that free weights offer constant resistance needing a machine's correction. For the
machine-count comparison, the implied mechanism is that a free-weight compound exercise
recruits and trains many more muscle groups, movement patterns and stabilising demands
simultaneously than any single-joint NFR machine, so many NFR machines are needed in
combination to approach the same total training stimulus.

**When to apply** — Use the Newtonian-mechanics point directly to counter variable-resistance
machine marketing claims that free weights offer "constant" resistance needing a machine's
correction; a skilled lifter's own proprioceptive control of bar speed already varies the
true resistance throughout the lift (see also
[[framework--compensatory-acceleration-training]], Ch1). Use the machine-count/cost
comparison when evaluating equipment-purchase decisions for a training facility: a small set
of free-weight stations and racks may replicate the combined training effect of a much
larger, more expensive NFR machine circuit, per this section's specific figures.

**When NOT to apply** — The specific machine-count figures (12, 4, 3+, 5, 24+, roughly 40x
cost) are the authors' own estimates for approximating specific free-weight exercises' total
training effect with combinations of NFR machines; they do not cite a controlled study
establishing these exact ratios, so treat them as illustrative rather than precisely
measured. This entry does not argue variable-resistance machines have zero value, only that
their core "free weights are constant resistance" marketing premise is physically incorrect;
some variable-resistance designs may still offer useful features (feedback display, joint
isolation for rehabilitation) independent of that specific flawed premise.

**Example** — Inference: a facility manager comparing the cost of building a small free-weight
area (barbells, plates, a rack, a platform) against an equivalent machine circuit covering
the same major lift patterns should expect, per this section's estimates, the free-weight
option to require far fewer total pieces of equipment and far lower combined cost.

resistance already varies via the lifter's own acceleration).

# Chasing Perfect Bilateral Symmetry Is Ill-Advised: Sprinters' Push-Off and Swing Legs Are Naturally, Usefully Asymmetric

**What it is** — This entry fulfils the forward reference in this library's Ch3.3.4.4 entry
(see
[[claim--natural-asymmetry-caution-against-isokinetic-symmetry-correction]]), which deferred
"The Concept of Symmetric Training" to this section. Asymmetry in leg length, muscle
strength, stride length and other structural/functional features has been blamed for
inefficient movement and injury, and imbalances in agonist/antagonist and left/right strength
(including isokinetically-measured muscular endurance imbalances) have been singled out as
leading causes of muscle injury. However, research shows isokinetically-measured left/right
leg-strength or agonist/antagonist discrepancies as large as 10% do not statistically
correlate with a major increase in muscular injuries. Because the body, its muscles, tendons
and bones are characteristically asymmetric, attempts to force greater anthropomorphic and
kinesiological symmetry "could exacerbate the existing problem or cause new injuries";
assessing asymmetry properly requires both static structural measurement (leg length, spinal
curvature) and dynamic biomechanical analysis (force distribution, movement patterns, ground
reaction force, postural/phasic muscle EMG, movement-phase timing). Structural differences
in muscle/tendon length, tendon and ligament attachment sites, and bone size/mass are
"quite natural in all humans"; surgical or orthotic correction is warranted only when such
structural differences cause serious functional movement imbalances, and even then only
after confirming the asymmetry is primarily structural rather than a centrally-stored
neuromuscular pattern (in which case the answer is modifying the motor skill itself, not
correcting the body). There is "no perfect way" to execute any movement; style is the
individualised expression of a motor skill and should be developed by observing and
experimenting against an "ideal" model until the athlete's own central nervous programme
fits their body, rather than enforcing one rigid technique standard. Yushkevich (1989)
analysed leg-strength symmetry in sprinters and concluded striving for full leg symmetry is
inappropriate: extensors of all joints of the push-off leg were generally significantly
stronger than the swing leg's, with no consistent flexor-strength difference between legs.
Asymmetry patterns differed by qualification level: novices showed stronger push-off-leg hip
flexors than swing-leg; novice, Class 3 and Class 2 athletes showed stronger swing-leg knee
flexors, while Class 1, Master and higher-qualified athletes showed stronger push-off-leg
knee flexors; dorsiflexion was stronger in the push-off leg for all athletes except novices.
Leg-strength asymmetry increased with sprint proficiency, peaking among Class 2 athletes,
then diminishing again with further mastery. Good sprint times correlated more closely with
swing-leg strength than push-off-leg strength. Yushkevich's conclusion: not only is
symmetrical muscle strength a misguided target, but deliberately asymmetric exercise
(running or jumping on sloping/inclined tracks, running curves in both directions, running or
jumping in shoes with soles of different thickness, or running with unevenly-weighted legs)
can be beneficial, since "asymmetric exercises destabilise the space-time characteristics of
motor acts, thereby helping athletes break speed barriers." The authors close with an analogy
to the tradition that Eastern-carpet weavers deliberately include at least one flaw, since
"perfection is reserved for the Divine," implying similar asymmetry or imperfection may be
desirable in human physical performance and training generally. (PDF pp. 251-252, printed pp.
240-241, "4.2.8 The Concept of Symmetric Training.")

**Why it works** — No cellular or biomechanical mechanism is given for why swing-leg strength
predicts sprint time better than push-off-leg strength, or for why asymmetry peaks at Class 2
then declines with further mastery; the authors report these as empirical findings from
Yushkevich (1989) without deriving them from a stated causal model. For the general "why
symmetry-forcing backfires" claim, the implied mechanism is that the body's natural asymmetry
already reflects functional adaptation (dominant-limb skill specialisation, individualised
structure), so imposing uniformity fights that adaptation rather than supporting it.

**When to apply** — Use this claim, together with the Ch3.3.4.4 entry it completes (see
Related), as the book's full position on symmetry testing and correction: a numeric L/R or
agonist/antagonist discrepancy up to about 10% (isokinetically measured) is not itself
evidence of elevated injury risk, and deliberately asymmetric training elements (uneven
surfaces, directional bias, unevenly weighted limbs) may have a specific rationale for sprint
athletes rather than being technique flaws to eliminate. **Eric's baseline:** this bears
directly on how the department should interpret force-plate symmetry data, which the HD ODSF
framework lists only as an optional reporting category (`method-baseline.md`, "Force plate
and neuromuscular monitoring," citing `hd-odsf-metrics.md`); this entry supports treating
symmetry as descriptive/monitoring information rather than an automatic correction target.

**When NOT to apply** — The Yushkevich (1989) sprinter data are single-study, era-specific
findings from a Soviet sprint-training research programme with an unspecified sample size in
this passage; the specific qualification-level patterns (novice through Master) and the
Class-2 asymmetry peak should not be assumed to generalise to other sports or populations
without separate verification. The 10%-discrepancy-does-not-correlate-with-injury finding
does not mean no discrepancy threshold matters; it means this specific threshold, measured
isokinetically, was not found to correlate in the cited research. Do not read the
"deliberately asymmetric training" recommendation as license to ignore genuine
functional/structural imbalances that are causing injury or performance-limiting compensation
(the authors' own scoping condition above); the recommendation applies specifically to
sprint-specific push-off/swing-leg asymmetry, not to every kind of asymmetry.

**Example** — The book's own example is the Yushkevich (1989) sprinter data described above;
no further invented example is added here.

# "Muscle Isolation" Works at Low Loads, but Heavy Resistance Recruits Stabilisers Until Isolation Is Essentially Impossible

**What it is** — Generations of bodybuilding-magazine authors have advocated specific
machines, joint-fixation methods, precise limb placement and other techniques claimed to
maximally recruit one muscle group while others stay "largely dormant." EMG studies confirm
isolation of small muscle groups is genuinely possible under low loading intensity
(Basmajian, 1978): low-intensity actions like eye-blinking, piano-playing or relaxed speech
rely on precise, isolated action of specific small muscle groups. However, once a large load
is imposed on a target muscle group by intense resistance, isolation becomes "virtually
impossible": as soon as the load is applied, stabilising muscles engage to keep the body or
specific joints stable while prime movers cope with the load, and if the nervous system
signals the prime movers are inadequate, assistant movers, shunt muscles, emergency muscles
and neutralisers may all be recruited to help. The authors' worked example: during heavy
standing triceps push-downs on a cable machine, the abdominals, latissimus dorsi and erector
spinae may contract nearly as strongly as the triceps, despite this being nominally a
triceps-isolation exercise; analysis of many seated or reclining "isolation" machine
exercises shows isolation simply does not occur under heavy load, even when joints are
restrained (as on an isokinetic test machine). This is the same load-dependent
proximal/distal recruitment shift already noted earlier in the chapter (see
[[claim--muscle-recruitment-consistency-and-proximal-distal-shift-with-load]]), where the
authors explicitly forward-referenced this section. This does not negate the value of
isolation-style exercises: they can be useful in early injury rehabilitation, and the
overflow to other muscle groups at higher loads can itself be desirable (if performed
safely), since it simultaneously offers isometric and dynamic training to those other muscle
groups. The authors' stated purpose for this section is to correct the "misleading concept
of muscle isolation" still promoted by many instructors and training texts. (PDF p. 252,
printed p. 241, "4.2.9 The Concept of Muscle Isolation.")

**Why it works** — The mechanism given is neuromuscular: as external load rises, the central
nervous system recruits whatever additional muscle groups (stabilisers, assistant movers,
emergency muscles, neutralisers) are needed to keep the targeted joint and the body stable
and to complete the movement, since a single "isolated" muscle group's own capacity is
insufficient against a heavy load; at low loads, this recruitment is unnecessary, so true
isolation (confirmed by EMG) is achievable.

**When to apply** — Use this claim to correctly interpret what a "muscle isolation" exercise
is actually training at a given load: at rehabilitation-level, low-intensity loading, genuine
isolation is achievable and useful for targeted, precise re-education of a specific muscle
(consistent with [[claim--irradiation-effect-raises-prime-mover-force-and-endurance]]'s
adjacent finding that effort and tension spread between muscle groups). At hypertrophy- or
strength-level heavy loading, expect and potentially embrace the overflow to stabilisers and
assistant movers as additional, simultaneous isometric/dynamic training for those muscle
groups rather than treating it as a programming failure.

**When NOT to apply** — Do not describe a heavy-load exercise (a heavy cable push-down, a
heavy leg-extension set) as "isolating" the target muscle group in marketing, coaching cues,
or programme design; per this claim and its EMG evidence, that description is only accurate
at low training intensities. Do not assume the specific triceps push-down example
generalises identically to every "isolation" exercise; the authors give it as one worked
illustration, not a universally quantified finding across exercises.

**Example** — The book's own example is the heavy triceps push-down engaging abdominals,
latissimus dorsi and erector spinae described above; no further invented example is added
here.
