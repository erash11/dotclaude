    14	# Eccentric Force Can Exceed Isometric Strength by 50-100%, Which Raises Injury Risk
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Force in the yielding (eccentric/plyometric) phase of a motion "can easily
    17	exceed the maximal isometric strength of an athlete by 50 to 100%," and the same holds true
    18	for isolated muscles, where "the eccentric force for a single muscle may reach a level of
    19	up to twice the zero velocity (isometric) force" (printed p. 33). Eccentric forces rise
    20	substantially with initial increases in joint movement velocity, then remain essentially
    21	constant with further velocity increases (figure 2.14): mainly true for qualified
    22	athletes and multijoint motions such as leg extension; the authors separately note that in
    23	untrained persons, per "recently published data," maximal voluntary eccentric knee
    24	extension/flexion torque is independent of movement velocity and stays at an isometric
    25	level. If the same external force is developed both concentrically and eccentrically, fewer
    26	muscle fibers are activated eccentrically, so EMG activity is lower in eccentric actions
    27	than concentric ones at matched force (printed p. 34). A worked example: the maximal
    28	isometric grip force of male weightlifters, measured with a dynamometer, is typically under
    29	1,000 N; an athlete lifting a 250-kg barbell applies a maximal instantaneous force of "well
    30	over 4,000 N" to the bar, meaning roughly 2,000 N per arm: double the grip dynamometer
    31	ceiling: yet the athlete sustains the lift "without extending the grip" (printed p. 34).
    32	Because eccentric exercises involve high force development, injury risk is elevated even
    33	when the eccentric force is submaximal; downhill running is given as an example that can
    34	"easily induce delayed muscle soreness, especially in unprepared athletes," caused by
    35	damaged muscle fibers, though "a small magnitude of the damage is considered by some
    36	experts a normal precursor to the adaptation of muscle to increased use." Conditioning
    37	muscle reduces injury amount (printed p. 34). (PDF pp. 43-44, printed pp. 34-35.)
    38	
    39	**Why it works** — Inference: the authors do not state the cross-bridge/sliding-filament
    40	mechanism for why eccentric actions produce higher force at lower metabolic/neural cost
    41	(fewer active fibers per given force) in this passage; this is consistent with, but not
    42	explained by, the force-velocity relationship covered elsewhere in this chapter (see
    43	[[framework--maximal-muscular-performance-and-parametric-relations]]) and in this library's
    44	Supertraining entries on muscle mechanics.
    45	
    46	**When to apply** — As a quantified, citable rationale for treating eccentric loading
    47	(downhill running, eccentric-emphasis lifting, drop landings) as a distinct injury-risk
    48	category requiring its own progression, separate from concentric/isometric strength
    49	qualification. Inference: the grip-strength example is a useful coaching illustration that
    50	an athlete's ability to sustain a lift does not require the grip muscles to be working
    51	maximally in the sense a dynamometer test would show, since the mechanical demand (holding
    52	position under eccentric-capable tension) differs from a maximal concentric or isometric
    53	grip effort.
    54	
    55	**When NOT to apply** — This is a single-textbook synthesis citing unnamed "recently
    56	published data" for the untrained-eccentric-velocity-independence claim (printed p. 34);
    57	treat the 50-100% and "up to twice" figures as the range the authors report, not as a fixed
    58	individual multiplier: do not compute a specific athlete's eccentric capacity as exactly
    59	1.5-2.0x their tested isometric max without direct eccentric testing. Inference: "a small
    60	magnitude of damage... a normal precursor to adaptation" is reported as some experts'
    61	view, not the authors' own asserted mechanism; do not treat controlled muscle damage as a
    62	required or recommended training stimulus on this text's authority alone.
    63	
    64	**Example** — Downhill running as unprepared-athlete DOMS trigger (printed p. 34, the
    65	authors' own example): even without near-maximal eccentric force, repeated eccentric
    66	loading in an athlete not conditioned to it produces delayed soreness from fiber damage,
    67	illustrating that "not maximal" does not mean "not risky" for eccentric work specifically.
    68

    14	# Four Reasons the Stretch-Shortening Cycle Increases Force, and Why Elite Athletes Store Energy in Tendons
    15	
    16	**What it is** — If a muscle shortens immediately after being stretched (the
    17	stretch-shortening cycle, SSC, "reversible muscular action"), force and power output
    18	increase and energy expenditure decreases versus a shortening-only (concentric) action. The
    19	authors give four reasons for the increased force in the shortening phase: (1) at the peak
    20	of the cycle (the lengthening-to-shortening transition), force is developed under
    21	isometric-like conditions, so Fmm rather than the lower, velocity-limited Fm is exerted;
    22	(2) because force begins rising during the eccentric phase, the time available for force
    23	development is longer than in a pure concentric effort: countermovement jumps, not drop
    24	jumps, are given as the authors' example of this mechanism; (3) **peripheral** (muscle and
    25	tendon elasticity): stretched muscle or tendon stores elastic (strain) energy proportional
    26	to the applied force and the induced deformation, which is recoiled during the concentric
    27	phase (figure 2.16: stiffer bodies store less energy per unit force, more compliant bodies
    28	store more, for the same force); and (4) **central** (neural/reflex): the myotatic
    29	(stretch) reflex adds a positive, force-increasing contribution from spindle discharge
    30	during stretch, while the Golgi tendon reflex adds a negative (inhibitory) contribution
    31	that limits force when tension rises sharply: the net SSC force enhancement is the
    32	combination of a muscle-tendon-elasticity component and these two opposing
    33	length-feedback/force-feedback neural components (figure 2.18, from Houk 1974 via Komi
    34	1986). Tendon stiffness is constant; muscle stiffness varies with the force exerted: the
    35	more tension, the stiffer the active muscle. In elite athletes the active muscle's
    36	stiffness can exceed the tendon's stiffness, meaning the tendon deforms more than the
    37	muscle under load and so stores most of the elastic energy: the authors note fast-running
    38	animals such as horses have "short, strong muscles and lengthy, compliant tendons" that
    39	"work as springs" for exactly this reason (figure 2.17, printed p. 35). (PDF pp. 44-46,
    40	printed pp. 34-36, 38.)
    41	
    42	**Why it works** — The authors state the mechanism directly across the four listed reasons
    43	(force-time history, elastic energy storage, and the myotatic/Golgi neural interplay), so
    44	this is the source's own stated mechanism rather than this entry's inference, except where
    45	marked.
    46	
    47	**When to apply** — As the mechanistic basis for why plyometric/reactive training targets a
    48	different quality than either pure concentric strength training or static flexibility work
    49	(see [[claim--eccentric-force-exceeds-concentric-and-isometric-force]] for the elevated
    50	eccentric force capacity this cycle exploits, and
    51	[[claim--reversible-muscular-action-is-a-trainable-skill-not-automatic]] for why this
    52	capacity does not transfer automatically from heavy lifting). Inference: the muscle-vs-tendon
    53	stiffness relationship (figure 2.17) supports why tendon stiffness/compliance, not just
    54	muscle strength, is a relevant training target for reactive-strength qualities in elite
    55	athletes specifically.
    56	
    57	**When NOT to apply** — Inference: the four-reason breakdown is presented as the authors'
    58	synthesis of separately-cited mechanisms (Komi 1986 for the neural figure; general
    59	elasticity physics for figures 2.16-2.17), not as a single unified, independently-tested
    60	model; treat each of the four contributions as separately-supported components rather than
    61	a fully integrated, quantitatively-partitioned account of SSC force enhancement. Do not
    62	assume the horse-tendon analogy transfers quantitatively to human athletic populations; it
    63	is offered as an illustrative comparison, not a measured human finding.
    64	
    65	**Example** — The authors' own comparison: countermovement jumps are evidence of the
    66	first mechanism (force developed isometrically at the stretch-shorten transition, and a
    67	longer available time for force development than a static-start jump): this is why
    68	squat-jump (static start) performance and countermovement-jump performance are trained and
    69	interpreted as distinct qualities, consistent with force-plate practice that runs both
    70	variants separately.
    71

    14	# Reversible Muscular Action (SSC Skill) Must Be Specifically Trained, Not Assumed From Strength
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Reversible muscular action (using the stretch-shortening cycle
    17	effectively) is innate to some movements (e.g. running takeoffs, "a spring in the leg")
    18	but must be learned for others, such as throwing. Because many sport movements are complex
    19	and executed briefly, "even some elite athletes fail to perform this reversible muscular
    20	action correctly" (printed p. 35). The authors state that before 1960, SSC training was
    21	accidental: improvement was a byproduct of other exercises; only since drop jumps and
    22	similar exercises were incorporated into training has SSC capacity been trained
    23	deliberately (they note the training method "has been erroneously called plyometrics by
    24	some," since the reversible, not strictly eccentric, muscular action is the actual training
    25	objective). The direct claim: "maximal muscular strength (Fmm) and forces produced in fast
    26	reversible muscular action (Fm) are not correlated in good athletes and should be treated,
    27	and trained, as separate motor abilities": as evidence, "even world-class weightlifters
    28	cannot compete with triple jumpers in drop jumping" because, absent specific training, the
    29	Golgi tendon reflex inhibits extensor activity during landing even in very strong athletes,
    30	capping how much force they can sustain without a compensating drop in output; specific
    31	training inhibits that reflex, letting a trained athlete sustain high landing forces without
    32	a force decrease, which is also why dropping height can then be increased. In beginners,
    33	performance in SSC-type exercises can be improved through other exercises such as heavy
    34	weightlifting; in qualified athletes, "this skill is very specific" and drop-jump
    35	performance does not improve from usual strength exercising alone, even with heavy weights
    36	(figure 2.19, comparing 24 weeks of heavy-resistance vs. explosive/power training on
    37	drop-jump height by dropping height: Hakkinen & Komi 1985). (PDF pp. 47-48, printed pp.
    38	38-39.)
    39	
    40	**Why it works** — The stated mechanism is neural: the Golgi tendon reflex normally
    41	inhibits extensor muscle activity when tension rises sharply during landing, protecting the
    42	muscle-tendon unit; specific SSC/drop-jump training desensitizes or inhibits that reflex
    43	response so the athlete can sustain higher landing forces without the reflex-driven force
    44	drop. This is the authors' own stated mechanism, not this entry's inference.
    45	
    46	**When to apply** — As direct justification for programming drop jumps, depth jumps, or
    47	other SSC-specific exercises as their own training category, separate from and not
    48	substitutable by heavy strength training, once an athlete is past the beginner stage where
    49	general strength work still transfers. **Eric's baseline:** training time is deliberately
    50	weighted toward high motor-unit-recruitment core/ballistic movements including jumps
    51	(`method-baseline.md`, "Strength and power," citing
    52	`01192022-hp5355-class1-transcript.md p.13`; `week1-lecture.md p.31`); Inference: this
    53	claim supports keeping jump/SSC-specific work in the program as its own category for
    54	qualified athletes rather than assuming continued heavy-strength gains will carry over to
    55	reactive/SSC qualities.
    56	
    57	**When NOT to apply** — Inference: the 1960-pre/post historical claim about accidental vs.
    58	deliberate SSC training is presented without a specific citation in this passage; treat it
    59	as the authors' historical framing, not a verified dated fact. The figure 2.19
    60	Hakkinen-and-Komi comparison is cited as supporting evidence but is a single study (n=11
    61	heavy-resistance group, n=10 explosive-training group); do not treat "heavy strength
    62	training does not improve drop-jump performance in qualified athletes" as settled beyond
    63	this cited study's population and protocol. Do not use "even world-class weightlifters
    64	cannot compete with triple jumpers in drop jumping" to argue that strength training has no
    65	value for reactive-strength athletes generally: the claim is about specific-skill
    66	transfer, not about strength training's value for other qualities that also support jump
    67	performance (peak force capacity, injury resilience).
    68	
    69	**Example** — The authors' own contrast: a beginner's drop-jump-type performance can still
    70	improve from general heavy weightlifting (transfer exists at low training age), but a
    71	qualified athlete's drop-jump performance requires drop-jump-specific training and does not
    72	improve further from added heavy-resistance work alone (figure 2.19a vs. 2.19b).
    73

    14	# Joint Strength Curves, Sticking Points, and the Moment-Arm Transformation to End-Point Force
    15	
    16	**What it is** — A **strength curve** plots the external force (or moment of force) an
    17	athlete can exert at a given motion against body posture/joint angle. Fmm is a function of
    18	muscular force that undergoes two transformations: muscle-tendon force → joint moment
    19	(torque, M = Fd, where d is the moment arm) → external end-point force (strength). Single-
    20	joint strength curves take three general shapes: ascending, descending, or concave (figure
    21	2.21). Worked examples (Zatsiorsky & Raitsin, 1973; n=24): elbow flexion strength peaks
    22	near 90° (about 100% of Fmm) and falls to roughly 46-50% near full extension/flexion
    23	(figure 2.22a); shoulder flexion strength is highest with the arm slightly behind the trunk
    24	(around −30°, ~100%) and falls to about 37% at 150° (figure 2.22b). The weakest position in
    25	a joint's range is the **sticking point**: the heaviest weight liftable through a full
    26	range of motion cannot exceed the strength at the sticking point, even though other
    27	positions in the range are stronger. Muscle length itself changes tension via two factors:
    28	overlapping actin/myosin cross-bridge availability, and the contribution of elastic
    29	(mainly parallel elastic) components: as a general rule (with a few exceptions, e.g. the
    30	rectus femoris in some cyclists) muscles exert smaller tension at smaller lengths and higher
    31	tension when stretched. Moment arm also changes with joint angle: the text's own example is
    32	the biceps brachii (long head), whose moment arm was 11.5 mm at 180° (full extension) and
    33	45.5 mm at 90° (elbow flexion): a fourfold difference: meaning that if muscle tension
    34	were identical at both angles, the moment of force (and thus external strength) would
    35	change fourfold purely from the moment-arm change (printed p. 43, figure 2.24). For
    36	two-joint muscles (e.g. gastrocnemius), Fmm at one joint depends on the angular position of
    37	the *other* joint the muscle crosses: when the knee is maximally flexed and the ankle
    38	plantarflexed, the shortened gastrocnemius cannot produce active plantar-flexion force: a
    39	position usable for selectively training the soleus instead. (PDF pp. 49-54, printed pp.
    40	40-44.)
    41	
    42	**Why it works** — The mechanism is explicitly two-part and stated directly by the authors:
    43	(1) muscle tension varies with muscle length (cross-bridge overlap plus elastic-component
    44	contribution), and (2) the moment arm of that muscle force varies with joint angle
    45	independent of tension itself; strength at any joint angle is their product (figure 2.24: a
    46	muscle tension-angle curve × a moment-arm-angle curve = the resulting joint torque curve).
    47	
    48	**When to apply** — As a quantified, citable rationale for coaching cues about
    49	range-specific loading and for anticipating where in a lift's range an athlete will be
    50	weakest (the sticking point), useful for accommodating-resistance programming (bands,
    51	chains) or partial-range strength work targeted at a known sticking point. **Eric's
    52	baseline:** this complements, rather than duplicates,
    53	[[supertraining--verkhoshansky/principle--torque-optimization-in-lifting-technique]]
    54	(bar-path/torque-minimization coaching cues) and
    55	[[supertraining--verkhoshansky/claim--joint-angle-and-limb-position-changes-force-output]]
    56	(a separate set of joint-angle findings, e.g. forearm pronation, trunk flexion, head tilt,
    57	from later in Supertraining); this entry supplies the moment-arm mechanism (the
    58	11.5-to-45.5-mm biceps example, fourfold difference) underlying why those position effects
    59	occur, which neither Supertraining entry itself derives from the muscle-length/moment-arm
    60	product shown here.
    61	
    62	**When NOT to apply** — Inference: the specific curve shapes and percentages (elbow flexion
    63	peak ~90°, shoulder flexion peak near −30°, the 11.5/45.5 mm biceps figures) are
    64	single-study data (Zatsiorsky & Raitsin, 1973 and 1992) from a specific population (n=24);
    65	do not assume they transfer exactly to a different joint, muscle, or population without
    66	verification. The rectus femoris exception (higher tension at shorter length, in some
    67	cyclists) is noted as an exception to the general muscle-length rule, not a contradiction of
    68	it: do not overgeneralize either the rule or its exception.
    69	
    70	**Example** — The pull-up supination example (printed p. 44, the authors' own): gymnasts
    71	performing pull-ups on rings always supinate the arms while flexing the elbows, though
    72	nobody teaches them to; the movement pattern is simply more mechanically convenient. This
    73	illustrates athletes self-organizing technique to work with, not against, their own joint
    74	strength curves and moment-arm geometry (see
    75	[[heuristic--athletes-self-organize-technique-to-minimize-secondary-moments]] for the
    76	related self-organization claim about secondary/antagonist moments in the same example).

    14	# Five Types of Mechanical Resistance, and Why Equipment Choice Can Break Sport-Movement Specificity
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Resistance training equipment can be classified by the physical law
    17	governing the resistance it provides: **elasticity** (F = k₁D; force proportional to
    18	displacement/deformation D, coefficient k₁ = stiffness: springs, stretch cords, rubber
    19	bands; force is greatest at the end of range, when deformation is largest); **inertia**
    20	(Newton's second law, F = ma; force proportional to the mass being accelerated: hard to
    21	isolate purely inertial resistance outside lab devices like an inertia wheel, since gravity
    22	and friction intrude); **weight** (F = W + ma, W = object weight, a = vertical
    23	acceleration); **hydrodynamic resistance** (F = k₂V², proportional to velocity squared:
    24	swimming, rowing, kayaking); and **viscosity-based resistance** (F = k₃V, proportional to
    25	velocity, k₃ = a coefficient: used in machines substituting for water-sport dryland
    26	training). Exercises are also classified by whether they have **mechanical feedback**: in a
    27	paddling stroke, higher muscular force raises oar velocity, which raises water resistance,
    28	which in turn demands more muscular force (a closed loop, figure 2.4): most sport
    29	movements have this feedback loop. Mechanical feedback is **absent** specifically in
    30	isometric exercise and with isokinetic devices (constant velocity regardless of force
    31	applied), which is why those modes do not reproduce most sport movements' natural
    32	force-resistance interaction. (PDF pp. 30-31, 33-34, 51-52, printed pp. 21, 23-24, 42-43.)
    33	
    34	**Why it works** — Inference: because different sport movements are naturally resisted by
    35	different physical laws (hydrodynamic for swimming, elastic/momentum-based for most
    36	throwing and jumping, weight-and-inertia for lifting), an exercise's resistance type
    37	determines how closely its force-velocity demand matches the target movement, independent
    38	of which muscles it uses: this is the authors' own point in the dryland-training case
    39	study below, though the general "resistance type governs specificity" framing is this
    40	entry's synthesis of the mechanics they lay out.
    41	
    42	**When to apply** — As a checklist when selecting resistance training equipment intended to
    43	transfer to a specific sport skill: match the resistance-type profile (elastic, inertial,
    44	weight-based, hydrodynamic, viscosity-based) to the target movement's actual resistance
    45	type, not just its visible muscle groups or general movement pattern. This complements
    46	[[framework--transfer-of-training-coefficient-calculation]] (Chapter 1) by giving a
    47	mechanical reason, in addition to a measured-transfer-coefficient reason, that an exercise
    48	might fail to transfer.
    49	
    50	**When NOT to apply** — Inference: this is a mechanics-based classification, not itself a
    51	ranked hierarchy of "better" and "worse" resistance types; each type is appropriate for
    52	different training goals (elastic resistance for end-range loading, isokinetic/isometric
    53	for eliminating the confound of a changing external load during rehab-style force testing,
    54	despite lacking mechanical feedback). Do not treat "lacks mechanical feedback" as
    55	disqualifying isometric or isokinetic training outright; the authors present it as a
    56	descriptive difference from most sport movements, not a stated flaw.
    57	
    58	**Example** — The authors' own case study, "Selection of Dryland Exercises for Swimmers"
    59	(printed p. 25): a swim coach trialed extensible rubber bands (elastic resistance, pulling
    60	force increased through the pull, unlike the customary stroke), a weightlifting pulley
    61	machine (near-constant resistance across the pull, but the athletes could not relax at the
    62	end of motion, unlike a real stroke), and friction/hydrodynamic training devices (either
    63	constant resistance or resistance proportional to pull velocity, both meant to mimic water
    64	resistance): none matched the true stroke's resistance profile (proportional to the
    65	squared hand velocity relative to water) exactly, illustrating why matching a device's
    66	underlying resistance law to the sport movement's own is harder than it looks, even when
    67	targeting the same muscle groups and general movement pattern.

    14	# Near-Full Limb Extension Minimizes Moment Arm and Maximizes End-Point Force
    15	
    16	**What it is** — In a single-joint task, strength (end-point force) equals joint moment
    17	divided by the moment arm of the external force: the smaller that moment arm, the larger
    18	the external force the same joint moment can generate. In leg or arm extension, the highest
    19	end-point forces occur when the extremity is nearly fully extended, because the line of
    20	force action then passes close to the joint center, shrinking the moment arm toward zero
    21	(figure 2.25): at full extension with the force line passing through the joint center,
    22	the joint moment required to bear a given load approaches zero, which is why "people can
    23	sustain extremely large forces" in that position. A seated leg-press experiment (Hugh-Jones,
    24	1947; n=6, isometric pushes against a pedal at five thigh angles and varying knee angles)
    25	found up to a **ninefold** difference in maximal push force purely from limb-position
    26	changes, with the largest forces recorded near the limiting (near-straight) knee angle
    27	(figure 2.26). A separate example: forces in baseball pitching (Fleisig et al., 1995) show
    28	the highest shoulder torques and forces occurring near maximal external rotation
    29	(approaching 180°), where passive elastic tissue deformation is largest (figure 2.23: 64
    30	N·m and 67 N·m torques, 310 N force at that position). (PDF pp. 55-57, printed pp. 44-45.)
    31	
    32	**Why it works** — The mechanism is stated directly: joint moment = muscle tension ×
    33	moment arm, so for a fixed joint moment capacity, minimizing the moment arm of the
    34	*external* resistance (by extending the limb, bringing the line of force action close to
    35	the joint) maximizes the external force that same joint moment can produce or resist. This
    36	is standard leverage mechanics, applied here specifically to the near-full-extension case
    37	in leg and arm extension tasks.
    38	
    39	**When to apply** — As the mechanical explanation for why the heaviest loads in exercises
    40	like squats and leg presses are borne in near-extended positions, and why athletes and
    41	lifters intuitively position limbs near full extension when maximal force output (not range
    42	of motion) is the goal. **Eric's baseline:** relevant context for interpreting force-plate
    43	and strength-testing data collected at different joint angles or limb positions: a test at
    44	one limb position is not directly comparable to a test at another without accounting for
    45	this moment-arm effect, consistent with
    46	[[framework--joint-strength-curves-sticking-points-and-moment-arm]].
    47	
    48	**When NOT to apply** — Inference: this is a single-joint, isometric-testing-based finding
    49	(Hugh-Jones 1947, seated pedal-push, n=6); the ninefold figure is specific to that
    50	apparatus and population, and multi-joint dynamic movements (e.g. a full squat, a pitching
    51	delivery) involve additional factors (muscle-length-tension effects, momentum, multi-joint
    52	coordination) this simple moment-arm account does not fully capture on its own: see
    53	[[framework--joint-strength-curves-sticking-points-and-moment-arm]] for the muscle-tension
    54	side of the same joint-angle-strength relationship. Do not treat "near full extension
    55	maximizes force" as a universal safety or technique recommendation; near-lockout positions
    56	carry their own joint-loading and injury considerations not addressed in this passage.
    57	
    58	**Example** — The pitching-shoulder example (figure 2.23, the authors' own): during arm
    59	cocking, external rotation approaches 180°, and the passive elastic tissues of the
    60	shoulder, resisting this extreme range, contribute to the high torques (64-67 N·m) and
    61	force (310 N) measured at that position: illustrating that near-end-range positions
    62	generate high forces both through moment-arm effects and through passive tissue tension,
    63	not moment-arm effects alone.
    64	
    65	**Related** — [[framework--joint-strength-curves-sticking-points-and-moment-arm]],
    66	[[supertraining--verkhoshansky/claim--joint-angle-and-limb-position-changes-force-output]].

    14	# Athletes Tend to Self-Organize Technique to Minimize Secondary (Non-Primary) Joint Moments
    15	
    16	**What it is** — Muscles produce moments of force not only in the intended (primary)
    17	direction but also in other directions (secondary moments); the body counterbalances
    18	unwanted secondary moments by activating additional muscles, which increases the number of
    19	active muscles while sometimes decreasing net strength in the primary direction (worked
    20	example: a forceful arm supination with the elbow flexed at a right angle, as in driving a
    21	screw: the triceps, though not a supinator, becomes active to counterbalance the flexion
    22	moment the supinating biceps also produces as a secondary effect). The authors' second
    23	example: gymnasts performing pull-ups on rings consistently supinate the arms while flexing
    24	the elbows; "nobody teaches them to do so; the movement pattern is simply more convenient
    25	for the performers" (printed p. 44). The authors present this as their stated general
    26	observation, "athletes tend to perform forceful movements in a way that minimizes
    27	secondary moments," using the pull-up example as its sole illustration. (PDF p. 55,
    28	printed p. 44.)
    29	
    30	**Why it works** — Inference: the authors do not name a specific motor-control mechanism
    31	(e.g. an optimization process in motor learning, energy-cost minimization, or proprioceptive
    32	feedback) for why athletes converge on secondary-moment-minimizing technique without
    33	coaching; they state the observation and the biomechanical reason it would be advantageous
    34	(fewer competing/counterbalancing muscles active, potentially preserving more net force for
    35	the primary moment), but not a mechanism for how an untaught athlete arrives at that
    36	pattern.
    37	
    38	**When to apply** — Inference: as a caution against over-coaching a self-selected technique
    39	detail that may already reflect an athlete's own biomechanically favorable adaptation (e.g.
    40	a specific grip rotation or limb orientation an athlete adopts without being taught it)
    41	before assuming it needs correction; worth checking whether an unprompted technique quirk
    42	reduces secondary-moment demand rather than treating it as a fault by default.
    43	
    44	**When NOT to apply** — This is a single stated observation illustrated with one example
    45	(pull-up arm supination); the authors do not provide data (EMG, force measurement across
    46	technique variants, or a controlled comparison) showing that self-selected technique
    47	actually reduces secondary moments more than an alternative, taught technique would.
    48	Confidence is `untested`: treat this as a plausible coaching heuristic worth watching for,
    49	not an established finding. Do not use it to dismiss legitimate technique coaching broadly;
    50	the claim is narrow (secondary-moment minimization specifically), not a general argument
    51	against coaching intervention.
    52	
    53	**Example** — Inference: a lineman who naturally rotates his hand/wrist into a particular
    54	position during a punch or block, without being cued to, may be self-organizing to reduce a
    55	secondary moment at the wrist or elbow; before correcting it, a coach could check whether
    56	the self-selected position is biomechanically explicable (as the ring-gymnast supination
    57	example is) rather than assuming it is simply incorrect technique. This application to a
    58	football skill is this entry's own extension, not stated in the source.
